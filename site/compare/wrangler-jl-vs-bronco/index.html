Jeep Wrangler JL vs Ford Bronco

Jeep Wrangler JLFord Bronco (sixth generation)

For most buyers, the Bronco: its independent front suspension makes it the better truck to drive every day, and its Sasquatch package put 35s on from the factory three years before the two-door Wrangler got them. The Wrangler Rubicon still has what rock crawlers want, a solid front axle and a 4:1 transfer case, and used two-doors start cheaper.

The short answer

Buy the Jeep Wrangler JL if

you crawl rocks, want a solid front axle and a 4:1 low range to build on, or want the cheapest way into a capable doors-off 4x4

Buy the Ford Bronco (sixth generation) if

you'll spend more time on pavement and fast dirt than in boulders, want factory 35s or a manual with a crawler gear, or want doors you can carry along

Off-road

The big split is the front axle. The JL keeps a solid front axle on coils, which articulates well and takes lifts and bigger tires without much fuss. The Bronco uses independent twin A-arms up front and a solid Dana axle at the back. AutoTrader.ca put a Bronco Badlands against a Wrangler Rubicon 4xe and concluded the Ford 'gives up nothing in off-road capability', and the geometry backs that up: a two-door Bronco on 35s has 43.2° approach, 37.2° departure and 29.0° breakover, against 44°, 37° and 27.8° for a 2018 JL Rubicon.

Both offer front and rear electric lockers and a front sway-bar disconnect on their off-road trims. The Rubicon's 4:1 Rock-Trac gives up to 84.2:1 crawl; a manual Bronco with the Advanced transfer case reaches 95:1 by Ford's figure, thanks to its crawler gear. The difference shows after you modify. A solid axle shrugs off big lifts and 37s, while bigger tires load a Bronco's tie rods; Bronco Nation says the tie-rod failures owners report usually involve lifts, big tires or hard driving.

On-road and daily use

Not close. Car and Driver's 2021 comparison of a Bronco Outer Banks and a Wrangler Unlimited, as reported by Ford Authority, called the Ford 'far easier to live with' and the Jeep 'woefully uncouth on the road'. AutoTrader.ca found the same: the Bronco tracks straight and true, while the Wrangler's solid axle needs constant corrections on the highway. The Jeep was the quieter of the two in that test, and its interior scored higher.

Power is roughly even. The JL's 3.6 makes 285 hp and its 2.0 turbo 270 hp with 295 lb-ft; the Bronco's 2.3 makes 300 hp and 325 lb-ft and its 2.7 V6 330 hp and 415 lb-ft, both on premium (Ford quotes 275 and 315 hp on regular). EPA ratings are close too: 18/23 mpg for a 3.6 automatic JL, 20/22 for a 2.3 Bronco, and 17–18 combined once a Bronco has the Sasquatch package.

Reliability and what breaks

Both had a rough start. The JL's trouble spots are steering (a damper campaign, the track-bar bracket weld recall and early aluminum steering boxes), three clutch recalls on manual trucks, the Pentastar's leaking oil cooler housing and, on 2021–25 trucks, a power-steering pump wiring recall. SlashGear counted more than 1,000 complaints for the 2018 model alone.

The Bronco's are roofs and recalls. Molded-in-color hardtops on 2021–23 trucks craze and delaminate, 2021–22 2.7 V6s are in an intake-valve recall that can end in a new engine, Bilstein rear shock reservoirs corrode off, and 2024–25 trucks have front control-arm fastener recalls. Jalopnik counted about 20 recalls by September 2026. CarGurus says to avoid 2021–22 and calls 2023 the best year.

Running costs, parts and modifying

The Wrangler's aftermarket has a three-year head start, with lifts, steering, lockers and armor for the JL everywhere. The Bronco's covers the essentials: 1.25–2 in levels for 35s, heavy-duty tie rods, Ford's own HOSS 3.0 parts and 4.46 or 4.7 gears. Oil changes are cheap on both: 5 qt of 0W-20 for the JL Pentastar, 6.2 qt of 5W-30 for the Bronco 2.3.

On tires, a stock JL Sport or Sahara takes a 33 and a Rubicon runs 35s on bump-stop spacers. A stock Bronco takes a 285/75R17 (about 33.8 in), and narrow 35s once the crash bars come off and it has a small level. If you want 35s from the factory, the Sasquatch package has offered them since 2021 on 4.7 gears; the two-door Wrangler's Xtreme 35 package arrived for 2024, with 4.56 gears and the 2.0 turbo only.

Buying one now

Two-door JLs run from about $17k for an early Sport to $50k for a late Rubicon X. Stock or lightly modified 2021–23 Outer Banks, Wildtrak and First Edition Broncos sold for $38k–56k on Bring a Trailer, Raptors for $60k–105k, and several two-door seven-speed trucks were bid to only $23,000–29,500 without selling. Run the VIN on either before you look further.

For the Jeep, buy 2019-on or a 2018 with every recall and steering fix documented. For the Bronco, buy a 2023 or later, or a 2021–22 with proof of the 2.7 valve test, the roof program and the shock recall. Four-door buyers face the same choice: the JL Unlimited adds diesel, 4xe and 392 options, while the four-door Bronco is the only body offered as a Raptor.

Questions, answered

Is the Bronco better off-road than the Wrangler?

Out of the box, about equal. AutoTrader.ca found the Bronco 'gives up nothing in off-road capability' against a Wrangler Rubicon, and a Sasquatch Bronco's angles are within a degree or two of a JL Rubicon's. The Wrangler's solid front axle articulates more and is easier to build for serious rock crawling.

Which is better on the highway?

The Bronco. Its independent front suspension tracks straighter and steers more precisely; Car and Driver called the Wrangler 'woefully uncouth on the road' in its 2021 comparison. The Wrangler was quieter in AutoTrader.ca's test.

Which tows more?

The Bronco. The standard Bronco is rated at 3,500 lb and the Raptor at 4,500 lb. A two-door JL is rated at only 2,000 lb, and the four-door JL Unlimited at 3,500 lb, or 5,000 lb with the 2024-on Dana 44 HD rear axle and automatic on non-4xe trucks.

Wrangler Rubicon or Bronco Sasquatch?

The Rubicon has a solid front axle, front and rear lockers and a 4:1 transfer case, but 33-in tires unless you add spacers or buy a 2024-on Xtreme 35. The Sasquatch package has 35s, 4.7 gears, position-sensitive Bilstein dampers, the sway-bar disconnect and up to 11.6 in of clearance, at an EPA cost of about 3 mpg.

Do the doors and roof come off both?

Yes. Both hardtops lift off in sections (three on the Wrangler, four on the Bronco in AutoTrader.ca's test), and both sets of doors need tools. The Bronco's frameless doors stow in bags in the truck; the Wrangler's stay in the garage.

Which is more reliable?

Neither has a clean record. Early JLs had steering, track-bar weld and clutch recalls; early Broncos had hardtop, 2.7 valve and rear shock problems, and about 20 recalls in total by September 2026. Avoid the first model years of each (2018 JL, 2021–22 Bronco) unless the fixes are documented.

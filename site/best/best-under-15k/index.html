The best boxy 4x4s under $15,000

Fifteen grand doesn't buy a good Land Cruiser any more. Our October 2026 value checks put a driver-quality 80 Series at $18k–34k and a 100 Series at $15k–36k. What it does still buy is a surprising amount of truck: V8 Toyotas, a Lexus with Prado bones, straight-six Jeeps, a nine-seat Suburban and, if you're the brave sort, a Range Rover.

Every truck here has a driver-quality range that starts under $15,000. Several stretch well past it, and the best years tend to sit at the top of the range, so read each price line as where decent ones start, not what the best one costs. At this money you're buying a truck's history as much as the truck itself, so ask for paperwork before you ask about the lift.

How we picked We started from the driver-quality price range in each model guide's October 2026 value check, which is built mostly from recent Bring a Trailer sales, and kept only trucks whose driver range starts under $15,000. Best years come from the buy and avoid flags on each guide's timeline, which follow owner-forum consensus and published buyer's guides. The watch line is the failure that most often turns a cheap truck into an expensive one. Every pick was sold new in the US.

  1. No. 1 · Best all-rounder

    Toyota 4Runner 4th Gen

    YEARS 2006–09, ideally a V8PRICE $8k–23k for a driver

    The fourth-gen 4Runner sits on Prado 120 bones and is the only 4Runner ever sold with a V8, the 4.7 2UZ-FE, with a lockable Torsen centre diff behind it. Start at 2006: that's the refresh year, HotCars picks the 2006-on V8 trucks as the better buys, and late-2006 V6s got the revised head gasket. The 2009 Trail Edition adds a factory rear e-locker.

    It's the safest bet on this list for the money. Our value check, from 23 Bring a Trailer sales, puts the median at about $13,750. The ceiling is rising, though: the top two sales were low-mile 2006–07 V8 4WD trucks at $35,000–39,000.

    Check first: On a V8, get proof of the timing belt (Toyota lists 90,000 miles or 108 months) and check for a failed secondary air-injection system, which CarComplaints owners put at $2,000–4,000; on a 2003–05 V6, look for coolant loss and a cylinder 6 misfire.

  2. No. 2 · Best budget overlander

    Lexus GX 470

    YEARS 2003–09 (no year flagged to avoid)PRICE $12k–25k for a driver

    A Prado in a dinner jacket: the same J120 family as the 4th-gen 4Runner, but with the 4.7 V8 as standard, a five-speed automatic and rear air suspension. Our guide flags no bad year. 2005 brought VVT-i and 270 hp, and the new Sport model that year had KDSS as standard.

    It has become the default budget overlander, and the market has noticed: 115 Bring a Trailer sales from January 2025 to October 2026 had a median of $20,000, and the top sales were trucks with under 40,000 miles. Under $15k you're buying miles, so buy records with them.

    Check first: Look for a rear end that sags overnight and for rust on the frame and rear air-spring perches, and budget roughly $1K at a shop if there's no timing-belt receipt.

  3. No. 3 · Best old-school Toyota

    Toyota 4Runner 3rd Gen

    YEARS 1999–2000, ideally with the rear e-lockerPRICE $13k–22k for a driver

    Small, simple, coil-sprung and stupidly durable, the third gen is the 4Runner that became a proper SUV without going soft. IH8MUD regulars rate 1999–2000 the best years: the facelift and new dash arrived, Multi-Mode 4WD became an option, and the rear e-locker was still on the list (2001–02 trucks swapped it for traction control).

    It only just squeezes in. The driver range starts at $13k, the median of 24 recent Bring a Trailer sales was about $18,750, and lockered 1996–2000 trucks sit at the top of a market our guide calls strong and rising. Under $15k buys a driver with a few stories.

    Check first: Ask what lower ball joints are fitted (2001–02 trucks were covered by recall 50J, and AdventureTaco's advice is OEM Toyota only), then crawl underneath and probe the frame welds and rear control-arm mounts with a screwdriver.

  4. No. 4 · Best cheap wheeler

    Jeep Cherokee XJ

    YEARS 1991–99 (4.0 High Output)PRICE $8k–16k for a driver

    A small unibody box with two solid axles and one of the best straight-sixes ever fitted to a 4x4, sold to suburbia for 18 years and now the budget wheeler everyone recommends. Buy a 1991-on 'High Output' 4.0: 190 hp, and Chrysler multi-point injection that Off Road Xtreme says is easier to diagnose than the earlier Renix. 2000–01 trucks got the 0331 head, which can crack, so our guide stops the best years at 1999.

    October 2026 Bring a Trailer results (about 105 sales) had a median around $11,500, with the middle half roughly $8,250–15,250. Clean, low-mile 1997–2001 Sports and Classics now bring real money, but most XJs still don't.

    Check first: Rust on the unibody frame rails near the suspension mounts is the deal-breaker; after that, idle it until the electric fan comes on, because XJs are known for running hot.

  5. No. 5 · Best Wrangler

    Jeep Wrangler TJ

    YEARS 2000–06PRICE $10k–20k for a driver

    Round headlights, coil springs at all four corners, the 4.0 six carried over and, from 2003, a Rubicon with factory lockers. For many people the TJ is the sweet spot of the whole Wrangler line. Our guide flags 1997–99 for early recalls, engine-computer and fuel-system bugs and a cowl vent that leaks onto the floors, so start at 2000, when the NV3550 five-speed replaced the AX-15.

    256 short-wheelbase TJ sales on Bring a Trailer gave a median of about $15,500, with the middle half about $11,000–20,500. Rubicons had a median of about $20,000, so under $15k generally means a non-Rubicon.

    Check first: Check the frame at the rear lower control-arm brackets, where Quadratec warns rust can become structural, and on a 2005–06 ask whether the oil pump drive assembly has been replaced.

  6. No. 6 · Best for a big family

    Chevrolet/GMC Suburban (1973–1991 square body)

    YEARS 1987–91 (throttle-body injection)PRICE $8k–16k for a driver

    A three-quarter-ton pickup wearing a station-wagon body, with seats for nine and room for a camp kitchen behind them. From 1987 every gas engine got throttle-body injection, and owners on GMSquareBody and CK5 point to the TBI trucks for better mileage, more power and better reliability than the carbureted ones.

    Prices are steady rather than rising: 17 Bring a Trailer sales of mostly original 4x4 Suburbans had a median of about $15,750, and Hagerty calls one usable under $10,000. It's thirsty and it rusts like any 40-year-old GM truck, but Mill Supply and Auto Metal Direct sell the panels.

    Check first: Check the rockers, floors and wheel wells, Hagerty's main problem areas, and on any lifted truck look for cracks or a brace at the steering box.

  7. No. 7 · Most truck for the money

    Land Rover LR3 / Discovery 3

    YEARS 2008–09PRICE $7k–13k for a driver

    The Discovery that swapped beam axles for air springs and software, and somehow got better off-road for it. US trucks from 2008 are V8-only seven-seaters, and ReRev rates 2008–09 the most reliable LR3s; they also log the fewest NHTSA complaints. 2005, the launch year, is the one our guide says to avoid.

    Most LR3s sell for under $14,000: 24 Bring a Trailer sales from September 2025 to October 2026 had a median of about $9,100. It's heavy, complicated and cheap for a reason, so this is the pick for someone who owns a diagnostic tool and doesn't mind using it.

    Check first: Run the air suspension through every height and leave it overnight to see whether a corner sags, then check that the electronic parking brake applies and releases cleanly more than once.

  8. No. 8 · Best for the brave

    Land Rover Range Rover L322

    YEARS 2007–09PRICE $13k–20k for a driver

    A Munich-engineered, Solihull-built limousine that still walks up a rock garden on its tiptoes. 2007 added Terrain Response, a locking centre diff and an electronic rear diff, and guides and owners rate the 2007–09 Jaguar-engined trucks the most dependable L322s. Skip 2003–05 BMW-engined trucks without timing-chain receipts and 2010–12 5.0s without proof of updated tensioners.

    22 Bring a Trailer sales from late August to October 2026 had a median of about $14,600. As our guide puts it, most sell for less than a set of air struts and a timing job together, which tells you where the rest of your budget is going.

    Check first: Cycle the air suspension through every height, and read the codes before and after a long test drive, because sellers can clear faults just before you arrive.

Questions

Can you still buy a Land Cruiser for under $15,000?

A running one, yes. A good one, mostly no. Our October 2026 checks put project 80 Series trucks at $5k–13k and drivers at $18k–34k, and 1991–92 FJ80s sold for a median of about $15,750. Project 100 Series trucks run $6k–13k. For Toyota dependability with less risk at this price, the 4th-gen 4Runner and GX 470 are the safer bets.

High miles with records, or low miles without?

Records. Our Toyota and Lexus guides keep landing on the same owner advice: a high-mileage truck with full records beats a low-mileage one with none. On the V8 Toyotas the record that matters most is the timing belt; on the Land Rovers it's the air suspension and, on the L322, the timing chains.

Is a Land Rover under $15,000 a mistake?

Not automatically, but go in with your eyes open. The LR3 and L322 are cheap because buyers price in air suspension, electronics and, on the L322, timing-chain work. Buy the best-documented truck you can find, avoid the years our guides flag (2005 for the LR3; 2003–05 and 2010 for the L322) and get a diagnostic scan first. The Discovery II ($8k–15k for a driver) keeps a ladder frame and live axles, but it has its own rust and head-gasket list.

Are these prices going up?

We can tell you what they've done, not what they'll do. Our guides describe the 3rd-gen 4Runner market as strong and rising and say tidy TJs are now firmly collectible, while the square-body Suburban is steady and the LR3 is cheap and flat. Check recent sales before you buy.

Buyer’s guide · prices checked October 2026

The best boxy 4x4s for rock crawling

Rock crawling is the one kind of off-roading where an independent front end, a heated steering wheel and a big touchscreen don't help much. What helps is a solid axle at each end, a locker or two, a low-range ratio deep enough to idle up a ledge, a short wheelbase and an aftermarket that sells the parts you're about to break.

That's why four of the eight picks are Jeeps. The Wrangler has had factory lockers and 4:1 low range since 2003, and nothing else on our site comes close on aftermarket support. The Toyotas and the little Suzuki earn their places by being tough, simple or, in the Samurai's case, light enough to drive over things rather than through them.

How we picked We started from the driver-grade prices in our October 2026 value checks (mostly recent Bring a Trailer results), took the best years and the years to skip from each truck's guide, and favored solid axles, factory lockers, deep low range and aftermarket support. Tire sizes come from the fitment notes in each guide.

  1. No. 1 · Best all-round crawler

    Jeep Wrangler TJ

    YEARS 2003–06 Rubicon (skip 1997–99)PRICE $10k–20k for a driver; $20k–39k for a Rubicon or low-mile truck

    The TJ put coil springs at all four corners on a 93.4 in wheelbase, kept the 4.0 straight-six, and from 2003 offered the Rubicon: Dana 44s front and rear, air lockers and the 4:1 NV241OR transfer case straight from the factory. A 2005–06 truck has a 42.2° approach angle and a 36.7 ft turning circle, which is the kind of number you notice halfway up a ledge.

    Our guide flags 1997–99 for early teething trouble (recalls, cracked exhaust manifolds and a cowl vent that leaks onto the floors). Prices have firmed up: across 256 Bring a Trailer sales, Rubicons had a median of about $20,000. The aftermarket is as deep as it gets, and the long-wheelbase Unlimited (LJ) is the same recipe with more room, for $16k–27k as a driver.

    Check first: Check the frame, especially the rear lower control-arm brackets, where Quadratec warns rust can become severe enough to cause structural failure.

  2. No. 2 · Best factory-locked two-door for the money

    Jeep Wrangler JK (2-door)

    YEARS 2012–18 RubiconPRICE $12k–20k for a driver; $20k–33k for a low-mile Rubicon

    Every JK Rubicon has Dana 44s with lockers, the 4:1 Rock-Trac transfer case and an electronic sway-bar disconnect. The two-door keeps a short 95.4 in wheelbase, and a Rubicon on 32 in tires has a 44.3° approach, 40.4° departure and 25.4° breakover. JEGS puts the stock-height tire limit at about 33 in, and 37s need about 3.5 in of lift plus axle, driveshaft and gearing upgrades.

    Buy a 2012 or later for the 3.6 Pentastar and the five-speed automatic, which Quadratec and Driving Line both call the engine to have; first-year 2007s drew several recalls and the most complaints. On a 2012–13 truck, ask whether the left cylinder head was replaced under Chrysler's extended coverage.

    Check first: Engage both lockers and the disconnect and confirm 4-Low is the 4:1 Rock-Trac: a Moab or Willys Wheeler looks the part but has the 2.72:1 case.

  3. No. 3 · Best crawler straight from the factory

    Jeep Wrangler JL

    YEARS 2019–on Rubicon (skip 2018)PRICE $27k–36k for a driver Rubicon

    The JL Rubicon is the most capable short Wrangler Jeep has built. A 2018 Rubicon has a crawl ratio of up to 84.2:1, a 44° approach angle and 30 in of wading depth, and it clears a 35 in tire at ride height with bump-stop spacers. From 2024 the two-door could get Jeep's Xtreme 35 package (Rubicon and Willys with the 2.0 turbo only): a 1.5 in lift, 4.56 gears and beadlock-capable wheels.

    Our guide calls 2018 the year to be wary of: track-bar bracket welds, the intermediate steering shaft and rear frame-rail corrosion are all under recall, and owners complained about highway wander. A later truck with every fix documented is the buy, and two-door JLs hold their value well.

    Check first: Run the VIN for the track-bar, clutch and steering recalls, then feel for vague or wandering steering at highway speed on 2018–2020 trucks.

  4. No. 4 · Best big crawler

    Toyota Land Cruiser 80 Series

    YEARS 1993–97 FZJ80PRICE $18k–34k for a driver

    Solid axles at both ends on coil springs, full-time 4WD with a lockable center diff and, on 1993–97 FZJ80s with option K294, factory front and rear lockers. That makes the 80 one of the few wagons you can buy with three lockers from the factory. Christo Slee says 35s work on a 2 in Old Man Emu lift if you lower the bump stops, and advises re-gearing from the stock 4.11s.

    It's big and heavy for tight trails, at 4,751 lb and with a 40.4 ft turning circle on a 1997, so it suits the crawler who also wants to sleep in the back. Clean, locked trucks have become collector items; tired drivers are still cheap enough to use properly.

    Check first: Engage the lockers in low range on a loose surface and make sure they actually lock; actuators that won't engage are a common fault.

  5. No. 5 · Best budget crawler

    Jeep Cherokee XJ

    YEARS 1991–99 (4.0 High Output)PRICE $8k–16k for a driver

    The XJ has live axles front and rear (Quadra-Link coils in front, leaves behind), the 4.0 straight-six and a 101.4 in wheelbase, and a 2001 Sport has 10.6 in of ground clearance. It's still cheap by classic-4x4 standards, and it's the truck most people recommend when you want to learn to wheel without spending Wrangler money.

    Buy a 1991-on truck for the 190 hp 'High Output' 4.0, which Off Road Xtreme recommends because it's easier to diagnose than the Renix engine. Skip 1984–86 unless you plan an engine swap, and check the head on any 2000–01, whose '0331' casting can crack between cylinders 3 and 4. A tow-package Dana 44 or a Chrysler 8.25 rear is worth more than the Dana 35 on ABS trucks if you plan bigger tires.

    Check first: It's a unibody, so check the frame rails near the suspension mounts first; rust there can mean cutting and repairing the rails before a lift.

  6. No. 6 · Best small crawler

    Suzuki SJ / Samurai

    YEARS 1986–95 (US)PRICE $8k–17k for a good driver or modified truck

    At about 2,094 lb for a convertible, the Samurai weighs less than half of most trucks here and fits where they don't. Suzuki's 1986 brochure lists a 43° approach and 38° departure. With a 1.5–2 in lift or some trimming it takes 235/75R15s (about 29 in), and Trail Tough's deluxe spring-over kit is rated for up to 31s with knuckle-over steering.

    Prices have climbed from throwaway to collectible, with a median of about $9,400 across 22 recent Bring a Trailer sales. Most have been modified. A well-built spring-over or engine swap saves you money; a bad one costs you, and Trail Tough notes that 1996-on 1.6 swap donors are OBD-II and won't be smog-legal in California.

    Check first: Look at the transfer-case mounts and the rear frame hanger, which Trail Tough calls common stress failures, especially on re-geared trucks.

  7. No. 7 · Best vintage crawler

    Toyota Land Cruiser FJ40

    YEARS 1976–84 (1978 the coveted year)PRICE $22k–42k for a driver

    Leaf springs, solid axles, a 90 in wheelbase and a torquey straight-six: the FJ40 is the truck that made Land Cruisers famous off-road. IH8MUD's tried-and-true tire answer is 31x10.50R15 on a stock truck, and 33x9.50s fit with bump-stop spacers and the steering stops adjusted.

    Most buyers prefer 1976-on trucks for the front disc brakes, the 2F and the four-speed, and IH8MUD members call 1978 the coveted year. From 1979 power steering became optional, but the US axle ratio went from 4.11 to 3.70 for highway cruising, which a crawler will want to undo. Prices are well off their 2016 peak but strong for rust-free trucks.

    Check first: Rust beats everything: check door bottoms, the rear sill, the frame horns and the body mounts before you look at anything mechanical.

  8. No. 8 · Best solid-axle Toyota sleeper

    Toyota 4Runner 1st Gen

    YEARS 1985 (1984–85 for the solid axle)PRICE $13k–22k for a driver

    Only the 1984–85 first-gen 4Runners have the solid front axle on leaf springs, and 1985 adds the fuel-injected 22R-E and a rear seat on every trim. It's a Hilux pickup underneath, which means the huge mini-truck aftermarket applies. Owners run 31s without a lift or a regear; 33s need about 1.5 in of lift, some hammering and lower gears.

    Purists still seek them out, so they cost more than the 1986–89 IFS trucks: the top three of 20 recent Bring a Trailer sales were all 1984–85 solid-axle five-speeds, including a 42k-mile 1985 SR5 at $60,500. Avoid the 1988–89 V6 trucks, which most enthusiasts skip in favor of the four.

    Check first: Look underneath before you pay the premium: leaf springs and manual hubs up front mean solid axle; torsion bars and CV axles mean IFS.

Questions

Do I need factory lockers?

No, but they save money. A TJ, JK or JL Rubicon, or an 80 Series with option K294, comes with front and rear lockers already fitted. Everything else needs aftermarket lockers; Trail Tough, for example, sells a Spartan automatic locker for the Samurai. Price that in when you compare a cheap non-Rubicon with a Rubicon.

Two-door or four-door?

For tight rock trails, the short wheelbase wins on breakover and turning. A JK Rubicon two-door has a 95.4 in wheelbase and a 25.4° breakover on 32s. Four-doors are more stable on steep climbs and carry more, and the long-wheelbase TJ Unlimited (LJ) sells for more than a comparable TJ ($16k–27k as a driver).

What's the biggest tire I can run without a lift?

Roughly: 31 in on a TJ, about 33 in on a JK, 33 in on a JL Sport or Sahara and 35 in on a JL Rubicon with bump-stop spacers, about 33 in (285/75R16) on an 80 Series, 235/75R15 on an XJ, 215/75R15 on a Samurai and 31x10.50R15 on an FJ40 or a first-gen 4Runner. Each truck's guide lists the lifted sizes and what rubs.

Why aren't the IFS trucks here?

Trucks with independent front suspension, such as the fifth-gen 4Runner or the GX 470, are excellent on trails, and several of them lead our overlanding guide. On the rocks, though, a solid front axle keeps its ground clearance at full articulation and takes bigger tires and lockers more easily, which is why serious crawler builds start with one.

What is death wobble, and should it put me off?

It's a violent shake of the front end after a bump at speed, and it's a solid-axle problem. Our guides trace it most often to a worn or loose track bar, plus ball joints, tie-rod ends and bushings, and it's common on cheaply lifted trucks. Test-drive at 30–65 mph over bumps; a truck that wobbles usually needs front-end parts.

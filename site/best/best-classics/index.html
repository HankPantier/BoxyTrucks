The best classic boxy 4x4s to buy and keep

A classic 4x4 is the rare old vehicle you're allowed to use the way it was meant to be used. Every truck here can still go down a trail on the weekend, and every one has a parts supply, a club scene and a forum full of people who have already fixed whatever is about to go wrong with yours.

What they all share is rust. On a 40- or 60-year-old truck, the body and frame decide the price far more than the engine, the trim or the year, and a cheap rusty one is usually the most expensive way in. We talk about what values have done because buyers ask, but this is a list of trucks to enjoy, not a list of investments.

How we picked We started from the driver-grade prices in our October 2026 value checks (mostly recent Bring a Trailer results, plus Hagerty where our guides cite it), took the best years from each truck's guide where owners agree on them, and favored trucks with good parts supply, active clubs and a body you can actually repair. Values describe past sales only.

  1. No. 1 · Best all-round classic

    Toyota Land Cruiser FJ40

    YEARS 1976–84 (1978 the coveted year)PRICE $22k–42k for a driver

    The FJ40 is the reason anyone outside Japan cares what a Land Cruiser is, and it's still the easiest classic 4x4 to own: a straight-six, leaf springs, a huge parts and club scene, and simple enough to fix with basic tools. Most buyers prefer 1976-on trucks for the front disc brakes, the 2F and the four-speed, and IH8MUD members call 1978 the coveted year.

    On values, FJ40 prices have come off their 2016 peak but stay strong for original, rust-free trucks. Across 244 Bring a Trailer sales from January 2025 to October 2026 the median was about $29,400, and V8-swapped trucks sold for a median of about $25,300, below stock ones. ICON-style restomods, at $98,500–140,000, are a separate market.

    Check first: Rust beats everything: IH8MUD veterans rank it above every mechanical issue combined, so check door bottoms, the rear sill, the frame horns and the body mounts first.

  2. No. 2 · Best V8 classic, if you can pay for it

    Ford Bronco (1966–1977)

    YEARS 1966–77 (buy the truck and the builder, not the year)PRICE $41.5k–70k for a stock or mildly modified driver

    The early Bronco has what the FJ40 didn't: a coil-sprung front axle, a V8 and, from 1973, an optional automatic and power steering. Mechanical parts are easy because it uses common Ford engines and Dana axles. Only 650 Baja Broncos were sold, so clones exist.

    Values roughly doubled between 2016 and 2019 and kept climbing, but Hagerty recorded a 12 percent drop in the second quarter of 2026. Across 306 Bring a Trailer sales the median stock or mildly modified truck sold for about $55,500, and Hagerty says a driver-grade truck still costs $50,000 or more. Who built the truck now matters more than the year.

    Check first: Check the rockers, floors, door posts and frame rails for rot even on dry-state trucks, and whether the rear fenders are cut: uncut trucks are rarer and worth more.

  3. No. 3 · Best for the purist

    Land Rover Series I–III

    YEARS 1961–71 Series IIA or 1971–85 Series IIIPRICE $19k–34k for a driver

    The Series is the vehicle that taught most of the world what a 4x4 is: aluminium body, leaf springs, a 2.25 four and a cruising speed of 55–60 mph. Classic Car Hub calls an 88 or 109 Series IIA the sweet spot of the range, and Octane calls the Series III, with its all-synchro gearbox, by far the easiest to live with.

    Hagerty says Series values have kept climbing, and in its 2019 analysis petrol trucks were worth about 6% more than diesels and soft-tops about 2% more than wagons. Recent Bring a Trailer medians were about $26,500 for 88s and $25,500 for 109s. Factory sales to North America ended in 1974, so most US trucks are private imports; in California, pre-1976 petrol trucks are the easy route to registration.

    Check first: Feel along the chassis rear crossmember and outriggers and check the bulkhead footwells; serious rot in either can mean a new chassis or a body-off job.

  4. No. 4 · Best classic you can actually drive daily

    Land Rover Range Rover Classic

    YEARS 1970–96 (buy on rust, not year)PRICE $16k–28k for a driver

    The original luxury 4x4 still rides on coil springs with permanent four-wheel drive and a Rover V8, which makes it the most comfortable truck on this list by a distance. Factory US imports began in March 1987, four-door only, and the 1992-on County LWB with the 4.2 V8 is the long-wheelbase version.

    Prices are wide. Rough coil-sprung SWBs and diesel projects are still cheap, clean County LWBs and two-doors bring real money, and V8-swapped restomods sold for $115k–153k. The median across 20 recent Bring a Trailer sales was about $24,000. Trucks from 1993 on may have electronic air suspension; many have been converted to coils, which is fine if it was done with a proper kit.

    Check first: The aluminium skins hide a steel frame, so check the body mounts, rear crossmember, sills and inner wings before you look at the paint.

  5. No. 5 · Best American underdog

    International Scout II

    YEARS 1971–80 (Dana 44s front and rear from 1975)PRICE $18k–35k for a driver

    Built by a tractor company to beat the Jeep, the Scout II is a tough ladder-frame truck with the 345 V8 available, Dana 44 axles at both ends as standard from 1975, and a club and parts scene that makes keeping one alive easier than you'd expect. The factory line ticket lists the engine, axles, ratio and options, and replacements can be ordered.

    It's the most-traded Scout, and prices keep rising. Hagerty's median condition #2 value for 1972–80 Scout IIs is $42,200, and $64,750 for the Super Scout II. Stock 345 trucks mostly sold for $17k–24k in recent Bring a Trailer results, while the top of the market belongs to LS-swapped restomods.

    Check first: Scouts were never fully galvanized: start with the rear spring mounts, the body mounts and the area under the doors, where structural rust can mean a full rebuild.

  6. No. 6 · Best European classic

    Mercedes-Benz G-Class W460

    YEARS 1979–91 (front and rear locks standard from 1985)PRICE $25k–45k for a driver

    The original Geländewagen is hand-built, has diff locks front and rear (standard from 1985) and will outlive most owners if the rust and hydraulics are kept in check. Mercedes never sold it new in the US, but every W460 is now past the 25-year import line.

    Diesel W460s are now proper US collector trucks: 34 sold Bring a Trailer auctions from January 2025 to October 2026 ranged from $11,550 to $86,000, with a median of about $32,000. Hagerty, quoting Club G-Wagen co-founder Mike Serpe, says buyers pay up to 30% more for an all-original G. Know what you're buying: a 300 GD makes 88 PS and tops out around 79 mph.

    Check first: Rust starts in the rear body corners, so check those, the inner wings, the bottom of the rear door and the shock top mounts, then engage both diff locks on a loose surface.

  7. No. 7 · Best classic wagon

    Toyota Land Cruiser FJ60

    YEARS 1981–87 (US)PRICE $20k–40k for a driver

    The FJ60 is the Land Cruiser that learned to carry a family without forgetting it was a truck: the carbureted 2F six, a four-speed, leaf springs, a 23.8-gallon tank and round headlights. It's slow and thirsty, at 11 city and 18 highway by Corsetti's figures, and wonderful anyway.

    Clean, rust-free FJ60s keep climbing as the analog alternative to an 80 Series, and rust decides most of the price. Across 100 recent Bring a Trailer sales the median was about $29,100, and Hagerty has written that Land Cruiser wagons do nothing but go up in value. That describes what has happened, not what will.

    Check first: Drop the spare if you have to and look at the rear frame rails; Corsetti says if the frame is rusted through, walk away.

  8. No. 8 · Best classic Jeep

    Jeep CJ-7

    YEARS 1976–86 (look for a T-18A or T-176 gearbox)PRICE $13k–26k for a driver

    The CJ-7 is the classic Jeep that still makes sense: a 93.5 in wheelbase, real doors, AMC's 258 six and a big parts supply. Its Bring a Trailer market is the busiest of any classic Jeep, with a median of around $19,000 across about 83 sales, and original-engine Laredos and Renegades with honest tubs set the pace.

    The gearbox matters more than the trim. JeepCJ.com's guide wants a T-18A (1977–79) or T-176 (1980–86), and Novak says the light-duty SR-4, T-4 and T-5 suffer from sheared splines behind the 258. Swapped V8 and LS builds cluster in the mid-$30k to low-$40k range if they're done right.

    Check first: Probe the frame at the spring-shackle mounts and all six body mounts, and look for cracks or splice plates around the steering box, especially on lifted trucks.

Questions

Are classic 4x4 values still going up?

It depends on the truck, and past prices don't tell you future ones. Our October 2026 checks found early Bronco values down 12 percent in Hagerty's second-quarter 2026 data after years of climbing, FJ40 prices off their 2016 peak, and FJ60s and Series Land Rovers still rising. Buy one because you want to drive it.

Original or restomod?

Originals and restomods are different markets. On Bring a Trailer, V8-swapped FJ40s sold for a median of about $25,300, below stock trucks, while ICON FJ40s brought $98,500–140,000. Early Bronco restomods ran $122k–166.5k, and engine-swapped FJ62s had a median of about $44,000 against about $30,000 for all FJ62s. A swap done by a known builder can hold its value; an amateur one usually costs you.

How bad is too much rust?

Surface rust is fine. Structural rust in the frame, spring mounts, body mounts, bulkhead or rear crossmember is where it gets expensive, and every guide on this list says to check those first. If the frame or bulkhead is rotten, price the truck as a project, or walk away.

Can I register an imported classic?

Federally, NHTSA lets a vehicle at least 25 years old (from its date of manufacture) be imported without meeting US safety standards, so every truck on this list qualifies. States are another matter. In California, for example, petrol vehicles from 1975 and older are smog-exempt, but later direct imports may need CARB testing. Check your state's titling and emissions rules before you buy abroad.

What's the cheapest way into a classic 4x4?

A CJ-5 is still the cheapest classic CJ, at $8k–18k for a driver in our checks, and a first-generation Pajero or Montero runs $6.5k–12k. Both rust, so the same checks apply.

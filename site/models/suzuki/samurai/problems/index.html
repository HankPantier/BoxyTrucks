Exercise your lockers

Engage the lockers every six months or so, on almost any boxy truck. Both of my locker problems, on the 80 Series and the G500, came from systems that weren’t used often enough.

Suzuki Samurai problems to check before you buy

  1. Rust and hidden trail damageMotorTrend's advice is to check the underbody carefully, for rust and also for dents, scrapes and dried mud in hard-to-reach places, which all point to a hard off-road life. If you want a stock truck, an unmodified Samurai is less likely to have been abused.
  2. Transfer case mounts and frame hangersTrail Tough calls cracked transfer case mounts and a wallowed-out, bent rear frame hanger common Samurai stress failures, and lower gearing makes them worse. Look at the rubber mounts and the frame brackets on both sides of the case.
  3. Transfer case that won't shift or sticks in neutralMotorTrend lists bad transfer-case bushings that can lock the case in neutral. The usual culprit is the plastic 'shift sheet' under the lever. It's a cheap OEM or Delrin part, but check that 4WD and low range both engage.
  4. Oil leaks and carburetor mannersMotorTrend's known trouble spots include oil leaks at the distributor, leaking brake master cylinders, fiddly carburetors on 1986–89 trucks and failing ECMs on fuel-injected ones. A clean cold start and a steady idle are worth a lot.
  5. Quality of past modificationsMost Samurais have been modified. A well-built spring-over or engine swap saves you money; a bad one costs you. Check the steering geometry, brake lines at full droop, driveshaft angles and welds. Swapped engines may not pass emissions in some states, and Trail Tough notes that 1996-and-later 1.6 donors are OBD-II and won't be smog-legal in California.
  6. Is it an automatic? It's a swapEvery US Samurai had a manual gearbox, so an automatic means someone swapped it in. Ask what was done and look for the paperwork.
  7. Front knuckles weeping gear oilOil-soaked knuckles, loose steering and a vague front end point to worn kingpin bearings and inner axle seals. Trail Tough's rebuild kit covers both sides, and it's a common catch-up job.
  8. Know which Samurai you're buyingFor the US, 1986–89 trucks are carbureted (AMSOIL's lookup lists engine code 5) and 1990–95 are injected (code 3). The '1988½' update changed the ride, the gearing and the transfer case drain plugs. Santana and Gypsy trucks use the same basic design, but many parts differ.

What to bring when you look at a Suzuki Samurai

Six inexpensive tools that catch most of the problems above before you hand over the money. Or book a specialist: see how to get a pre-purchase inspection.

  • Bluetooth OBD-II scannerReads stored and pending codes, and shows the readiness monitors. If several say "not ready", someone may have cleared the codes just before you arrived.
    Amazon ↗
  • Paint thickness gaugeThick readings on one panel mean body filler or a respray, which usually means an accident or rust repair the seller hasn’t mentioned.
    Amazon ↗
  • Inspection borescope cameraFits through drain holes into frame rails, rockers and sills, where these trucks rust from the inside out.
    Amazon ↗
  • Bright LED flashlightFor the underside: leaks at the axle seals, transfer case and rear main, and the frame behind the rear wheels.
    Amazon ↗
  • Telescoping inspection mirrorShows the back of the engine, the top of the fuel tank and the inner fenders without crawling under.
    Amazon ↗
  • Compression testerOn a high-mileage or long-parked engine, uneven compression is the cheapest way to spot a tired engine before you pay for it. Diesels need a diesel-rated tester.

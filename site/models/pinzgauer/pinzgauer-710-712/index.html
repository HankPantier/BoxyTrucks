PART 01

Overview

Where it came from, why it matters, and how it changed year to year.

The story

Most 4x4s start as a ladder frame with axles bolted underneath. The Pinzgauer starts as a steel tube. Steyr-Daimler-Puch began developing it in 1964 as the bigger brother of its Haflinger. Pilot production started in 1969, and the 710 (4x4) and 712 (6x6) went into series production in Graz in 1971. They have an air-cooled 2.5-liter petrol four, swing axles with portal reduction hubs, hydraulic diff locks you engage on the move, and a 24-volt system built to military spec. The result is one of the most capable off-roaders ever built, and one of the strangest to drive on the street.

THE HAFLINGER'S BIG BROTHER

The Pinzgauer, like the Haflinger, was developed in Graz under Erich Ledwinka. He was the son of Tatra's Hans Ledwinka and served as Steyr-Daimler-Puch's chief engineer from 1950 to 1976. The Austrian sources say the name comes from the Pinzgauer horse of the Pinzgau region. English-language sources usually credit the region's cattle instead. To win the Austrian Bundesheer contract, it had to beat the Husar from rival maker ÖAF, and it did.

Where the Haflinger carried its engine in the tail, the Pinzgauer put it up front over the front axle. The backbone-tube layout made it easy to build many different bodies on the same chassis. Steyr's two main customers were the Austrian and Swiss armies, and Yugoslavia bought 3,975 of them in both 4x4 and 6x6 form. Wikipedia counts 18,349 first-generation 710s and 712s built between 1971 and 1985, for civilian and military buyers.

HOW IT WORKS

The central tube contains the drivetrain. The transfer case and differentials sit inside it, and the swing half-axles pivot from it, ending in portal gearboxes that drop the drive by 2.266:1 at each wheel. No diff housing hangs underneath to catch on rocks, so ground clearance is 335 mm (13.2 in). SDP-Pinzgauer's technical data quotes 45-degree approach and departure angles, 700 mm (27.6 in) of fording depth and a 100 percent climbing ability. Steering is a ZF-Gemmer worm and roller with five turns lock to lock, and there is no power assistance.

The engine was designed for the vehicle: a 2,499 cc pushrod four with twin Zenith 36 NDIX carburetors and separate pressure and return oil pumps, so it isn't starved of oil on steep slopes. BaT lists it at 87 hp and 133 lb-ft. It drives a ZF five-speed and a two-speed transfer case, which gives ten forward gears. Front-wheel drive and the front and rear lockers are engaged hydraulically with levers in the center stack. There is no center differential, so the Pinzgauer runs as a part-time 4x4. Everything electrical runs on 24 volts through two 12-volt batteries and push-pull circuit breakers.

FOUR WHEELS OR SIX

The 710 was the more popular model, but Steyr designed the 6x6 into the platform from the start. The 712's rear bogie uses inverted parabolic leaf springs where the 710 uses coils. It raises payload from 1,000 to 1,500 kg and seats 14 in M (troop carrier) form, against 10 in a 710M. Owners say the 712 rides better on the road and the 710 copes better in tight spaces. Bodies included the M soft top, the K five-door hardtop, the T flatbed, ambulances, workshop and fire trucks, and the 712 DK crew cab.

Production figures from Das Puch Automobil Buch show the mix changing. In 1978 Graz built 444 710s and 167 712s, and from 1981 the 712 outsold the 710 every year. Late in production, some 712s, mostly ambulances, got a 2.7-liter version of the air-cooled four. Otherwise the first generation hardly changed in 15 years. That helps if you are hunting for parts, and it means there are no particularly good or bad years to look out for.

AFTER THE ARMY

In 1985 the redesigned 716 and 718 took over. They had a turbocharged VW diesel six, disc brakes, a slightly wider track and an optional automatic. The petrol trucks stayed in service for decades and then came onto the surplus market, mostly from Switzerland. In America they became tour buses as well as campers. Wikipedia notes that the Navajo Nation was still using a small fleet for visitor trips in Canyon de Chelly in 2024. Car executive Bob Lutz bought an ex-Swiss 710M in 1991.

Today a 710 makes a strong case as a low-cost expedition truck, as long as you accept its limits. Bring a Trailer sales from 2023 to 2026 mostly land between about $10,000 and $25,000. Parts are still made or stocked by specialists such as Swiss Army Vehicles in Arkansas and Expedition Imports. And the community has documented almost every job, from the 0.2 mm valve lash to fitting split axle boots.

SIDEBAR · THE SWISS PIPELINE

Why most American Pinzgauers come from Switzerland

The Swiss Army bought petrol Pinzgauers in large numbers and kept them for decades. As the Swiss and Austrian armies retired their trucks, demand grew in the US. Swiss Army Vehicles in Fayetteville, Arkansas, says it has dealt in European military vehicles and parts since 1998. The SDP-Pinzgauer owners' site warned buyers that the trucks' infrared-reflecting paint fades quickly and isn't very water resistant. The Swiss Army repainted the trucks by hand at regular intervals, so a shiny coat can hide plenty.

There are other Swiss details too. Swiss trucks use a single amber bulb for turn and brake lights plus a red tail lamp, where Austrian trucks have separate red brake lights. The 710K radio command trucks, or Funkkommandowagen, carry their own wiring. Some trucks arrive with paperwork as well: the ex-Lutz 710M that sold on BaT in 2026 came with a Swiss registration document alongside its Texas title. The checklist also notes that the trucks were steam-cleaned before shipping, and still advises lifting the footwell plates, where 'most likely you will find half of the Alps.'

PART 02

Specs & capability

How it’s built, what it can do, and what fits under it.

How it’s built

There's no ladder frame. A torsion-resistant central tube carries the transfer case and axle drives, with swing half-axles pivoting off it and portal reduction gearboxes at the hubs. The engine is an air-cooled 2.5-liter pushrod four mounted over the front axle. It drives through a ZF five-speed and a two-speed transfer case, with hydraulically engaged front drive and diff locks. Brakes are dual-circuit power-assisted drums, and the parking brake is a disc on the main driveshaft.

Buying one

What to pay, what to check, and how to tell a good one from a clone.

What to check before you buy

  1. 01
    Hunt rust where the Alps hideThe SDP pre-purchase checklist lists the spots: both bottom corners of the air tunnel (sound-deadening foam holds water), the fuel filler neck, the doors (seams, bottoms and inside the window channels), the footwells under the diamond plates, behind the wooden rub rails, the bedside-to-tub seams and the rear door. Owners add the battery and tool boxes and the heat exchanger. Shiny paint over these areas proves nothing.
  2. 02
    Check the spring cups and swivel ballsThe upper spring cups rust through and make an ugly repair, so check them along with the limiting straps. Flaking or pitted plating on the front swivel balls is, in the SDP checklist's words, 'a costly repair.'
  3. 03
    Look for red flakes in the fuel filterRed flakes mean the fuel tank's lining is peeling. The checklist says 'be very careful!' and owners on the Portal Hub still ask about tanks that need stripping and relining.
  4. 04
    Run a compression testThe absolute numbers matter less than consistency. The SDP guide accepts 120–160 psi as long as the cylinders are within about 10 psi of each other, and warns that cylinder 4 (the rearmost) can have overheated. Some owners also do a leak-down test and look inside the oil filler tube for yellow, greasy residue.
  5. 05
    Engage everythingOn a test drive, try low and high range, front drive and both lockers. The SDP guide notes that disengaging sometimes takes a few left-right turns, which is normal. A locker that won't engage usually points to the hydraulics: old fluid, cracked rubber elbows or a seized slave bleeder.
  6. 06
    Get under it with a creeperLook for leaks at the portals, diffs, gearbox and transfer case. Some weeping is normal on a 40-year-old truck. Check for torn axle boots, and with the transfer case in neutral, feel for excessive backlash in the driveline. Oil inside a brake drum means a leaking wheel-drive seal.
  7. 07
    Drive it at speedIf it shakes, engage front drive. If the vibration stops, suspect driveshaft balance. If it doesn't, the wheels were probably never balanced: the SDP checklist says the Swiss didn't balance them. The rims are lug-centric, so a tire shop needs an adapter. Owners also describe a low rumble from the flexible front driveshaft that grows with mileage.
  8. 08
    Test the 24-volt electrics and the canvasOpen the black distribution box under the right rear corner and check for corrosion. Then check the dash breakers, the fuel and speed gauges (they can stick at zero or full) and the wiper motor. Look over the tarps too: replacing UV-damaged canvas is expensive.

Spotting the real thing

The 710/712 barely changed in 15 years, so identification comes down to wheels, body code, engine and which army a truck served with. Don't confuse a petrol truck with a later Turbo D.

  1. ID
    710 vs 712Count the axles. The 710 is the 4x4 on a 2,200 mm wheelbase with rear coil springs. The 712 is the 6x6, with a leaf-sprung rear bogie on a 2,000 + 980 mm wheelbase.
  2. ID
    Body codesM is the soft top with rear bench seats, K the hardtop station wagon, T the flatbed and AMB the ambulance (AMB-Y, or AMB-S with an air-portable shelter). On the 712 there are also W (workshop), DK (crew cab), FW (fire truck) and FLA.
  3. ID
    Petrol 710/712 vs Turbo D 716/718The 710/712 has the air-cooled 2.5-liter petrol four, a five-speed manual, drum brakes and narrower tires. The 716/718 (from 1985) has a water-cooled VW turbodiesel six, disc brakes, a slightly wider track, bigger tires and an optional automatic.
  4. ID
    Swiss vs Austrian trucksSwiss trucks have a single amber bulb for turn and brake plus a red tail lamp. Austrian trucks have amber turn signals with separate red brake and tail lamps. Swiss 710K radio command trucks (Funkkommandowagen) have their own wiring.
  5. ID
    EngineCheck the engine plate and bore. The standard engine is 2,499 cc (92 x 94 mm). Late 712s, mostly ambulances, can have the 2.7-liter air-cooled four.

Importing & legality

Steyr never sold the 710/712 new in the US, so every American truck is a used import, almost always ex-military. Most are ex-Swiss Army, with some ex-Austrian. Every 710 and 712 is now more than 40 years old, comfortably past the 25-year line that lets an older vehicle into the US without meeting federal safety standards. OffGrid noted in 2017 that early Pinzgauers are old enough to be exempt from many US import equipment requirements.

The model-specific question is paperwork. Ask for the import entry, any Swiss or Austrian registration or army documents, and a US title in the seller's name. The ex-Lutz 710M that sold on BaT in 2026 came with a Swiss registration document and a clean Texas title. Established dealers such as Swiss Army Vehicles in Arkansas have sold ex-military trucks for more than 20 years. Rules for registering former military vehicles vary by state, so check with your DMV before you buy.

Post-purchase baseline

After you buy it

Assume nothing was done after the truck left the army. On a 710 or 712 that means about a dozen separate gear-oil fill points, a hydraulic system for the lockers as well as the brakes and clutch, and an air-cooled engine that depends on clean fins and correct valve lash. The intervals come from the factory schedule (in hours, page 214 of the manual, per Portal Hub owners), but most US owners simply change everything on purchase and then once a year. Fit new copper washers on every plug.

SYMPTOMSLockers or front drive that are slow to engage or won't engage at all, dark fluid in the reservoir, or seepage at the rubber elbows and T-piece under the reservoir.
THE FIXEmpty the reservoir with a syringe or baster. Pull the reservoir and work the cracked rubber elbows and T-piece off the metal lines, then fit new ones with their tube-ring clamps and refill with fresh DOT 3. One Portal Hub owner checked a power bleeder held 10 psi, then filled it with DOT 3. He dropped each locker lever and opened each slave bleeder until clean fluid ran out. Buy spare bleeder valves first: his 4x4 slave's bleeder was so seized that fluid leaked out around the threads.
TOOLS & PARTSPower bleeder (owner used a Motive) · baster or syringe · foil drip tray · DOT 3 brake fluid · spare slave bleeder valves.

Rebuild the mechanical fuel pump →
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · ≈ An evening (the write-up's author is not a mechanic)SHOP COST · Repair kit about $38 at Swiss Army Vehicles
SYMPTOMSLong cranking after the truck has sat for a few days, getting worse over time, and a wet patch under the pump.
THE FIXRemove the engine cover and air filter. Clamp the fuel hoses a few inches from the ends and twist them off the pump, then undo the two 13 mm base nuts and lift the pump, its push rod and the plastic guide out. Clean the outside before opening it. Replace the diaphragm and gaskets in the top section. Then take out the side panel and spring, remove the lever-pivot circlips and fit the new lower parts (the kit includes spare circlips). Grease the lever, refit the pump with new gaskets, and crank in 10-second bursts until fuel arrives.
TOOLS & PARTS13 mm open-ended wrench · small and medium flat screwdrivers · two hose clamps · grease. The write-up's kit was part number 712.1.08.560.0.

Adjust the drum brakes (and renew tired wheel cylinders) →
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · ≈ An afternoon for all four cornersSHOP COST · Adjustment is free. A full cylinder set is about $379 (710) or $468 (712) at Swiss Army Vehicles
SYMPTOMSA long pedal, uneven braking, or a wet backing plate from a weeping wheel cylinder.
THE FIXRaise one wheel at a time onto stands and remove the wheel. Refit two or three lug nuts to hold the drum square. Take out the adjusting plug in the drum with a 10 mm Allen key (it seals with a copper ring, like the fluid plugs) and check that at least 1/8 inch of lining is left. Each front has two star adjusters (duplex shoes) and each rear has one. Turn each adjuster until the shoe locks, then back it off until the drum turns freely; the SDP guide says about five clicks. Pump the pedal hard several times, and again while driving slowly, before you trust it.
TOOLS & PARTS10 mm Allen key · brake spoon · jack and stands · torque wrench (one owner torques the lug nuts to 110 lb-ft) · flashlight.

Set the valve lash →
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · ≈ Under 30 minutes with practice (SDP guide)SHOP COST · Four rocker-cover gaskets, about $26–49 a set
SYMPTOMSClattering valvetrain, or simply an unknown service history. It's a routine job on this pushrod air-cooled engine.
THE FIXWith the engine cold, remove the engine cover, the throttle linkage and all four rocker covers. Bring cylinder 1 to TDC on compression, with the timing mark aligned and the rotor pointing at the No. 1 lead. Set both valves to 0.2 mm (intake and exhaust) by feel, lock the adjuster and check again. Turn the crank half a turn at a time to do cylinders 2, 4 and 3, following the 1-2-4-3 firing order. Fit new gaskets. The SDP guide says never to use blue RTV and to check carburetor sync afterward. It also warns that a deformed head gasket can lose enough oil on one highway trip to ruin the engine.
TOOLS & PARTS0.2 mm feeler gauge · screwdriver · combination wrenches · chalk · socket to turn the crank · carburetor synchronizer.

Free a stuck clutch or replace it →
DIFFICULTY · 4 / 5TIME · Big job: the gearbox comes outSHOP COST · Complete clutch kit about $408 at Swiss Army Vehicles
SYMPTOMSThe engine runs, but no gear will engage after the truck has sat for months, because the disc has rusted to the flywheel or pressure plate. Or the clutch slips or grabs suddenly.
THE FIXFor a lightly stuck disc, put it in gear with the engine off, make sure the way ahead is clear, and start it on the starter. The truck lurches, and the disc often frees once you work the pedal. If that fails, the gearbox has to come out. Support the engine sump on a stand, wire the two driveshaft halves together (they are balanced as a pair), undo the shift-rod nuts (17 and 19 mm) and the six 13 mm driveshaft bolts, then strap the 39 kg gearbox to a cross-beam and slide it back. Use a proper alignment tool when fitting the disc, and torque the bolts to the 22–25 Nm the write-up calls for.
TOOLS & PARTSClutch alignment tool · 10, 13, 15, 17 and 19 mm sockets · 6 mm Allen key · torque wrench (20 Nm range) · two jacks and stands · ratchet strap.

Replace rusted spring cups →
DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · ≈ 2 hours per corner, not counting painting (SDP / Expedition Imports)SHOP COST · Cups run about $140–200 each new. An aluminum rear upper upgrade is $159
SYMPTOMSUpper spring cups (and their studs) rusted through, because dirt and water collect in the cup on top of each coil.
THE FIXSet the parking brake and jack the center of the truck. Remove the wheel, then use the Pinzgauer's own jack under the hub to control the suspension. Remove the lower shock bolt and the limiting-strap bolt, lower the hub until the coil is extended, and fit the factory spring compressor arms, levering between body and coil for room. Jack the hub to compress the coil, fit the lower plates, tighten, lower and lift out the spring. Fit new cups, gasket and centering ring on the bench, then reverse the steps. The write-up, by Scott Ingham of Expedition Imports, recommends treating and painting the coil while it's out.
TOOLS & PARTSFactory spring compressor kit · Pinzgauer jack plus a floor jack · pry bar · impact or air tools help.

Adjust the driveshaft parking brake →
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · Several rounds of trial and errorSHOP COST · Adjustment is free. Disc with lining about $99 each (two per truck)
SYMPTOMSThe handbrake won't hold the truck on a slope.
THE FIXThis follows a Portal Hub write-up based on the manual. The handle should pull out 90–100 mm (about 4 inches) to engage, and the pin at the brake lever at the back must travel the same distance. Measure both. If they differ, take out the pin and adjust the cable length, then set the brake nut on the other side so the brake fully engages. The nut is sensitive, and 90 degrees can make a big difference, so expect several rounds. Test on a hill without using the clutch. Finally, pre-tension the cable with two or three clockwise twists, as seen from the front.
TOOLS & PARTSRuler and tape · wrenches · a hill.

Asked on every forum

Pinzgauer 710 / 712 questions, answered

What's the biggest tire I can fit without a lift?

About 33 inches. Portal Hub owners settle on 255/85R16 as the sweet spot, roughly the height of the original Maloyas (which they measure at about 245 mm wide), and 265/75R16 is close to stock size. 285/75R16 goes on but rubs at full lock, and one owner says Discount Tire stopped mounting that size on the narrow factory rims. Another owner's rule: 33s are the maximum 'unless you like wheel well rubbing on the right front side,' and 32s are the smallest worth running.

SOURCE · PORTAL HUB
Can I legally drive one in the US?

The 710 and 712 were never sold new in the US, but every one is now well over 25 years old, which is why ex-Swiss and ex-Austrian trucks have been imported for years. Dealers such as Swiss Army Vehicles sell them titled. The ex-Lutz 710M that sold on BaT in 2026 came with a Swiss registration document and a clean Texas title. Ask for the import and title paperwork, and check with your state before you buy.

SOURCE · BAT / SAV
How fast will it cruise?

The SDP technical data gives top speeds of 110 km/h (68 mph) for the 710 and 100 km/h (62 mph) for the 712. One owner on stock tires finds 60 mph comfortable and says the truck starts to feel strained at about 63. It's loud too: owners joke that at highway speed you can't hear the tires.

SOURCE · SDP / PORTAL HUB
What fuel economy and range should I expect?

Factory figures are 14.2 L/100 km (16.6 mpg) for the 710 and 15.8 L/100 km (14.9 mpg) for the 712, from a 75-liter (19.8 US gal) tank. Owners quoted by OffGrid report 15–16 mpg at 60 mph. Wikipedia gives a range of over 400 km, or nearly 700 km with the optional 125-liter tank.

SOURCE · SDP / OFFGRID
How much can it tow and carry?

Rated payload is 1,000 kg (2,205 lb) for the 710 and 1,500 kg (3,307 lb) for the 712. Both are rated to tow 5,000 kg (11,023 lb) on the road. Off-road, the rating drops to 1,500 kg (3,307 lb) for the 710 and 1,800 kg (3,968 lb) for the 712.

SOURCE · SDP TECH DATA
710 or 712?

Owners quoted by OffGrid say the 712 rides better on the road and the 710 copes better in tight spaces. The 712 carries 500 kg more and seats 14 in M form against 10, but its turning circle is 12.28 m against 10.37 m. It also has a third axle to maintain and usually costs more: 712s have sold on BaT for $9,500 (an ambulance) to $24,250.

SOURCE · OFFGRID / SDP / BAT
Does the air-cooled engine overheat?

Not if the cooling system is intact. One owner has driven a 710K through 14 southern Arizona summers at 110–115°F without trouble, keeping the fan and every cooling tin in place and the fins clean. The more common hot-weather problem is vapor lock after shutdown, when heat soaks into the intake runners. One owner says SDP added intake insulation partway through 1973 production, and Swiss Army Vehicles sells the insulation pieces.

SOURCE · PORTAL HUB
Why does it use oil?

Partly by design. One experienced owner explains that the exhaust valve guides have no seals, so a little oil gets through to help cool the exhaust valves. Two quarts in 1,000 miles is a lot, though. Look for leaks around the cylinders, the oil cooler and the oil pan (its small bolts often work loose), then do a compression test.

SOURCE · PORTAL HUB / SDP
What oil should I use?

Opinions differ. A long-time Portal Hub member says to use straight 30-weight, as the operating instructions specify, with a ZDDP additive, and warns that thinner oils get burned past the valve guides and scuff pistons. Others run mineral 20W-50 solid-lifter oils such as Valvoline VR1 or Lucas, or Rotella T4. Owners who explain their choice tend to avoid synthetics and change the oil every 3,000 miles or once a year.

SOURCE · PORTAL HUB
Are parts still available?

Mostly, yes. In the US, Swiss Army Vehicles (Fayetteville, Arkansas) and Expedition Imports list parts by factory group, from fuel pump kits to new Zenith carbs, and many original part numbers are still in stock. One owner says the prices fall 'somewhere between Lexus and airplane part pricing.' The usual advice is to rebuild rather than throw away, especially hydraulic parts.

SOURCE · SAV / PORTAL HUB
Will it fit in my garage?

Factory height is 2,045 mm (80.5 in). Owners measure about 7 ft 2 in on a 712M with a low antenna mount and 33-inch tires, and about 7 ft 6 in on a K with its roof hatch. One 710M owner gets through a door about 83¾ inches high by running 225/85R16 tires, replacing the antenna mount with a flat plate and weighing the back down.

SOURCE · SDP / PORTAL HUB
Can I use ordinary lug nuts?

No. Expedition Imports warns that the Pinzgauer needs ball-seat lug nuts. A conical nut contacts the wheel wrongly, can let it come loose and can even split the wheel. The factory rims are also lug-centric, not hub-centric, so a tire shop needs an adapter to balance them.

SOURCE · EXPEDITION IMPORTS / SDP

Pinzgauer 710 / 712 problems: 8 things to check

The problems owners and specialists flag most on the Steyr-Puch Pinzgauer 710 / 712, the repairs they end up doing and what those cost, plus the years and recalls to watch. Sourced, not scraped.

Pinzgauer 710 / 712 problems to check before you buy

  1. Hunt rust where the Alps hideThe SDP pre-purchase checklist lists the spots: both bottom corners of the air tunnel (sound-deadening foam holds water), the fuel filler neck, the doors (seams, bottoms and inside the window channels), the footwells under the diamond plates, behind the wooden rub rails, the bedside-to-tub seams and the rear door. Owners add the battery and tool boxes and the heat exchanger. Shiny paint over these areas proves nothing.
  2. Check the spring cups and swivel ballsThe upper spring cups rust through and make an ugly repair, so check them along with the limiting straps. Flaking or pitted plating on the front swivel balls is, in the SDP checklist's words, 'a costly repair.'
  3. Look for red flakes in the fuel filterRed flakes mean the fuel tank's lining is peeling. The checklist says 'be very careful!' and owners on the Portal Hub still ask about tanks that need stripping and relining.
  4. Run a compression testThe absolute numbers matter less than consistency. The SDP guide accepts 120–160 psi as long as the cylinders are within about 10 psi of each other, and warns that cylinder 4 (the rearmost) can have overheated. Some owners also do a leak-down test and look inside the oil filler tube for yellow, greasy residue.
  5. Engage everythingOn a test drive, try low and high range, front drive and both lockers. The SDP guide notes that disengaging sometimes takes a few left-right turns, which is normal. A locker that won't engage usually points to the hydraulics: old fluid, cracked rubber elbows or a seized slave bleeder.
  6. Get under it with a creeperLook for leaks at the portals, diffs, gearbox and transfer case. Some weeping is normal on a 40-year-old truck. Check for torn axle boots, and with the transfer case in neutral, feel for excessive backlash in the driveline. Oil inside a brake drum means a leaking wheel-drive seal.
  7. Drive it at speedIf it shakes, engage front drive. If the vibration stops, suspect driveshaft balance. If it doesn't, the wheels were probably never balanced: the SDP checklist says the Swiss didn't balance them. The rims are lug-centric, so a tire shop needs an adapter. Owners also describe a low rumble from the flexible front driveshaft that grows with mileage.
  8. Test the 24-volt electrics and the canvasOpen the black distribution box under the right rear corner and check for corrosion. Then check the dash breakers, the fuel and speed gauges (they can stick at zero or full) and the wiper motor. Look over the tarps too: replacing UV-damaged canvas is expensive.

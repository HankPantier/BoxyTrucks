PART 01

Overview

Where it came from, why it matters, and how it changed year to year.

The story

Steyr-Daimler-Puch started work on a second Pinzgauer in 1980, and after six years of development it reached production in the mid-1980s: German Wikipedia puts the switch in 1985, English Wikipedia says the first trucks rolled off the line in 1986. The recipe was the 710 and 712 with a modern heart. The air-cooled petrol four made way for a liquid-cooled 2.4-liter Volkswagen turbo-diesel six, and the new names followed the axle count: 716 for the 4×4 and 718 for the 6×6.

THE SAME BONES, A DIFFERENT ANIMAL

Underneath it is still a Pinzgauer: a central backbone tube with the drivetrain inside it, swing half-axles, portal hubs and pneumatically engaged differential locks. What changed was everything you touch. The Turbo D got disc brakes, a slightly wider track, bigger tires and, on most trucks, a ZF four-speed automatic, with a five-speed manual as the option. The 716 carries what the old 6×6 712 carried, and the 718 carries more.

Owners on the Real 4x4s Pinzgauer forum are blunt about how little interchanges. 'Don't use anything you read for the 710/712 for the 716/718,' says one long-time owner. 'Very few things apply.' The wheel bolt pattern, outer axle housings and portals all differ, and the 716 got a self-levelling rear air spring that the petrol trucks never had.

P80, P90, P93 AND THE BRITISH YEARS

The Turbo D was built in batches, with revisions that owners know by the year they were designed: P80 for the original, P90 for a 1990 update, and P93 for the 1993 version, which added an intercooled engine. The cooling system went through four versions, and the radiator cover (the 'nose') grew with them. One owner's P80 716 had the small nose and the 105 hp engine without an intercooler.

In 2000 Magna, which by then owned Steyr-Daimler-Puch, sold the Pinzgauer rights to Automotive Technik Ltd in the UK. Owners say the tooling moved from Graz between 2000 and 2002, and Austrian-built trucks carry chassis numbers below 6000. ATL fitted a five-cylinder 2.5-liter VW TDI from 2002–03 to meet Euro 3. After a run of takeovers that ended with BAE Systems, UK production ended around 2007.

SOLDIERS, FIRE CREWS AND TWENTY AMERICAN 718s

Almost every Turbo D went to a government. The British Army used them as light gun tractors for the 105 mm Light Gun, New Zealand bought 321 in eight variants as its Light Operational Vehicle, and Malaysia took 168 716 gun tractors and 164 718 mortar carriers. Fire brigades, mountain rescue teams and oil companies bought the rest, often with bodies built to order.

The US Army bought about 20 turbo-diesel 718Ms in the early 1990s for special operations deep reconnaissance. They served in both Gulf wars before being phased out around 2010–11. Some of these were then sold as surplus, and they remain the rare Turbo Ds with an American paper trail. Everything else in the US arrived as a 25-year import.

SIDEBAR · THE GOVERNMENT LIQUIDATION TRUCKS

How a handful of special-ops 718s ended up in barns

In 2011, several Real 4x4s members bought ex-US military 718s through government surplus sales and compared notes. They reported that the trucks were set up as P90s, though some VINs suggested they had been built in the 1980s and upgraded before delivery. One importer said the upgraded trucks had their cowls cut by hand to clear the bigger radiator hoses.

The trucks came with lever ignition switches and military fittings, and some came without doors, windshields or roll bars. One buyer bought the take-off parts from another owner, then used wiper arms and heater parts from a G-Wagen and seat belts from an old Mercedes 280 SE. An importer converted the lever switch to a keyed one in about eight minutes using a lock and switch shared with the G-Wagen.

PART 02

Specs & capability

How it’s built, what it can do, and what fits under it.

How it’s built

The 716 and 718 keep the Pinzgauer's central-tube chassis, with the transfer case and differentials built into the backbone and portal reduction hubs at each wheel for ground clearance. Power comes from a Volkswagen 2.4-liter belt-driven SOHC inline-six turbo-diesel, the same family as the Volvo 740's D24T, driving a ZF 4HP22 automatic or a five-speed manual. The electrics run on 24 volts.

Buying one

What to pay, what to check, and how to tell a good one from a clone.

Exercise your lockers

Engage the lockers every six months or so, on almost any boxy truck. Both of my locker problems, on the 80 Series and the G500, came from systems that weren’t used often enough.

What to check before you buy

  1. 01
    Buy the complete oneA UK specialist's first rule is to get the truck with everything fitted, because spares are never easy. Another veteran owner says to plan on buying two: a replacement diff can cost as much as some trucks sell for.
  2. 02
    Test the diff locks and low rangeOperate every switch and lever and make sure the diff locks engage and release, and that low range works. Lazy unlocking usually points to the air system: heat-damaged lines near the exhaust, a leaking lock box, or a sticky valve under the centre dash.
  3. 03
    Check the 716's rear air springOn a 716, disconnect the level control arm, push it fully up, run the engine above 1,800 rpm and check that the air bag inflates within about 30 seconds. A 716 that sits on its tail in the morning has a leak. The parts are expensive.
  4. 04
    Autos: watch the first-to-second shift coldThe ZF 4HP22 is generally reliable. Its best-known fault is sticking in first when cold, usually from a contaminated governor. Also check for harsh or slipping shifts, a worn park brake and a disconnected breather.
  5. 05
    Look for oil where it shouldn't beCheck the axle tubes and yellow swivel boots for leaks, the portals for weeping seals, and the engine for valve-cover, rear cam seal and oil-cooler leaks. Look in the coolant too: oil there points first to the oil cooler, then to the head gasket.
  6. 06
    Listen at speed and from coldStart it from cold and look for smoke and leaks. Big miles can mean a tired rear diff, which whines at speed. Higher mileage alone isn't a big worry for the VW engine, which is relatively easy to source parts for.
  7. 07
    Work out the year and buildInternational VINs have no model year, so titles and insurers can get it wrong. The chassis number (last digits) and the transmission and transfer-case plates are better guides, and only a build certificate from the factory is definitive.
  8. 08
    Check the paperwork before you payEx-military trucks were never certified for US roads, and some states treat them differently. A Colorado DMV clerk flagged a Pinzgauer VIN as military in 2022, and owners say California is especially hard. Buy one already titled in your state, or ask your DMV first.

Spotting the real thing

Pinzgauers were built to order in batches and often updated before delivery, so the badge on the nose doesn't settle much. Work out the revision, the engine and the paperwork before you pay.

  1. ID
    716/718 vs 710/712The Turbo D has a bulged radiator nose, disc brakes and a 5×130 wheel pattern. The petrol 710/712 has a flatter front, drums and a different bolt pattern, and few drivetrain parts interchange.
  2. ID
    P80, P90 or P93Look at the nose: the P80 has the smallest radiator cover, and it grew with the P90 and P93. P93s have an intercooler. Some P80s were factory-upgraded to P90 spec; one importer says trucks upgraded outside the factory have cowls cut by hand to clear the radiator hoses.
  3. ID
    VINPinzgauers use the international 17-digit VIN with the WMI VAG, for example VAG71623110001645 on a 1990 716K. The model number follows the WMI. Position 10 means left-hand drive (1) or right-hand drive (2), not the model year, which confuses US insurers. The last digits are the chassis number.
  4. ID
    Build yearNo model year is stamped on a 716 or 718. The transmission and transfer-case data plates sometimes carry a date (for those parts only). A build certificate from the factory is the only definitive record.
  5. ID
    Ex-US special-ops 718US military trucks were P90s or upgraded P80s with lever-action ignition switches; some had a control for a crawler reduction box. Ask for the surplus sale paperwork.

Importing & legality

Steyr-Daimler-Puch never sold the Turbo D new in the US. The only domestic trucks are the roughly 20 718Ms the US Army bought for special operations, some of which were sold as surplus around 2011. Every other 716 and 718 here is an import, usually ex-military from the UK or elsewhere in Europe.

Under NHTSA's 25-year exemption, every Austrian-built truck (production ended in Graz in 2000) is now eligible, and UK-built ATL trucks become eligible as each reaches 25 years from its build date, so the 2.5 TDI trucks are still mostly too new. Because the VIN carries no model year, use a factory build certificate to prove age.

Federal eligibility doesn't guarantee a plate. A veteran owner notes that each state decides; his 716 was registered in California by mistake and later grandfathered, and owners describe California as the hardest state. In 2022 a Colorado county DMV flagged a Pinzgauer VIN as military and refused it for on-road use, while a 718 bought new from Steyr by a German civilian company was expected to be easier to register. Insurers may balk at the 'abnormal' VIN too.

Post-purchase baseline

After you buy it

Most Turbo Ds have spent years in storage, so treat every fluid as unknown. Two things are unusual: the wheel drives take a polyglycol gear oil that doesn't mix with mineral or ordinary synthetic oils, and each diff and box has two plugs, with the lower one as the fill level. Owners also stress that 716/718 specs differ from the 710/712, so use a Turbo D manual.

Asked on every forum

Pinzgauer 716 / 718 questions, answered

What's the biggest tire I can fit without a lift?

The Turbo D's swing-axle suspension has no lift kits to speak of, so this is about gearing more than clearance. Owners' favorite is 255/85R16, about the same height as a 285/75R16 but narrower, which one Belgian owner says 'just fits perfect'. German Wikipedia says the trucks often run 235/85R16s, and ATL put 285/75R16 BFGoodrich Mud-Terrains on its 2.5 TDI 718. A US owner advises against 285s on a 2.4 because of the extra drag and scrub, and found 34-inch 8.25 tires too tall for the engine.

SOURCE · REAL 4X4S FORUM
What's the difference between a 716 and a 718?

The axle count. A 716 is the 4×4 and a 718 is the 6×6, and the same body letters apply: M for soft top, K for hard top and so on. One owner says the extra axle is the only drivetrain difference, though the 716 has the self-levelling rear air spring and the 718's rear suspension is basically a 712's, rated for a heavier load.

SOURCE · REAL 4X4S FORUM
What are P80, P90 and P93?

Revision levels, named for the years they were designed. P80 is the original, with a small radiator nose and no intercooler. P90 brought revised cooling and a larger nose in 1990. P93 added the intercooled engine. The cooling system changed at chassis 265, 1791 and 1937, according to an old TD manual quoted by an owner.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · REAL 4X4S
How fast will it go?

A 716 should cruise comfortably at 65 mph and touch 75 in short bursts, according to a UK owner. Wikipedia gives top speeds of 110 km/h (68 mph) for the 4×4 and 100 km/h (62 mph) for the 6×6. With the manual box you have to wind each gear out, or the turbo falls off boost in the next gear.

SOURCE · REAL 4X4S FORUM
Automatic or manual?

Most Turbo Ds have the ZF automatic, and owners prefer it. The auto hides the old turbo-diesel's lag by kicking down. One veteran also says the auto helps protect the drivetrain by controlling the torque thrown at it.

SOURCE · REAL 4X4S FORUM
What fuel economy should I expect?

Not much. One UK P93 718 owner measured about 20 L/100 km (11.8 mpg US), and a US owner remembered about 11 mpg from his P80 716. An ATL 718K with the 2.5 TDI and automatic returned about 15 mpg.

SOURCE · REAL 4X4S FORUM
How much can it tow?

Wikipedia gives 5,000 kg (11,023 lb) on road for both the 4×4 and the 6×6, and 1,500 kg (4×4) or 1,800 kg (6×6) off road. Those figures are listed in Wikipedia's first-generation section, so check your truck's data plate. German Wikipedia puts the permitted gross weight at 3,850 kg for the 716 and 5,000 kg for the 718.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA
Which wheels fit?

The TD uses 5×130 mm with an ET 73 offset on the stock 16-inch steel wheels, and military ball-seat bolts. Steel G-Wagen wheels (ET 63) only clear the calipers with half-worn pads. Owners have fitted SsangYong Rexton alloys (5×130, ET 43, about 2.4 in wider overall) and 2002–06 Sprinter T1N steel wheels with conical-seat bolts.

SOURCE · REAL 4X4S FORUM
Can I put more power through it?

Carefully. Owners see the transfer case as the weakest link, and one remembers the original driveline being rated for about 140 hp. Popular swaps are a 2.5 TDI five-cylinder (from a VW, or from a 1999 Volvo V70 in one owner's 716) and, in the UK, a BMW diesel on the ZF auto. A specialist warns that an outer axle assembly is expensive and slow to source if you break one.

SOURCE · REAL 4X4S FORUM
Are parts available?

For the engine, mostly yes: it's the VW/Volvo 2.4 turbo-diesel six, and owners buy filters at NAPA and gaskets as Volvo 740 parts. Bearings and seals are standard sizes from a bearing supplier. Pinzgauer-specific parts are scarcer and pricier; Expedition Imports in the US stocks TD parts.

SOURCE · REAL 4X4S FORUM
Can I register one in the US?

It depends on the truck and the state. Austrian-built 716s and 718s are now old enough for the 25-year import exemption, but a veteran owner points out that registration is still up to each state. His 716 was registered in California in error, and the DMV grandfathered it when it noticed. In 2022 a Colorado DMV flagged a Pinzgauer VIN as military and refused it for road use.

SOURCE · REAL 4X4S FORUM

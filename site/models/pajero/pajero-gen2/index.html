PART 01

Overview

Where it came from, why it matters, and how it changed year to year.

The story

Mitsubishi launched the second Pajero in January 1991, and its designers admit their toughest rival was the first one. Work started in the autumn of 1986, when the original was still selling strongly. Early scale models scored worse in surveys than the truck they were meant to replace. The finished design was longer, wider and 50 mm lower overall, but the driver's eye point dropped only 30 mm, because rally driver Kenjiro Shinozuka insisted that visibility is vital. It worked: Mitsubishi's designers say it sold 1.15 million in eight years and seven months, and at one point it was Japan's best-selling new car of the month.

SUPER SELECT: FOUR MODES, ONE LEVER

The first Pajero's part-time 4WD bound up on tight paved corners. To engage it you had to stop, lock the freewheeling hubs and roll back about a metre. The second generation fixed that with Super Select 4WD, which Mitsubishi calls a world first and which North America knew as Active-Trac. One gated lever offers 2H, 4H with an open centre differential for full-time use on any surface, 4HLc with the centre locked, and 4LLc in low range. You can shift between 2H and 4H on the move; Wikipedia and JDMBUYSELL give the limit as 100 km/h. Instead of hubs, a dog clutch on the front differential disconnects the right-side front axle in 2H and 4H. Bring a Trailer's listings note a viscous coupling in US Active-Trac transfer cases.

The rest of the spec sheet was just as ambitious. A pneumatic rear locker was a factory option and engages only in 4HLc or 4LLc. Multi-mode ABS works in every Super Select mode. Optional electronic dampers have Soft, Medium and Hard settings. Body styles were metal-top and canvas-top short wheelbases and semi-high and high-roof long-wheelbase wagons. Its rivals were the Land Cruiser 80, the Patrol Y60 and the Isuzu Trooper, and Super Select was the Pajero's trump card.

THE ENGINES: TWO V6S, TWO DIESELS

At launch you could have the 6G72 3.0-litre SOHC 12-valve V6 or the 4D56 2.5-litre intercooled turbo-diesel. The diesel's top-mounted intercooler is fed through two hidden scoops at the top of the grille and ducts in the bonnet. In July 1993 Mitsubishi added the 6G74 3.5-litre DOHC 24-valve V6, rated at 215 hp and 228 lb-ft in the US Montero SR, and the 4M40 2.8-litre intercooled turbo-diesel, along with a larger transmission and transfer case. Wikipedia lists 92 kW and 294 Nm for the 4M40 in this Pajero. In 1996 the 3.0 got a 24-valve head and coil-pack ignition; BaT lists a 1995 Montero LS at 177 hp and 188 lb-ft.

Two details matter for owners. The V6s and the 4D56 use timing belts, and the 4M40 uses a chain. And the 3.5 changed: Australian owners say the NJ and NK used the DOHC 3.5 and the 1997-on NL switched to a SOHC 3.5 with less peak power but a broader spread of torque. BaT's 18k-mile 1998 Montero is rated at 200 hp. The US Monteros we found on Bring a Trailer are all V6s; the diesel Pajeros in the US today are Japanese-market imports.

THE EVOLUTION

For 1997 the Dakar banned manufacturers from the prototype class and outlawed petrol turbos, so Mitsubishi built a T2 car from the short-wheelbase second-generation Pajero, kept the production 6G74 V6 and stiffened the chassis. Shinozuka won and became the first Japanese driver to win the Dakar overall, and Pajeros filled the top four places. For 1998, with new FIA rules, Mitsubishi needed a road car to base the next T2 car on. That road car was the Pajero Evolution (V55W): wider tracks, double-wishbone independent rear suspension, overfenders, an aluminium bonnet with a scoop, Recaro seats and a 3.5-litre 6G74 with MIVEC variable valve timing and a dual-plenum variable intake.

Japanese catalogues rate the road car at 280 PS. About 2,693 were built, and Bring a Trailer sellers put the number with a five-speed manual at around 600. Jean-Pierre Fontenay won the 1998 Dakar in the competition version, again with Pajeros in the first four places, and Mitsubishi says its cars won 15 of the 16 special stages. That independent rear end became the basis for the third-generation Pajero.

HOW IT ENDED

The monocoque third generation arrived in Japan in 1999 and reached the US for the 2001 model year, so the last second-generation Monteros are 2000 models. Production carried on elsewhere: as the Pajero SFX in India until 2012, in the Philippines until 2008, and in China, where Changfeng's Leopaard models were built from 1995 to 2021. In 1998 export and Gulf trucks got the 'blister' wide-fender facelift.

Late trucks have one modern problem. NL Pajeros built from 31 May 1996 to 30 September 2000 with the NADI 5-AT driver's airbag inflator are subject to a buy-back in Australia, because no replacement inflator exists. In the US, the same issue is NHTSA recall 20V035000 for 1998–2000 Monteros. Check the VIN before you buy.

SIDEBAR · DAKAR 1992–1998

Prototypes first, then production trucks

The second-generation era began with Mitsubishi prototypes. Hubert Auriol won the 1992 Paris–Cape Town in a works Pajero, ahead of two more Pajeros. Bruno Saby won in 1993 in a prototype developed by SBM, with a 4G6-family turbo four and a six-speed gearbox. Neither car had much in common with a showroom Pajero.

That changed in 1997. The T2 cars that finished first to fourth used the production chassis, the production 6G74 V6 and a rear rigid axle, because the rules didn't allow Mitsubishi to change the rear suspension design. In 1998 the Evolution let Mitsubishi fit independent rear suspension. If you want a Pajero with Dakar DNA, this generation's T2 trucks are the closest link between the rally cars and the ones in showrooms.

PART 02

Specs & capability

How it’s built, what it can do, and what fits under it.

How it’s built

The second Pajero keeps the first one's recipe: a ladder frame, double-wishbone front suspension on torsion bars and a live rear axle on coil springs. The steering is integral ball-nut (recirculating ball); rack and pinion didn't arrive until the third generation. Super Select 4WD replaced part-time 4WD and freewheel hubs, and a pneumatic rear locker was optional. US Monteros were V6s; elsewhere you could have the 4D56 or 4M40 turbo-diesel.

PART 03

Buying one

What to pay, what to check, and how to tell a good one from a clone.

What to check before you buy

  1. 01
    Ask for the timing-belt receiptOn the V6s and the 4D56 the belt is a scheduled part, and owners say the 3.5 doesn't survive a snapped belt. Australian owners use about 100,000 km. With no proof, price in a belt, tensioner, idlers and water pump. The 4M40 uses a chain.
  2. 02
    Look hard at the V6 cooling systemCheck for milky oil, coolant loss and a temperature gauge that wanders. ExplorOz mechanics describe the V6 Pajeros as prone to head gaskets and say the heads crack. A combustion-leak test before purchase costs very little.
  3. 03
    Blue smoke on the overrunWorn valve stem seals are a known V6 complaint. One Australian owner says they 'normally need replacing at 200,000 km', and Montana Montero has a video on why Monteros burn oil. Watch the exhaust after a long idle and on the first blip of the throttle.
  4. 04
    Exercise the Super Select leverOn loose ground, go through 2H, 4H, 4HLc and 4LLc. The lights should stop flashing once each mode engages. A light that keeps flashing, or a front axle that won't engage, points to the vacuum system. Owners say the system misbehaves on trucks that rarely use 4WD.
  5. 05
    Check the steering linkageMitsubishi's pitman-arm notice covers every second-generation code, the Evolution included. Feel for play at the pitman and idler arms and inspect every boot. A cracked boot means the joint needs replacing.
  6. 06
    Measure the front ride heightTired torsion bars leave the nose low, and a bull bar makes it worse. Uneven ride height side to side or a nose-down stance means the bars need adjusting or replacing.
  7. 07
    Rust in the sills and chassisSteven Taylor's Mk2 Pajero video shows sill replacement and chassis welding on a 2.8. BaT's 1998 Montero needed its underside treated. Probe the sills, the rear chassis rails and the spring mounts.
  8. 08
    Run the VIN for recallsUS recalls include the 1998–2000 NADI driver airbag (20V035000), cracking front brake hoses on 1992–94 trucks (96V143), chafing brake lines on 1997–98 trucks (98V220) and a loose crank-pulley bolt on 1992–96 3.0s (00V311001). In Australia, NL airbag trucks are being bought back.

For the hands-on owner

Common repairs

The jobs owners actually end up doing, rated for a competent home mechanic with a decent tool kit. Always confirm torque figures, fluids and procedures against the factory service manual for your VIN, and work safely — support the truck on stands, never just a jack. You do this work at your own risk; see the disclaimer. Some parts links are affiliate links.

Timing belt, tensioner and water pump (6G72 3.0 / 6G74 3.5)
DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · ≈ A long daySHOP COST · Australian owners quoted about A$650–1,000 in 2006, the top figure including a water pump
SYMPTOMSUsually none before it fails. A whirring from the belt area can mean an over-tight belt or a failing idler. Australian owners change it about every 100,000 km and warn that the 3.5 has no valve clearance if the belt breaks, while the 3.0 does. Treat those as owner reports and check your engine.
THE FIXSet the engine at TDC and remove the accessory belts, fan and crank pulley. Replace the belt, idler and tensioner pulleys, and the water pump while you're there. Owners describe a spring-loaded tensioner on the 3.0 they worked on and a hydraulic one on the 3.5. On the 3.5, they compress the tensioner ram slowly in a vice and pin it, then preload the offset tensioner pulley before pulling the pin. One owner sets the preload with a torque wrench at 25 in-lb (SOHC) or 50 in-lb (DOHC); confirm the figures in the factory manual. Another rotates the crank two turns, waits a few minutes and checks that the pin still slides freely. Check the hydraulic tensioner for leaks and fit a new genuine crank-pulley (balancer) bolt, because owners report reused bolts breaking. A loose crank-pulley bolt is also the subject of NHTSA recall 00V311001 on 1992–96 3.0 Monteros. Montana Montero has a video walk-through of the job on a Montero.

Cure a flashing 4WD light (front axle engagement)
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · ≈ 1–3 hours to diagnose and fixSHOP COST · Usually a vacuum solenoid or hose; parts plus your time
SYMPTOMSThe Super Select lights keep flashing after you select 4H, or the front axle never engages. Owners say the system 'sometimes plays up if not used very much'.
THE FIXIn 2H and 4H a dog clutch on the front differential disconnects one front axle shaft, and Super Select uses it to engage the front axle. A light that keeps flashing means the front axle hasn't engaged. Start with the cheap parts: inspect every vacuum hose between the engine, the solenoid valves and the front-diff actuator for cracks or loose ends, then test each solenoid. The Montero Ventures video shows a flashing-light fault cured by replacing a vacuum solenoid valve. Afterwards, cycle through 2H, 4H, 4HLc and 4LLc on loose ground, and use 4WD regularly so the system doesn't seize from disuse.

Inspect the pitman-arm ball joint (Mitsubishi's procedure)
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · ≈ 1–2 hoursSHOP COST · Free to inspect; a pitman arm or boots if worn
SYMPTOMSSteering play, a clunk through the wheel, or a split ball-joint boot. Mitsubishi Japan warns that on old, hard-used trucks with integral ball-nut steering, corrosion and wear can break the pitman-arm ball joint and cause loss of steering. Every second-generation chassis code, the V55W Evolution included, is on its list.
THE FIXFollowing Mitsubishi's notice: lift the truck and rock every steering rod and arm by hand, looking for play, loose mounts, bends or missing split pins. Check every dust boot. A cracked boot may have let in muddy water, so replace that ball joint complete. To check the pitman joint, loosen the relay-rod nuts at the pitman and idler arms but leave them on, break the tapers, then remove the nuts and the relay rod (idler end first). Push the pitman-arm boot up, wipe off the grease and feel the stud where the boot lip runs. If you can feel a dent or a catch, replace the pitman arm. If it's smooth, grease the boot lip with molybdenum-disulfide chassis grease, refit the relay rod, torque the nuts to 44 N·m and pump grease in through the nipple. Mitsubishi recommends new boots every five years on trucks that go off-road often.

Reset or upgrade the front torsion bars
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · ≈ 1 hour to adjust; half a day to replace the barsSHOP COST · Free to adjust; uprated bars and rear coils as a kit
SYMPTOMSThe nose sits low. One ExplorOz contributor says many high-mileage Pajeros 'almost look like they're sitting on the front bump stops.' A heavy steel bull bar makes it worse.
THE FIXIf the bars aren't badly sagged, wind the adjusters up evenly to restore ride height. Measure both sides and get an alignment. Be aware that winding them up adds preload and puts more twist on tired bars. If the bars have sagged a lot, or you run a bar and winch, fit uprated torsion bars and match them with stiffer rear coils so the front and rear balance. One Australian owner reported 65 mm of lift and a firm ride from a TJM kit.

V6 head gasket replacement (and do it properly)
DIFFICULTY · 4 / 5TIME · ≈ 2–3 daysSHOP COST · Parts plus machine-shop crack test and skim
SYMPTOMSCoolant loss, milky oil on the dipstick or under the filler cap, overheating. One NJ 3.0 owner on ExplorOz had replaced the head gasket twice in about three years. Mechanics in that thread say the V6s have a weak spot for head gaskets and that the heads 'do crack a fair bit'.

Asked on every forum

Pajero / Montero (Second Generation) questions, answered

What's the biggest tire I can fit without a lift?

We found no single forum consensus, but the evidence points to about 31 inches. An Australian owner lists the 1996 NL GLX's stock tyres as 235/75R15 on 15x6 steel wheels and calls 31x10.5R15 'a good tyre size'. BaT's 1994 Montero SR runs 31x10.50R15s on its factory 15-inch alloys, and a 1995 Pajero sold on 265/70R16s. Go bigger and you'll want a lift, and probably some trimming.

SOURCE · EXPLOROZ · BAT
Can I drive in 4WD on the highway?

Yes, in 4H. That's the point of Super Select: 4H is full-time 4WD with an open centre differential, so it doesn't bind on pavement. 4HLc and 4LLc lock the centre and are for loose surfaces only. You can move between 2H and 4H on the move.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · MITSUBISHI
Does it have a locker?

A pneumatic rear locker was a factory option. It works only with the centre differential locked, in 4HLc or 4LLc. BaT's 1994 Montero SR and 1998 Montero both have locking rear differentials. Check the dash switch and the air lines before you assume a truck has one.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · BAT
Which engine should I buy?

The 3.0 V6 is the common US engine. Australian owners say it has valve clearance if the belt breaks, but it's down on power. The 3.5 is the strong one: 215 hp in the DOHC US SR, 200 hp in the later SOHC Montero. Outside the US, the 4M40 2.8 turbo-diesel has a timing chain and a good reputation, but some owners report head problems. The 4D56 2.5 is slower and belt-driven.

SOURCE · BAT · EXPLOROZ
Is the 3.5 an interference engine?

Australian owners on ExplorOz say the 3.5 has no clearance if the belt breaks and the 3.0 does. Treat that as owner experience and confirm with the factory manual or a specialist. Either way, the belt is a scheduled part, so change it on time.

SOURCE · EXPLOROZ
What fuel economy should I expect?

Owners report about 13–14 L/100 km around town and about 11 on the highway from a 3.5 petrol, and 14 highway and 16 town from an NL GLS automatic. Towing pushes that to about 20. A 3.0 owner averaged 11.3 L/100 km. One owner towing almost constantly with a DOHC 3.5 saw 25–30 L/100 km. Diesels are 10–20% better, according to an owner who runs one.

SOURCE · EXPLOROZ
Pajero, Montero or Shogun: what's the difference?

Just the name. Montero was used in North America, Spain and most of Latin America, because 'pajero' is a crude insult in Spanish. Shogun was the UK name. The name Pajero comes from the Pampas cat, Leopardus pajeros.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA
Can I import a Japanese-market Pajero or Evolution into the US?

Yes, if it's at least 25 years old, counted from its own build date. Every second-generation truck now qualifies. The EPA's exemption is separate: it needs the vehicle in original configuration (or with an equivalent EPA-certified engine) and at least 21 years old. State rules vary, and California is the hard one.

SOURCE · 49 CFR 591.5 · JDMBUYSELL
What's a Pajero Evolution worth?

On Bring a Trailer, sold Evolutions from late 2024 to October 2026 ran from $19,500 to $49,997, with a median of about $32,000. Low-kilometre trucks and the rare five-speed manuals sit at the top.

SOURCE · BAT
What are the lift options?

Uprated torsion bars at the front and coils at the rear. Old Man Emu torsion bars for 1991-on Mitsubishis are sold in the US, and Dobinsons sells a 45 mm kit for the NH–NL Pajero. One Australian owner reported 65 mm of lift from a TJM kit. Owners say to match front and rear spring rates, and you'll need an alignment.

SOURCE · HURREYS · SUPERSPARES · EXPLOROZ
Are there safety recalls I should know about?

The big one is the NADI driver airbag: NHTSA 20V035000 for 1998–2000 Monteros, and a buy-back program for affected NL Pajeros in Australia, because no replacement inflator exists. Earlier trucks have brake-hose recalls (96V143, 98V220) and a 3.0 crank-pulley bolt recall (00V311001).

SOURCE · NHTSA · UNSEALED 4X4

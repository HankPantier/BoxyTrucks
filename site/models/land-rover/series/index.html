PART 01

Overview

Where it came from, why it matters, and how it changed year to year.

The story

The Series Land Rover is the original: a boxed steel ladder frame, two beam axles on leaf springs, a four-speed gearbox and flat aluminium panels you can unbolt with hand tools. It was meant to be a stopgap for a car company with an empty factory, and it outlived nearly everything else that company built. Today a good one is a collector's car with a Hagerty valuation, but underneath it is still a farm implement, and the owners who get the most out of them treat it exactly like one.

A JEEP, A FARM AND A STEEL SHORTAGE

After the war Rover had a bombed Coventry factory, a shortage of steel and no affordable car to build. Chief designer Maurice Wilks, who used an army-surplus Jeep on his farm on Anglesey, proposed a light agricultural and utility vehicle with a power take-off. The first prototype used a Jeep chassis, a Rover P3 engine and gearbox, Birmabright alloy bodywork and a steering wheel in the middle, which earned it the nickname 'centre steer'. Early trucks were painted light green because surplus aircraft cockpit paint was available.

The production truck dropped the centre steering and every Jeep part, gained its own transfer box, and was launched at the Amsterdam Motor Show in 1948 on an 80-inch wheelbase with a 1.6-litre four of about 50 bhp. The freewheel four-wheel-drive system was replaced during 1950–51 by a simpler dog-clutch arrangement. A Tickford-bodied, wood-framed Station Wagon arrived in 1949 and flopped, with fewer than 700 sold. Land Rover learned the lesson fast: wheelbases grew to 86 and 107 inches in 1954, then 88 and 109 in 1956, and a 2.0-litre diesel followed in 1957.

SERIES II AND IIA: THE SHAPE EVERYONE DRAWS

The 1958 Series II brought David Bache's wider 'barrel side' body and the 2.25-litre petrol four, though the first 1,500 or so short-wheelbase trucks kept the 2.0. The 109 Station Wagon could be had as a 12-seater, which in Britain counted as a bus and dodged purchase tax. The IIA of 1961 added a 2.25 diesel, and from 1967 long-wheelbase models could have a 2.6-litre six with servo brakes. Just 811 of those were NADA (North American Dollar Area) trucks, the only long-wheelbase models built for the US and Canada.

By February 1968 total production was just under 600,000, with more than 70% exported, and sales peaked in 1969–70 at over 60,000 a year. From February 1969 home-market trucks moved their headlamps out of the grille panel and into the wings, which is the quickest way to tell a late IIA from an early one. Hagerty notes that because so few were sold in the US, a IIA feels more exotic here than a comparable Jeep or Land Cruiser, and the market has priced that in.

SERIES III AND THE LONG GOODBYE

The 1971 Series III was a modernisation by Land Rover standards: synchromesh on all four forward gears, a plastic grille, a moulded plastic dashboard with the instruments moved in front of the driver, and the 2.25's compression raised from 7:1 to 8:1. Long-wheelbase trucks got the one-piece Salisbury rear axle as standard. The millionth Land Rover rolled off the line in 1976, but North American factory sales had already ended in 1974, so most Series IIIs in the US arrived later through private import.

Land Rover kept fixing the known weak spots. In 1980 the 2.25 petrol and diesel engines got five-main-bearing crankshafts and the axles and hubs were strengthened, and in 1982 the driveshaft spline count went from 10 to 24 to deal with rear half-shafts that snapped under overloaded commercial use. A County station wagon trim arrived in April 1982, and a Stage 1 V8 (the Rover 3.5 detuned to 91 hp, with permanent four-wheel drive) ran from 1979 to 1985. Roughly 440,000 Series IIIs were built before the coil-sprung One Ten and Ninety took over.

WHY THEY STILL MATTER

A Series truck is about as mechanically transparent as a motor vehicle gets. There is no ECU, the body panels unbolt, the rear half-shafts slide out of fully floating hubs without even jacking the truck up, and the parts catalogue from Vermont to Solihull is still enormous. The same simplicity explains its faults: leaf springs ride hard, the gearbox wears out first and second, the steel chassis and bulkhead rot where mud sits, and a 2.25 petrol is happiest at 50–55 mph.

None of that has hurt values. In 2019 Hagerty reported a median 'Excellent' value of $39,000 for Series II/IIA trucks, with petrol engines worth more than diesels and soft-tops slightly ahead of wagons. Every boxy 4x4 on this site, from the Range Rover Classic to the Ineos Grenadier, is in some way an argument with this vehicle about how much comfort a working truck is allowed to have.

SIDEBAR · THE ODD ONES

Lightweights, Forward Controls and Pink Panthers

The military took Series trucks in directions civilians never saw. The Lightweight was an air-portable military 88, and the Dutch and Danish versions ran diesel engines. The 109-based 'Pink Panther' Long-Range Desert Patrol Vehicles were converted by Marshall's of Cambridge for the SAS, with doors and windscreens removed, weapon mounts added and extra fuel and water tanks fitted.

Civilian oddballs exist too. The Series IIA Forward Control of 1962 put the cab over a 2.25 petrol engine, and the later 101 Forward Control used the 3.5 V8. The One Ton 109, built from late 1968 to 1977, combined a standard 109 body with a 2.6 six, a lower-ratio transfer box and 900x16 tyres. Only 170 IIA and 238 Series III One Tons were built for the home market.

Specs & capability

How it’s built, what it can do, and what fits under it.

How it’s built

Every Series truck uses the same basic recipe: a fully boxed, welded steel ladder frame, beam axles on semi-elliptic leaf springs, a four-speed gearbox with a two-speed transfer box, and selectable four-wheel drive. The body is aluminium alloy panels bolted to the frame and a steel bulkhead, a choice made in 1948 because steel was rationed and kept even after aluminium became more expensive. The result is light, rust-resistant on top and rust-prone underneath, and almost entirely serviceable with hand tools.

What to check before you buy

  1. 01
    Chassis rear crossmember and outriggersThese are repaired or replaced more than anything else on a Series frame. Feel along the top of the box sections and above the bump stops, where off-road loads concentrate. Heavy rot can mean a replacement chassis, which series123's guide puts at £1,000-plus in the UK, so make sure the price reflects it.
  2. 02
    Bulkhead and door hingesCheck the lower bulkhead, the footwell corners and around the door hinges, plus anywhere the aluminium body touches steel: body-to-chassis mounts and where the wings bolt to the bulkhead. Serious bulkhead rot usually means the body has to come off.
  3. 03
    Gearbox jumping outFirst and second wear and can jump out of gear. Press and release the throttle in each gear on the test drive, try every ratio including low range, and listen for noises. The series123 guide rates the Series III gearbox as weaker than the Series II's.
  4. 04
    Mismatched differentialsOwners sometimes fit a taller rear differential for economy. If front and rear ratios don't match, the truck will fight itself in four-wheel drive. Drive a short distance on soft ground to confirm 4WD engages and behaves.
  5. 05
    Half-shafts and drive flangesA clunk from the rear when you come to a stop (the 'Salisbury clunk' on later trucks) usually means worn splines in the drive flange. Rovers North forum members report flanges strip out occasionally and recommend carrying a spare. Check for play in the propshaft UJs too.
  6. 06
    Springs and mountsLeaves should be uniform and the truck should sit level. Look for cracked or welded spring hangers; rust at the spring mounts is an MOT failure in the UK.
  7. 07
    Engine healthLow knocking points to worn crank bearings; a lighter, rapid tapping may just be tappets needing adjustment. Occasional blue smoke can be valve-stem seals, continuous blue smoke means rings and bores, and black smoke points at the carburettor.
  8. 08
    Wheels and tyresOriginal Series rims are tube-type. Tubeless radials need wheels with safety humps (later Defender-style wheels), and you shouldn't run tubes in tubeless wheels. Check what's fitted before you assume the tyres are a simple swap.

Spotting the real thing

Series trucks were built for 37 years, in several countries, and plenty have been rebuilt from mixed parts. The chassis number tells you what you're actually buying.

  1. ID
    Chassis number (pre-1979)Stamped on an aluminium plate, on the bulkhead inside the cab on earlier trucks and under the bonnet on later ones. The first three digits give model, body and engine: 241–245 is a IIA 88 petrol, 251–255 a IIA 109 petrol, 901–905 a Series III 88 petrol, 911–915 a Series III 109 petrol.
  2. ID
    17-character VIN (late 1979 on)Land Rover switched to 17-character VINs at the end of 1979. Glencoyne's examples: LBCAH… is a Series III 109 petrol, LBAAG… a Series III 88 diesel.
  3. ID
    Which Series is it?Series I (1948–58) has flat sides. Series II (1958–61) has the wider barrel-side body. IIA (1961–71) looks almost the same, and late home-market IIAs have headlamps in the wings. Series III (1971–85) has a plastic grille and a plastic dash with instruments in front of the driver.
  4. ID
    NADA trucks and licence-buildsOnly 811 long-wheelbase 2.6 six IIAs were NADA (North American Dollar Area) trucks. Bring a Trailer also sells many Spanish-built Santana and Colombian 'Ensambladora Automotriz' 88s and 109s, so confirm the maker.
  5. ID
    Heritage certificateThe British Motor Museum's Heritage Certificates cover Land Rovers from 1948 on and copy the factory ledger: build and despatch dates, engine number, colour and equipment.

Importing & legality

Factory sales to North America ended in 1974, so most Series trucks in the US today are private imports. Every Series truck is now well past NHTSA's 25-year line, so FMVSS isn't a barrier. On EPA Form 3520-1, code E exempts vehicles at least 21 years old in original configuration; a truck with a non-original engine can't use that exemption unless the replacement is the same engine model.

California is the catch. Gasoline vehicles of the 1975 model year and older are smog-exempt, but a non-California-certified direct import from 1975 on is supposed to pass a CARB laboratory test for a Certificate of Conformance. Rovers North forum members trying to register a 1978 Series III were told by ARB that the truck would need modifying and testing to 1978 California standards. Pre-1976 petrol trucks are the easy route there.

Rules change and vary by state and country. Confirm with your DMV and a licensed importer before you buy.

Asked on every forum

Land Rover Series I–III questions, answered

What's the biggest tire I can fit without a lift?

235/85R16 is the size most owners recommend: a Rovers North forum member calls it close to, but a little wider than, the factory-optional 7.50x16, and it should fit a stock 88 or 109 once the steering stops are adjusted so the tyre clears the frame at full lock (expect a slightly bigger turning circle). 215/85R16 is also close to 7.50x16. 265/75R16 on stock 5.5-inch rims rubbed the frame by about half to three-quarters of an inch for one owner, and another found 265s too wide on a 109. Original rims are tube-type, so check the wheels before fitting tubeless radials.

SOURCE · ROVERS NORTH FORUMS
What's the difference between a Series I, II, IIA and III?

Series I (1948–58) has flat sides and 80, 86/88 or 107/109-inch wheelbases. Series II (1958–61) brought the wider 'barrel side' body and the 2.25 petrol. Series IIA (1961–71) looks almost identical but added the 2.25 diesel and the 2.6 six, and late home-market IIAs have headlamps in the wings. Series III (1971–85) has a plastic grille, a plastic dash with instruments in front of the driver and an all-synchro gearbox.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA / HAGERTY
Were Series Land Rovers sold new in the US?

Yes, but not many. Factory sales to North America ended in 1974. Only 811 of the long-wheelbase IIAs with the 2.6 six were NADA (North American Dollar Area) trucks, the only long-wheelbase models built for the US and Canada.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA
What fuel economy should I expect?

Owners on AULRO report roughly 14–17 mpg (imperial) for a 2.25 petrol in normal use, with a best of about 21 mpg at a steady 50 mph in a short-wheelbase truck. Loaded, hilly driving pulls it back toward 14. In US gallons, knock about a sixth off those figures.

SOURCE · AULRO
How fast will it cruise?

Owners on the Rovers North forum put the comfortable cruise for a 2.25 petrol at 55–60 mph. With a Fairey overdrive, 60–65 on the flat is realistic. One owner advises 3,000–3,400 rpm for the four-cylinder petrol and warns that sitting at 4,000-plus wears engines out faster.

SOURCE · ROVERS NORTH FORUMS
Why do Series rear half-shafts break?

Partly the design of the shafts and partly overloading in commercial use. Land Rover strengthened the axles and hubs in 1980 and increased the driveshaft spline count from 10 to 24 in 1982. Forum members say shafts usually break at the differential end, and worn drive flanges announce themselves with a clunk well before they strip.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA / ROVERS NORTH
Should I fit free-wheeling front hubs?

Rovers North advises against it on Series IIA/III swivels with the Railko bush: running the hubs unlocked stops the front driveline turning, which it says starves the swivel ball, Railko bush and front propshaft of lubrication. If you fit them anyway, lock them in regularly.

SOURCE · ROVERS NORTH
Petrol or diesel? Soft top or wagon?

On value, Hagerty found in 2019 that Series II/IIA petrol trucks were worth about 6% more than diesels and soft-tops about 2% more than wagons. In use, the 2.25 petrol is the more pleasant road engine; the early 2.0 and 2.25 diesels are slow even by Series standards.

SOURCE · HAGERTY
What are the rust spots?

The aluminium body doesn't rust but corrodes where it touches steel. The steel parts do rust: rear crossmember, outriggers, chassis box sections above the bump stops, the lower bulkhead and footwells, door hinge areas and door bottoms.

SOURCE · SERIES123
Can I put tubeless tyres on the original wheels?

Not properly. Original Series rims are tube-type. Tubeless radials need wheels with safety humps, which later Defender wheels have. Don't fit inner tubes in tubeless wheels.

SOURCE · ROVERS NORTH FORUMS
How many were built?

Just under 600,000 by February 1968, more than 70% of them exported. The 1,000,000th Land Rover was built in 1976, and about 440,000 Series IIIs were made between 1971 and 1985.

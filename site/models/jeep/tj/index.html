PART 01

Overview

Where it came from, why it matters, and how it changed year to year.

The story

On January 2, 1996, Chrysler showed the new Wrangler at the Detroit auto show, and the square headlights were gone. The TJ brought back round lamps and, more importantly, threw out the leaf springs that Jeeps had used since the war. It used coil springs at all four corners on solid axles, with control arms largely borrowed from the Grand Cherokee. It kept the YJ's 93.4-inch wheelbase and its 4.0 straight-six, reached showrooms in April 1996 as a 1997 model, and became the Wrangler many owners still consider the best of the lot.

A $260 MILLION REINVENTION

Work on a YJ successor began in 1990 in Chrysler's Jeep-Truck Engineering pre-program group under Bob Sheaves and program director Craig Winn. YJ-based mules ran from 1990 to 1993. Michael Santoro's design was chosen in late 1992 and frozen in May 1993, and the program was formally approved in 1993 with a $260 million budget. Drivingline calls that a 'somewhat shoestring budget' and notes that the TJ was the first Wrangler developed with Chrysler's full resources rather than AMC's.

The frame was stiffened, and Drivingline says the new body panels doubled torsional rigidity, making the TJ roughly a third stiffer overall than the YJ. The coil springs and four-link control arms gave much better articulation and a far better ride. The body looked familiar on purpose, but small changes cut drag and noise.

SIXES, FOURS AND A LOT OF GEARBOXES

The 4.0-liter six was the engine to have. Drivingline lists 181 hp and 222 lb-ft for 1997–99 and 190 hp and 235 lb-ft from 2000. Entry trucks used the 2.5-liter AMC four until 2002, then the 2.4-liter PowerTech four with 147 hp. Automatics were the three-speed 30RH and 32RH until 2002, then the four-speed 42RLE. Wikipedia lists the AX-5 manual with the four, the AX-15 with the six through 1999, the NV3550 for 2000–04, the NV1500 with the 2.4 for 2003–04 and the six-speed NSG370 for 2005–06.

The front axle was a low-pinion Dana 30. The rear was a Dana 35 on most trucks, with a Dana 44 optional on the Sport and Sahara. Drivingline calls the Dana 35 'junk' because of its C-clip design. The NP231 Command-Trac transfer case handled part-time four-wheel drive on everything but the Rubicon.

THE RUBICON

In summer 2002 Jeep launched the 2003 Wrangler Rubicon, and it changed what buyers expected from a showroom Jeep. It had Dana 44 axles front and rear with 4.10 gears and air-actuated lockers built by Tochigi Fuji Sangyo. Jeep's launch release said the driver could lock them from a dash switch in low range below 10 mph, with the pneumatic pump running at just 5 psi. It also had the NV241OR Rock-Trac transfer case with a 4.0:1 low range, 31-inch Goodyear MT/R tires and diamond-plate sill guards. RockCrawler reported a crawl ratio of 65.92. Jeep's Craig Love said it combined 'what would typically be aftermarket hardware'. Drivingline calls it the most capable factory TJ you can buy. The long-wheelbase Unlimited (LJ) arrived for 2004, and a Rubicon version followed for 2005.

HOW IT ENDED

Production ran from January 1996 to July 2006, and 2006 was the final model year for both the TJ and the Unlimited. Special editions along the way included the 60th Anniversary (2001, 4,067 built), the Tomb Raider Rubicon (2003), the moss-green Willys (2004–05), the Columbia (2004), and the Golden Eagle and 65th Anniversary in 2006. The JK was unveiled at the Detroit show in January 2006 and went on sale as a 2007 model. It was bigger, wider and available with four doors as the JK Unlimited.

That growth is a big part of the TJ's appeal today. It is still small enough for tight trails, its 4.0 is nearly unkillable when maintained, and the aftermarket for it is enormous. Its enemies are rust, a handful of well-known leaks and, on 2005–06 trucks, an oil pump drive that owners learn to fear.

SIDEBAR · THE LAUGHING MONKEY

Why 2005–06 TJ owners listen to their engines

The 4.0 drives its oil pump through a shaft from the camshaft, called the oil pump drive assembly or OPDA, which also carries the cam position sensor. Quadratec's inspection checklist flags 2005–06 4.0 TJs for OPDA failure and says owners describe the warning noise as a 'laughing monkey'.

Sources describe the flaw slightly differently. Dorman's replacement listing says the factory upper bushing (steel, by its account) is starved of oil, so the center shaft can seize and destroy the cam bearing. Crown's listing at Quadratec describes two bronze bushings with a seal that cuts the upper one off from oil. Quadratec's advice is blunt: without records of a replacement or update, deal with it before it damages the engine.

Specs & capability

How it’s built, what it can do, and what fits under it.

How it’s built

The TJ is a ladder-frame Jeep with solid axles at both ends, located by coil springs, control arms, track bars and anti-roll bars. The standard wheelbase stayed at 93.4 inches. Most trucks pair a Dana 30 front axle with a Dana 35 or optional Dana 44 rear and the NP231 part-time transfer case. The 2003–06 Rubicon gets Dana 44s at both ends with lockers and the 4:1 NV241OR.

What to check before you buy

  1. 01
    Frame rust, especially the rear control-arm mountsQuadratec says plainly that TJ frames still rust. Check the rails inside and out, the skid-plate areas and the rear lower control-arm brackets, where it warns rust can become severe enough to cause structural failure. Rick Rotondo adds the transfer-case crossmember and central body mounts to the list.
  2. 02
    Floors and the early cowl leakLook under the carpet, especially in the front footwells. Off Road Xtreme notes that the cowl vent on early TJs leaks water into the cab, rusting the floor and growing mold in the carpet. Jeep revised the design later.
  3. 03
    Ask about the OPDA on 2005–06 4.0sListen for the 'laughing monkey' noise at idle. With no record of a replacement, Quadratec recommends replacing the oil pump drive assembly proactively, because a failure can damage the engine.
  4. 04
    Drive it at 30–50 mphOff Road Xtreme says that's the speed range to check for death wobble, usually caused by a loose lower track-bar bolt. Check the track bar, ball joints, tie-rod ends, stabilizer and control-arm bushings for wear, and look for bent steering linkage.
  5. 05
    Oil leaks are normal; puddles aren'tThe 4.0 commonly leaks from the rear main seal, oil pan gasket, valve cover gasket and oil-filter adapter. Drivingline adds pinion and transfer-case output seals. Price in the rear main seal if the bellhousing is wet.
  6. 06
    Radiator end tanksThe factory radiator has plastic end tanks crimped to an aluminum core, and Quadratec says many start leaking at the upper seams around 100,000 miles. Look for green crust, coolant smell and small leaks. Off Road Xtreme recommends an all-aluminum radiator, partly because the 4.0 runs warm.
  7. 07
    Which rear axle and which gearbox?Find the axle tag: a Dana 44 rear beats the Dana 35 if you plan bigger tires. Drivingline says the AX-15 has known synchro issues, the NV3550 is stronger, and the 32RH automatic is durable but revs high on the highway.
  8. 08
    Transfer case won't go into 4-Low?Quadratec says to check the shift linkage first, because it pops out of worn plastic bushings. It's a cheap fix, but it's a good haggling point.

Spotting the real thing

Rubicon badges, decals and axles are easy to bolt onto a Sport, so check the VIN and the hardware before paying a Rubicon price.

  1. ID
    VIN trim codeAll TJ VINs start 1J4FA. The 6th character marks the trim: 2 SE, 3 X, 4 Sport, 5 Sahara, 6 Rubicon. In BaT-listed VINs we checked, the 7th was 9 on short-wheelbase trucks and 4 on the Unlimited (a Rubicon reads 1J4FA69…, an Unlimited Rubicon 1J4FA64…), but NHTSA's decoder doesn't separate the two, so confirm by wheelbase.
  2. ID
    VIN engine codeThe 8th character is the engine: S is the 4.0 L six, P the 2.5 L four and 1 the 2.4 L four (NHTSA decoder: 1J4FA29P… is a 2.5 L SE, 1J4FA291… a 2.4 L SE).
  3. ID
    Real Rubicon hardwareFront and rear Dana 44s with air-actuated lockers and dash switches, the Rock-Trac NV241OR transfer case with a 4:1 low range, diamond-plate rocker guards and 16-inch alloys on LT245/75R16 tires.
  4. ID
    Rear axleSport, SE and X trucks normally have the weaker Dana 35. A Dana 44 rear was optional on Sport and Sahara, sometimes with a Trac-Lok limited slip. The axle tag and cover shape tell you which one you have.
  5. ID
    Transmission by yearManual 4.0 trucks used the AX-15 (1997–99), the NV3550 (2000–04) and the NSG370 six-speed (2005–06). Automatics are the 32RH 3-speed (1997–2002) and the 42RLE 4-speed (2003–06).
  6. ID
    Special editionsThe 2003 Tomb Raider Rubicon has a serial-numbered badge, red seat belts and Bright Silver paint. The 2001 60th Anniversary came only in black or Silverstone.

Cure death wobble: track bar, ball joints and tie rods
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · ≈ An afternoon to diagnose; a day to replace partsSHOP COST · A track bar costs a little over $50 (Drivingline); full steering kits cost more
SYMPTOMSUncontrollable side-to-side shaking of the front end, usually triggered by a bump at around 45 mph and stopped only by slowing right down.
THE FIXQuadratec's sequence: disconnect the steering stabilizer so it can't mask anything. With the key on and engine off, have a helper turn the wheel a quarter turn each way while you watch both ends of the track bar for movement. Then jack up the front and check tie-rod ends for play, ball joints by prying the tire, and wheel bearings by rocking at 12 and 6 o'clock. Inspect control arms and bushings. Tighten every steering and suspension bolt to spec, replace anything that moves, check tire balance and pressure, and finish with an alignment. Off Road Xtreme says a loose lower track-bar bolt is the most common cause. Drivingline warns a worn, oval frame-side hole may need drilling or an aftermarket bracket.

Replace the oil pump drive assembly (OPDA)
DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · ≈ 2–3 hours, if you're careful with the timingSHOP COST · Parts plus labor
SYMPTOMSA chirping 'laughing monkey' noise from the top of the engine, most often on 2005–06 4.0s. Left alone, Dorman says the center shaft can seize and destroy the cam bearing, and Quadratec warns failure can damage the engine.
THE FIXThe OPDA sits where a distributor would be and carries the camshaft position sensor, so its position matters. Following the factory service manual, bring cylinder 1 to top dead center on the compression stroke, mark the sensor's orientation, disconnect it, remove the hold-down clamp and lift the old drive out. Install the replacement in the same orientation so it engages the oil pump, then refit the sensor and complete the manual's synchronization check. Inspect the old unit's upper bushing and gear for wear, and change the oil and filter afterwards in case debris reached it. Dorman's version uses a self-lubricating upper bushing; Crown's moves the seal above the upper bushing.

SYMPTOMSOil weeping from the joint between the engine and bellhousing, and an oily crossmember and transmission. Rule out the valve cover, oil-filter adapter and pan gasket first.
THE FIXThe 4.0 uses a two-piece rear main seal that can be replaced without removing the transmission. On a TJ, drain the oil, remove the starter and drop the oil pan. Off Road Xtreme warns this may mean removing the front axle for clearance, and Jeepz.com owners loosened the exhaust for room. Mark the rear main cap, remove both seal halves, and install the new halves without nicking the lip (dish soap helps the upper half slide in). Use only a dab of RTV where the halves meet the cap. Make sure you have the right seal for your engine, because 1987–99 and 2000–06 engines take different kits. Check crank end play while you're in there.
TOOLS & PARTSPick · thin punch · O2-sensor wrench · torque wrench · RTV · jack and stands.

Replace a leaking plastic-tank radiator
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · ≈ 2–3 hours including bleedingSHOP COST · Around $150 for a replacement radiator (Drivingline)
SYMPTOMSCoolant smell, green crust at the top tank seam, and eventually a split tank spraying coolant over the engine.
THE FIXFrom Jeepfan's TJ write-up: let the engine cool and drain the radiator. Disconnect the upper and lower hoses and the transmission cooler lines if fitted, and pop the overflow bottle out of its holder. Loosen the fan shroud and push it back over the fan, since it doesn't need to come out. Remove the radiator bolts and lift it out. Thread the cooler fittings into the new radiator with sealant and drop it in, using the slotted lower bolt holes as a guide. Refit everything, fill with the correct coolant mixed 50/50 with distilled water, run it up to temperature while topping off, and recheck the level after a few drives as air works out.
TOOLS & PARTSDrain pan · hand tools · thread sealant · distilled water · correct coolant.

Replace a cracked exhaust manifold
DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · ≈ A long daySHOP COST · Parts plus labor
SYMPTOMSA ticking exhaust leak that's loudest from cold and may fade as the engine warms. Drivingline says it's more common on 1997–2000 trucks and is something you hear more than see.
THE FIXModern Jeeper's TJ/LJ guide: photograph and disconnect everything, then remove the O2 sensors, which can seize. The intake and exhaust manifolds share one gasket, so both come off. The hardest bolts are under the intake beside the exhaust tubes, so use extensions and wobble sockets. Clean the head face with a detail pad, not a grinding wheel. Fit the new gasket and manifold, put the intake back on top, then tighten the remaining fasteners. Reinstall the O2 sensors with anti-seize and check the new manifold's clearance to the front driveshaft.

Repair rusted rear lower control-arm mounts
DIFFICULTY · 5 / 5TIME · ≈ A weekend per sideSHOP COST · Repair section plus welding, or shop labor
SYMPTOMSFlaking, swollen or holed frame around the rear lower control-arm brackets, clunks from the rear, or a bracket that moves. Quadratec warns it can lead to structural failure.
THE FIXRick Rotondo's Quadratec guide on a 1998 TJ: support the frame on stands, remove the rear wheels, hold the axle on a jack, and unbolt the upper and lower arms, sway-bar link and shock on one side. Mark at least 1 inch of overlap past the rot, then cut out the bad frame, the sway-bar mount and the factory arm mount. Avoid the brake hose and fuel lines, and do not cut the body mount. Mock up the replacement section, trace and grind to bare metal, tack weld, recheck measurements, then fully weld. Prime and paint or coat it, refit the arms with Loctite, torque everything to spec, and recheck after a day. Rotondo says it's only for people experienced in cutting, grinding and welding.
TOOLS & PARTSFloor jack and stands · grinder · cutting wheel · welder · primer, paint or POR-15 · torque wrench.

Fix a transfer case that won't shift (linkage bushings)
DIFFICULTY · 1 / 5TIME · ≈ 1 hourSHOP COST · A few dollars in bushings
SYMPTOMSThe shifter moves but the transfer case won't go into 4-Low, or the lever flops around. The rod has usually popped out of a worn plastic bushing.
THE FIXQuadratec says to check this first when a TJ won't engage 4-Low. Under the Jeep, find the rod between the shifter and the NP231 or NV241OR range lever. Pop the rod out, pry out the old bushing, and press in a new one. Reconnect the rod and check that the shifter reaches every detent and the transfer case fully engages 2H, 4H, N and 4L. If the linkage has worn badly, or a body or motor-mount lift has changed its geometry, an adjustable or upgraded linkage is the permanent fix.
TOOLS & PARTSPliers · small pry bar · grease.

Asked on every forum

Jeep Wrangler TJ questions, answered

What's the biggest tire I can fit without a lift?

Four Wheel Trends says the biggest tire you can generally run on a stock TJ and keep full function is a 31x10.50-15, and that even that size may rub at full flex, which a washer or two on the steering stops usually cures. The Rubicon came from the factory on 31-inch LT245/75R16 Goodyear MT/Rs.

SOURCE · FOUR WHEEL TRENDS · QUADRATEC SPECS
Four-cylinder or 4.0?

Get the 4.0 if you can. Drivingline strongly recommends the six, which makes 190 hp and 235 lb-ft from 2000 against 120 hp for the 2.5 and 147 hp for the 2.4. The fours are reliable but run out of power once you add bigger tires, and swapping in a 4.0 later costs about as much as a V8 swap.

SOURCE · DRIVINGLINE
What is the 'laughing monkey' noise?

It's the sound of a failing oil pump drive assembly (OPDA), which Quadratec flags on 2005–06 4.0 TJs. Dorman says the factory upper bushing is starved of oil, and if the shaft seizes it can destroy the cam bearing. Replace it if there's no record it has been done.

SOURCE · QUADRATEC · DORMAN/NAPA
What causes death wobble, and will a new stabilizer fix it?

Worn or loose steering and suspension parts cause it, most often the track bar and its bolts, and Off Road Xtreme singles out a loose lower track-bar bolt. Quadratec says a bigger steering stabilizer only masks the problem. Find and replace the part that moves, then align.

SOURCE · QUADRATEC · OFF ROAD XTREME
Is the Dana 35 rear axle really that bad?

Drivingline calls it 'junk' and says its C-clip design is unreliable in stock form, while the low-pinion Dana 30 front is fine up to about 35-inch tires. A factory Dana 44 rear was optional on the Sport and Sahara and standard on the Rubicon. Otherwise, budget 'a couple grand' for a bolt-in upgrade.

SOURCE · DRIVINGLINE · WIKIPEDIA
What does the Rubicon add?

Dana 44 axles front and rear with 4.10 gears (Jeep spec sheets also list 4.11), air-actuated front and rear lockers that engage in low range below 10 mph, the NV241OR Rock-Trac transfer case with a 4.0:1 low range, 31-inch MT/R tires, 16-inch wheels and diamond-plate sill guards. RockCrawler reported a 65.92:1 crawl ratio. It was sold for 2003–06.

SOURCE · JEEP PRESS RELEASE · ROCKCRAWLER
Which manual transmission is best?

Drivingline says the AX-15 has notable synchro issues and the NV3550 that replaced it is stronger. Wikipedia lists the AX-15 for 1997–99, the NV3550 for 2000–04 and the six-speed NSG370 for 2005–06, though some sources say the six-speed arrived earlier. Fours used the AX-5 and, for 2003–04, the NV1500.

SOURCE · DRIVINGLINE · WIKIPEDIA
How much can a TJ tow?

Jeep's 2005–06 spec sheet rates the 4.0 at 2,000 lb, but the 2.4 and the 4.0 automatic with the base 3.73 axle at 1,000 lb, so check your build. Wikipedia gives 2,000 lb for the TJ and 3,500 lb for the long-wheelbase Unlimited.

SOURCE · QUADRATEC (JEEP SPECS) · WIKIPEDIA
What coolant does it take?

Quadratec's factory-based chart gives about 9 qt for the four-cylinders and 10.5 qt for the 4.0, mixed 50/50. Owners commonly report a change from conventional green coolant to Mopar's HOAT type around 2001, but we couldn't confirm the exact changeover from a primary source, so check the label on the overflow bottle and the owner's manual [CONFIRM IN MANUAL]. Don't mix types.

SOURCE · QUADRATEC · OWNER'S MANUAL
What gear oil goes in the axles?

Quadratec's factory-based chart lists GL-5 80W-90 or 75W-140 synthetic, 75W-140 synthetic for the Rubicon's Dana 44s, and 4 oz of limited-slip additive for a Trac-Lok. ExtremeTerrain recommends 75W-140 in the rear for warm-weather towing. Haynes' schedule changes the differential lubricant every 30,000 miles or 24 months.

SOURCE · QUADRATEC · EXTREMETERRAIN · HAYNES
Why does my TJ leak oil?

The usual spots on the 4.0 are the rear main seal, oil pan gasket, valve cover gasket and oil-filter adapter gasket. Off Road Xtreme notes the valve cover has many bolts and takes longer than expected. Drivingline says the two-piece rear main can be replaced without pulling the transmission.

SOURCE · OFF ROAD XTREME · DRIVINGLINE
TJ or LJ (Unlimited)?

The Unlimited, sold for 2004–06, stretches the TJ by 15 inches, 10 of them in the wheelbase (103.4 in versus 93.4 in). Drivingline says that adds cargo room and stability on steep trails, but the LJ costs more because it was built for only three model years.

SOURCE · DRIVINGLINE · WIKIPEDIA

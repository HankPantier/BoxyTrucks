PART 01

Overview

Where it came from, why it matters, and how it changed year to year.

The story

In February 1986 American Motors unveiled the 1987 Jeep Wrangler, and on May 13 of that year the first CJ replacement in four decades went on sale. It was called the YJ, and in Canada simply the Jeep YJ, because General Motors of Canada owned the Wrangler name there. Underneath, it was very much an updated CJ-7, with the same 93.4-inch wheelbase, leaf springs at both ends and a body that was largely interchangeable with the old tub. The rectangular headlights were new, and the fight over them has never really stopped.

A CJ-7 WITH MANNERS

Development of a CJ-7 replacement was approved in 1982. Chuck Mashigan's design was frozen by the fall of 1983, CJ-7-based mules ran late that year, and the first production-body prototypes followed in spring 1984. The brief was to keep the CJ's character while making it calmer on the road. The YJ kept leaf springs similar to the CJ's but made them wider, added track bars and anti-roll bars, and widened the track. Ground clearance dropped slightly as a result.

Wikipedia notes that the anti-roll bars made the Wrangler less easy to flip for untrained or unwary drivers. That mattered for a vehicle being sold to a much wider public than the CJ's farmers and diehards. The YJ also got a bigger windshield, with wipers that parked on the glass because their wider sweep needed the room. Both details disappeared when the TJ arrived. Hagerty calls the square lamps 'a point of contention for many Jeep lovers yet a point of pride for YJ owners', which is a polite way of describing a feud that has lasted since 1986.

FOURS, SIXES AND A FRENCH GEARBOX

Early YJs offered two engines. The 2.5-liter AMC four had throttle-body injection and 117 hp, which was actually more than the carry-over 4.2-liter six. That carbureted 258 made 112 hp but far more torque, and it fed its Carter BBD through a computer-controlled feedback system with a lot of vacuum hose. The six was originally paired with a Peugeot BA-10/5 five-speed, which ExtremeTerrain calls historically unreliable, and the 1987-only NP207 transfer case. The NP231 replaced the NP207 for 1988, and the Aisin AX-15 replaced the Peugeot from 1989.

The big change came in 1991, when the fuel-injected 4.0-liter six replaced the 258. Hagerty quotes 180 hp, and says it is often the most desirable YJ engine, worth a larger premium over the four. Through the run, the front axle was a Dana 30 with a vacuum-operated disconnect, and the rear was a Dana 35 with a Dana 44 available in some years. Later changes added an extended roll cage for rear shoulder belts (1992), optional anti-lock brakes (1993), an automatic for the four-cylinder (1994), and larger front U-joints (1995).

ISLANDERS, RENEGADES AND A THEME PARK

The YJ was sold as a fashion item as much as a tool, and the trim list shows it. The Laredo got chrome and a hardtop, the 1988 Sahara got body-color flares and green trail-cloth seats, and the 1988–1993 Islander wore sunset graphics. The Renegade Decor Group, fitted by Autostyle in Detroit from October 1990, added the 4.0, 8-inch alloy wheels and a widebody look for $4,266 over a base Wrangler in 1991. The 1995 Rio Grande closed the run with a Pueblo-themed interior. Sahara YJs also starred in Jurassic Park in 1993. Hagerty's price data says Renegades bring the most today, followed fairly closely by Saharas and Islanders. The Islander runs about 11 percent above a base model.

HOW IT ENDED

YJs were built in Brampton, Ontario, until that plant closed on April 23, 1992, and then in Toledo, Ohio, the factory that had built Willys Jeeps in the war. Production of the 1995 model year ended in December 1995, with 632,231 YJs built. There was no 1996 Wrangler, although leftover YJs were still being sold into mid-1996. The TJ that followed brought back round headlights and replaced the leaf springs with coils, which made the YJ the last Jeep with a leaf-sprung front axle.

For years that made the YJ the unwanted Wrangler: too new to be a classic CJ and too crude to compete with coil-sprung trucks. Hagerty now puts a median #3-condition YJ at about $8,100, against $9,900 for an earlier CJ-7, and notes that interest is rising, particularly among Gen Xers who grew up with the square-eyed Jeep.

SIDEBAR · THE RENEGADE

A $4,266 decal package that became the collector's YJ

The Renegade Decor Group arrived in October 1990 and ran until 1994. Jeep didn't build it on the line. Autostyle in Detroit fitted the package, which included the 4.0-liter six, 8-inch aluminum wheels and a leather-wrapped steering wheel, and gave the YJ a widebody look. For 1991 the package cost $4,266 over a base Wrangler, before options like a $923 hardtop and the mandatory $164 rear defroster.

The early colors were white, black and red, with blue added for 1992 and bronze for 1993. Three decades later Hagerty's data shows the Renegade at the top of the YJ price ladder, which says something about how markets treat once-unloved trucks.

PART 02

Specs & capability

How it’s built, what it can do, and what fits under it.

How it’s built

The YJ is a ladder-frame, leaf-sprung Jeep built on the CJ-7's 93.4-inch wheelbase, with wider springs, track bars and anti-roll bars at both ends. The front axle is a Dana 30 with a vacuum disconnect, and the rear is a Dana 35, with a Dana 44 available in some years. Part-time four-wheel drive came through the NP207 for 1987 only and the NP231 from 1988.

What to check before you buy

  1. 01
    Frame, spring hangers and floors firstQuadratec's inspection checklist names the rear leaf-spring mounts, frame rails, skid-plate mounts, rear crossmember, driver-side floor and roll-bar mounts. Lift the carpet: it warns that moisture trapped under old padding rots YJ floor pans from the top down.
  2. 02
    Does 4WD actually engage?The front axle is engaged by a vacuum motor. If the 4WD light stays off, or 4WD feels inconsistent, suspect dry-rotted, cracked or disconnected vacuum lines. Many owners convert to a cable-operated disconnect.
  3. 03
    Clutch slave cylinder (early YJs)Early manual YJs use an internal slave cylinder built into the release bearing. 4WD Mechanix dates it to 1987–92 with an external slave from 1993; Wikipedia says the slave moved outside the bellhousing for 1994, and Advance Adapters sells its internal-bearing hose kit for 1987–93. When the internal slave leaks, fluid can ruin the clutch disc, and replacing it means pulling the transmission. Look for fluid weeping from the bellhousing and a sinking pedal, and check whether the slave is visible on the outside of the bellhousing.
  4. 04
    Know the gearbox and transfer caseSix-cylinder trucks from 1987–89 may have the Peugeot BA-10/5, which ExtremeTerrain calls historically unreliable. A 1987 may also have the NP207 transfer case. An AX-15 and NP231 combination is what most buyers want.
  5. 05
    Carbureted 4.2 or injected 4.0?The 1987–90 4.2 uses a computer-controlled Carter BBD with a maze of vacuum lines that are prone to leaks. It can be made reliable, but the 1991–95 4.0 is the engine Hagerty says commands the premium.
  6. 06
    Leaf springs, shackles and bushingsCheck for sagging or broken leaves, cracked bushings, rusted shackles and uneven ride height. Most YJs are more than 30 years old, so tired springs are normal and fresh ones change the ride.
  7. 07
    Electrical groundsFlickering lights, jumping gauges and intermittent starting often come from bad grounds. Quadratec suggests cleaning the firewall ground straps and battery connections before chasing anything bigger.
  8. 08
    The rear axleMost YJs have the Dana 35 rear. Drivingline calls the same axle in the TJ 'junk' because of its C-clip design, so if you plan bigger tires, price in a Dana 44 swap or upgraded shafts. A factory Dana 44 is worth looking for.

Spotting the real thing

Sixes, trim packages and build plant all change what a YJ is worth, and a lot of 30-year-old Jeeps have had engines, axles and decals swapped. Let the VIN and the hardware tell the story.

  1. ID
    VIN, 1989–95The 5th character is Y (Wrangler). The 6th is the trim: 1 is the base S, 4 Sahara, 5 Laredo, 6 Renegade. The 8th is the engine: E is the throttle-body 2.5 L four (1989–90), P the multi-point 2.5 L four (1991–95), S the 4.0 L six (1991–95), and M (1989) or T (1990) the 4.2 L six. NHTSA's decoder confirms the E, P and S codes.
  2. ID
    VIN, 1987–88Early trucks use the older AMC-style VIN, starting 2BC on the Brampton-built examples we decoded. The 4th character is the engine (C is the 4.2 L six; H the 2.5 L TBI four) and characters 6–7 read 81 for Wrangler.
  3. ID
    Brampton or ToledoIn the NHTSA decoder, VINs starting with 2 decode to Brampton, Ontario, and VINs starting with 1J4 to Toledo, Ohio. Brampton built YJs until April 1992.
  4. ID
    RenegadeReal Renegades were finished by Autostyle near Detroit and carry a small Autostyle sticker on the driver's door jamb above the latch. They came with the 4.0 six, 8-inch alloys, 30x9.50 tires, fender-mounted fog lights and the 20-gallon tank.
  5. ID
    Engine and transfer caseA 4.0 L six means 1991 or later; a 4.2 means 1990 or earlier. The NP207 transfer case is 1987 only; everything after uses the NP231.
  6. ID
    Late YJ details1994–95 manual trucks have the external clutch slave cylinder (some 1993s may too). Some last-build 1995s got TJ-style hood bump stops, reinforced tailgate hinges and TJ rear bumpers.

Fix the vacuum front-axle disconnect (or convert to cable)
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · ≈ An afternoon to diagnose; longer if the axle shift parts are wornSHOP COST · Parts plus labor
SYMPTOMS4WD light stays off, the front axle won't drive in 4H, or it engages and drops out with a clunk under load at speed.
THE FIXThe 4WD Mechanix forum's diagnosis runs in order. First, bleed-down test the shift motor and lines with a hand vacuum pump and gauge, and confirm the motor holds vacuum. Check that the engage and disengage ports at the transfer case switch and motor get steady vacuum (about 17 in. Hg in that thread). Then test the supply side: reserve tank, check valve, hoses and manifold fittings. Tee a gauge into the engage line and road test, watching for drops under load. If vacuum holds, pull the shift motor and inspect the shift fork, clutch hub and axle shafts. The common permanent fix is a cable-operated disconnect, such as the JKS Posi-Lok.

Replace the clutch and internal slave cylinder (early YJs)
DIFFICULTY · 4 / 5TIME · ≈ A full weekendSHOP COST · Parts plus labor
SYMPTOMSSinking or spongy clutch pedal, fluid loss from the clutch reservoir, a slipping clutch after fluid has soaked the disc, and fluid at the bottom of the bellhousing.
THE FIXOn early YJs (1987–92 per 4WD Mechanix; through 1993 per Wikipedia and Advance Adapters) the slave is part of the release bearing inside the bellhousing, so this is a clutch job. 4WD Mechanix's YJ/TJ procedure: remove the transfer case, loosen the hydraulic line with flare-nut wrenches, then drain and cap the line. Loosen the crankshaft position sensor so it isn't damaged, and unclip wiring. Support the transmission on a jack and the engine under the oil pan, and lower no more than about 3 inches. Slide the transmission and bellhousing straight back. Mark the flywheel and pressure plate, loosen the cover bolts in a crisscross pattern, and inspect the flywheel and pilot bearing. Fit a new disc and slave/release bearing, then bleed with clean DOT 3 or DOT 4 fluid.
TOOLS & PARTSTransmission jack · flare-nut wrenches · clutch alignment tool · torque wrench · brake washer (treat old linings as if they contain asbestos) · DOT 3/4 fluid.

SYMPTOMSStalling, flooding, hunting idle, loss of power or running hot. Many of these are vacuum leaks or ignition faults rather than the carburetor itself.
THE FIXMoses Ludel's 4WD Mechanix guide says to diagnose before rebuilding. Check plugs, wires, cap and rotor, the vacuum and centrifugal advance, the PCV and every emissions vacuum hose. Mist penetrant near the throttle shaft ends at idle to find leaks. If the carb is the problem, record the idle mixture screw turns, strip it, and clean metal parts in carb cleaner. Clean rubber, plastic and electrical parts with alcohol. Clear the idle fuel pickup tubes, since a clogged one caused stalling in his example. Free the stepper motor's sticky rack. Set float height with a T-gauge, then the vacuum piston gap, metering rods, pump and choke to the kit specs. Refit with a new base gasket.
TOOLS & PARTST-gauge · hand vacuum pump · timing light · carb cleaner · alcohol · thread locker · Mopar or Echlin/NAPA premium rebuild kit (Ludel's preference).

Replace leaf springs, shackles and bushings
DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · ≈ 1 hour per pair of bushings (ExtremeTerrain); longer for full springsSHOP COST · Parts plus labor
SYMPTOMSSagging or uneven ride height, clunks over bumps, cracked or squeezed-out bushings, broken leaves and rusted shackles.
THE FIXFollowing Skyjacker's YJ instructions: support the frame on stands, remove the wheels, shocks and U-bolts, and hold the axle on a floor jack. Unbolt the old springs. Grease the new urethane bushings and sleeves and press them into the spring eyes. Factory rear springs put the small eye at the rear. Install the springs with the eye and shackle bolts loose, set the axle back on the spring center pins, and fit new U-bolts (the kit specifies 90 ft-lb at the front). Tighten the eye and shackle bolts only with the vehicle on the ground and loaded, then re-torque the U-bolts after 50 and 100 miles. For bushings alone, ExtremeTerrain says to clean the spring eyes and shackle brackets back to bare metal and grease every contact surface.
TOOLS & PARTSJack stands · floor jack · press or vise for bushings · wire brush · grease · torque wrench · new U-bolts.

Replace the rear main seal (4.0 / 4.2 six)
DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · ≈ A daySHOP COST · Seal kit and oil pan gasket; shops charge mostly for labor
SYMPTOMSOil dripping from where the engine meets the bellhousing, and an oily transmission and crossmember. Rule out the valve cover and oil pan gaskets first, because oil runs downhill.
THE FIXThe AMC six uses a two-piece rear main seal that is replaced from underneath without removing the transmission. Drain the oil, remove the starter and the oil pan, and mark the rear main bearing cap so it goes back the same way. Remove both seal halves, using a pick and a thin punch to work the upper half around the crank. Install the new halves with only a dab of RTV where they meet the cap, being careful not to nick the lip (one owner had to buy a second set). Torque the cap to the factory spec [CONFIRM IN MANUAL], refit the pan with a new gasket and refill. While the pan is off, check crank end play.

Cut out and replace rusted floor pans
DIFFICULTY · 4 / 5TIME · ≈ A weekend per sideSHOP COST · Panels plus welding consumables, or body-shop labor
SYMPTOMSSoft, flaking or holed metal under the driver's carpet, water in the footwell, and rust around the seat and roll-bar mounts.
THE FIXPull the carpet and padding: Quadratec notes trapped moisture under old padding is what rots YJ floors. Wire-brush the area to find good metal, mark a cut line inside the sound metal, and cut out the rot without cutting into the frame or body mounts. Trim the replacement pan to overlap or butt the opening, clamp it, tack it, then stitch-weld to avoid warping. Seam-seal and paint both sides, and don't put the old soggy padding back. Repair any body-mount or roll-bar mount areas properly, because those carry loads.

Chase down front-end shimmy and worn steering
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · ≈ An afternoon to inspect; parts swaps varySHOP COST · Parts plus alignment
SYMPTOMSA violent side-to-side shake after a bump at around 45 mph, wandering, or a clunk when you turn the wheel with the engine off.
THE FIXQuadratec's death-wobble guide applies to any solid front axle. Disconnect the steering stabilizer so it can't hide anything. Have a helper rock the wheel a quarter turn each way while you watch the track bar for movement at both ends. Jack up the front and check tie-rod ends for play, ball joints by prying the tire, and wheel bearings by rocking the wheel at 12 and 6 o'clock. Inspect control points and bushings. Tighten everything to spec, replace what moves, check tire balance and pressure, then get an alignment from a 4x4-savvy shop. A stronger stabilizer only masks the problem.

Asked on every forum

Jeep Wrangler YJ questions, answered

What's the biggest tire I can fit without a lift?

A stock YJ usually takes 30-inch tires on stock rims without trouble. Four Wheel Trends says 31x10.50s can be squeezed on, but they may need wheels with different backspacing and can still rub, so don't wheel that setup hard without a lift.

SOURCE · FOUR WHEEL TRENDS
Which YJ should I buy?

Most buyers want a 1991–1995 truck with the fuel-injected 4.0 six. Hagerty calls it often the most desirable engine and says it commands a larger premium over the four. For 1987–90, Hagerty says the four- and six-cylinder versions differ by only a few hundred dollars in #3 condition.

SOURCE · HAGERTY
Why does the YJ have square headlights?

Neither Wikipedia nor Hagerty gives an official reason. Wikipedia says only that the rectangular lamps set it apart from the round-eyed CJs. The TJ brought round headlights back for 1997. Owners who can't live with them fit round-headlight conversion kits.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · HAGERTY
My 4WD light won't come on. Is the transfer case broken?

Probably not. The YJ front axle is engaged by a vacuum motor, and Quadratec says dry-rotted, cracked or disconnected vacuum lines are the usual culprit. Test the system with a hand vacuum pump before buying parts, or convert to a cable-operated disconnect.

SOURCE · QUADRATEC · 4WD MECHANIX
Should I avoid the Peugeot gearbox?

Six-cylinder YJs from 1987–89 could have the Peugeot BA-10/5 five-speed, which ExtremeTerrain describes as historically unreliable. Wikipedia lists the Aisin AX-15 behind the 4.2 from 1989, and the AX-15 stayed with the 4.0 through 1995. Four-cylinder trucks use the AX-5.

SOURCE · EXTREMETERRAIN · WIKIPEDIA
NP207 or NP231 transfer case?

The NP207 was used on 1987 YJs only, and ExtremeTerrain calls it a poor unit. The NP231, used from 1988 onward, is the stronger and far better-supported case. A 1988–95 YJ will have the NP231.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · EXTREMETERRAIN
Which years have the internal clutch slave cylinder?

Sources disagree. 4WD Mechanix says 1987–92 YJs used a slave cylinder built into the release bearing and that 1993 brought the external slave and pivot arm used in the TJ. Wikipedia dates the move outside the bellhousing to 1994, and Advance Adapters lists its internal-bearing hose kit for 1987–93. Treat 1993 as either, and look for an external slave on the bellhousing. The internal type means pulling the transmission to replace it.

SOURCE · 4WD MECHANIX · WIKIPEDIA · ADVANCE ADAPTERS
What fluids do the axles and transfer case take?

Quadratec's factory-based chart lists GL-5 80W-90 or 75W-140 synthetic for the Dana 30, Dana 35 and Dana 44, with 4 oz of limited-slip additive for a Trac-Lok. ExtremeTerrain recommends 75W-140 in the rear for warm-weather towing. The NP231 takes automatic transmission fluid (ExtremeTerrain lists ATF+3 or ATF+4); Quadratec's chart gives 2.2 pints for the NP231 and 4.4 pints for the NP207. Haynes' maintenance schedule changes the transfer case and differential lubricant every 30,000 miles or 24 months. Capacities in retailer charts disagree, so fill to the bottom of the fill hole [CONFIRM IN MANUAL].

SOURCE · QUADRATEC · EXTREMETERRAIN · HAYNES
Can the 4.2's carburetor be made reliable?

Yes. Moses Ludel's 4WD Mechanix guide says the Carter BBD can be reliable when rebuilt to factory specs, and that a clogged fuel filter, a weak mechanical pump or a vacuum leak can mimic carburetor faults. He calls Mopar's multi-point injection conversion the bigger upgrade.

SOURCE · 4WD MECHANIX
Is a YJ a good investment?

Hagerty puts a median #3-condition YJ at about $8,100, against $9,900 for an earlier CJ-7. Its Hagerty Vehicle Rating is 86 and climbing while the CJ's has fallen, although Hagerty says the rating is not a predictor of future collectability. Renegades bring the most, followed by Saharas and Islanders.

SOURCE · HAGERTY
What's the difference between the Sahara, Islander and Renegade?

The Sahara (from 1988) included most options, with body-color flares, green trail-cloth seats and fog lamps. The Islander (1988–93) had sunset graphics and body-color flares and steps. The Renegade Decor Group (October 1990–94) added the 4.0, 8-inch alloy wheels and a widebody look.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA

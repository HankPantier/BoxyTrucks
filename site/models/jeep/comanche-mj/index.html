PART 01

Overview

Where it came from, why it matters, and how it changed year to year.

The story

AMC unveiled the Comanche in August 1985 to more than 1,500 dealers in the ballroom of the MGM Grand in Las Vegas. It was a compact pickup built from the Cherokee XJ: the same unibody, Quadra-Link coil front end and engines from the cab forward, and behind the cab a fully boxed frame and a removable bed. At $7,049 for a two-wheel-drive model it was the cheapest Jeep of 1986. It lasted seven model years and 190,446 trucks, and it was Jeep's last pickup until the Gladiator 27 years later.

A CHEROKEE WITH A TRUCK BOLTED ON

AMC needed money and needed a truck. Its finances were weak, a new Canadian plant was being built for the Eagle Premier, and compact pickups were selling. The answer was cheap to engineer: keep the XJ's front half and its 'Uniframe' unibody, and graft on a frame for the bed. Wikipedia describes that rear structure as fully boxed rather than the usual open C-channel, with rails more than eight inches deep and a large X-member over the rear axle. AMC had first tried the idea on its 1971 'Cowboy' pickup prototype.

The long-bed, seven-foot truck came first, for 1986. A six-foot short bed followed for 1987, on a 113-inch wheelbase against 119.9 inches for the long bed. Up front was the XJ's coil-sprung Dana 30 (or a beam axle on 2WD trucks). At the back were rear leaf springs much longer than the Cherokee's and, as standard, a Dana 35. The Metric Ton package for long beds added heavier springs, wheels and tires and a Dana 44, raising payload from 1,400 to 2,205 lb. Wikipedia notes that this was more than many full-size pickups could carry.

FROM A WEAK V6 TO THE 4.0

1986 Comanches had AMC's 2.5-liter four, a carbureted GM 2.8 V6 that made slightly less power than the four, or a Renault 2.1 turbodiesel. For 1987 the 4.0-liter straight-six arrived with 173 hp and 220 lb-ft, about 50% more power than the V6, together with the Aisin AW4 automatic and the NP231 Command-Trac and NP242 Selec-Trac transfer cases. Chrysler bought AMC in March 1987 and mostly left the truck alone. It gained an eight-slot grille for 1988, the sporty Eliminator trim, and the Aisin AX-15 five-speed in place of the Peugeot BA-10/5 during 1989.

For 1991 Chrysler replaced the Renix engine management with its own, and the 4.0 became the 190 hp 'High Output'. Quadratec's fitment listings also show Chrysler 8.25-inch rear axles in 1991–92 trucks. By then, though, the truck had stopped selling. Calendar-year output fell from 43,718 in 1988 to 5,188 in 1991. Jeep-Eagle dealers asked Chrysler to drop it and give them a Dakota-based pickup instead, and Chrysler wanted Jeep to sell SUVs and Dodge to sell trucks. The last Comanche left Toledo on June 12, 1992.

WHY THEY'RE WORTH SAVING

Today the Comanche is the XJ's rarer, more useful sibling. Comanche Club members got vehicle-in-operation figures from Key Parts showing about 18,600 MJs registered in the US and Canada in late 2021, roughly a tenth of production. Mechanically it is the XJ: the same 4.0, AX-15 and AW4, Dana 30 and NP231, so the drivetrain parts aren't hard to find. The cab interior and everything behind the doors are a different story. Bed sides, tailgates, taillights and bench-seat sliders are MJ-only and getting scarce.

On the trail it behaves like a long Cherokee with a bigger rear overhang. The XJ front suspension, the 4.0 and the easy 4WD conversions owners talk about make it a natural budget builder, and a Metric Ton or 8.25-axle truck has the rear end to carry a camper shell full of gear. Like the CJ-8 Scrambler, it suits owners who want a Jeep with a bed. The modern equivalent is the JL Unlimited-based Gladiator.

SIDEBAR · THE NAME THAT DIDN'T COME BACK

Comanche, Scrambler or Gladiator?

When Jeep returned to pickups for 2020, Wikipedia says it considered reviving the Comanche name alongside Scrambler before choosing Gladiator. It suggests the Comanche name's weaker recognition, and sensitivity to the Comanche Nation, likely counted against it.

AMC had other plans for the MJ. When it was setting up Beijing Jeep, it brought Chinese officials into the negotiations with the aim of building and selling Comanches there too. Wikipedia says the aim was to build and sell Comanches in China through the venture. In the end, Beijing Jeep became known for the Cherokee, which it built for decades.

PART 02

Specs & capability

How it’s built, what it can do, and what fits under it.

How it’s built

The MJ is an XJ Cherokee from the B-pillar forward: welded unibody, Quadra-Link coil front suspension with four control arms and a track bar, and the same engines and transfer cases. Behind the cab, a fully boxed rear frame with a large X-member over the axle carries a removable bed on long leaf springs. Two wheelbases, 113 in (six-foot bed, from 1987) and 119.9 in (seven-foot bed), share the cab.

Buying one

What to pay, what to check, and how to tell a good one from a clone.

What to check before you buy

  1. 01
    Pull up the carpetNAXJA and Comanche Club members agree: rust is the number-one issue, and unless the truck lived in the Southwest or Southern California, expect it in the floors. Rusty seat brackets or bolts usually mean rusty floor pans below.
  2. 02
    Check where the cab meets the bed, and the railsLook hard where the bed meets the cab and where the unibody joins the rear frame. NAXJA members warn that bulging rail bottoms mean rust inside. Water also enters through the rear cab bulkhead and window; one member says more than one MJ has gone to the crusher because of it.
  3. 03
    Look for cracks and old welds on the frameComanche Club members say to inspect the frame closely for cracks or signs of weld repair, especially on trucks that hauled or towed.
  4. 04
    Bed, tailgate and taillightsThese are MJ-only parts and hard to replace. A crushed bed side or a missing tailgate costs more and takes longer to fix than a tired engine.
  5. 05
    Identify the rear axleAn oval cover usually means a Dana 35, and an angular, roughly hexagonal cover means a Dana 44, per NAXJA. A 1991–92 truck may have the Chrysler 8.25. Ask whether it's a Metric Ton truck; sellers often don't know.
  6. 06
    Renix or HO?1987–90 4.0s run Renix management, which stores no codes and is diagnosed with a multimeter. 1991–92 HO trucks have Chrysler's system and 190 hp. Both are good engines, but Renix trucks reward owners who like electrical troubleshooting.
  7. 07
    Hydraulic clutch and fuse boxOn manual trucks, check the clutch master cylinder. Comanche Club members warn a leak can drip brake fluid into the fuse box, which damages it.
  8. 08
    2.5 or 4.0?The 4.0 is the one to have. If you buy a 2.5, Comanche Club members suggest looking for a 4.10 or 4.56 axle ratio. The AX-4 and AX-5 gearboxes behind the 2.5 won't bolt up to a 4.0.

Spotting the real thing

Every MJ looks alike from the doors forward, so the details are in the bed length, the axle and the VIN. Metric Ton trucks and 4.0 HO trucks are worth more, and sellers often don't know what they have.

  1. ID
    Metric Ton packageLong-bed only: heavier leaf springs, wheels and tires and a Dana 44 rear axle, rated at 2,205 lb payload. NAXJA members say to ask for it by name and confirm the Dana 44.
  2. ID
    Rear axle coverPer NAXJA, an oval cover usually means a Dana 35 and an angular, roughly hexagonal cover means a Dana 44. 1991–92 trucks may have the Chrysler 8.25 (Quadratec lists 27-spline 8.25 lockers for them). Offroaders lists an AMC 20 as a 1986 heavy-duty option.
  3. ID
    Bed lengthShort bed: 6 ft on a 113 in wheelbase (1987–92). Long bed: 7 ft on 119.9 in (1986–92). Per Comanche Club, the Eliminator, Chief and SporTruck were short-bed only and the Laredo long-bed only.
  4. ID
    Renix or HO1987–90 4.0s have Renix management; 1991–92 trucks are the 190 hp 'High Output' with Chrysler controls. 1986–87 grilles have ten slots and 1988–92 grilles eight.
  5. ID
    VINNHTSA's free vPIC decoder reads MJ VINs, including drive type and bed length on early trucks. The tenth character is the model year (G = 1986 through N = 1992). AMC-era VINs start 1JT and Chrysler-era 1989–92 VINs start 1J7. Comanche Club keeps a VIN registry.

Common repairs

The jobs owners actually end up doing, rated for a competent home mechanic with a decent tool kit. Always confirm torque figures, fluids and procedures against the factory service manual for your VIN, and work safely — support the truck on stands, never just a jack. SYMPTOMSWet carpet, rusty seat-bracket bolts, holes under the mats, crumbling rockers, and rust at the rear cab bulkhead where the bed meets the cab.
THE FIXStrip the cab, cut out the rot to clean metal, and treat what you can't cut. Trial-fit the panels, then plug- or stitch-weld them in. If the rails under the cab are swollen with rust, the job is much bigger.

Hot stalls and no-starts: CPS, ballast resistor, grounds
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · ≈ 1–2 hoursSHOP COST · A sensor or resistor plus your time
SYMPTOMSThe engine dies when hot and won't restart until it cools, cranks without spark, or the fuel pump won't run. Comanche Club owners name the crankshaft position sensor (CPS) and the Renix fuel-pump ballast resistor as the usual culprits, and say to check the body grounds first for strange electrical faults.
THE FIXTest before you buy. Clean and tighten the engine, body and taillight grounds, and the ground at the fuel tank. Then test the CPS hot, right after a failure, with a multimeter against the resistance values for your year. On Renix trucks, check the ballast resistor; a jumper wire from the battery to the pump circuit will prove it at the roadside. Replace the failed part with one for your exact year, because the parts changed for the 1991 HO system.

SYMPTOMSClunks over bumps, a rear end that steers itself, rusted or seized shackles, or bent lower shock-mount bolts. Comanche Club owners recommend upgrading the rear lower shock mounts.
THE FIXSupport the frame and take the weight off the springs. Soak every bolt in penetrant for a few days first. Unbolt the shackles and front hangers, drop each spring, press out the old bushings and fit new ones (polyurethane needs grease). Fit new shackles, torque everything at ride height, and replace the shocks while you're there.
TOOLS & PARTSJack stands · penetrating oil · bushing press or vise · torque wrench

Fix or bypass the front axle disconnect (CAD)
DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · ≈ 2–4 hoursSHOP COST · A manual-disconnect or 'shift-on-the-fly' kit
SYMPTOMS4WD that engages at the transfer case but doesn't drive the front wheels, or grinding from the front axle. Comanche Club members say to check the vacuum-operated central axle disconnect before your first trail, and to lock it in or bypass it if necessary.
THE FIXCheck the vacuum lines and the switch on the transfer case first. If the shift motor or fork is the problem, a manual disconnect kit replaces the vacuum actuator with a cable or lever, or the axle can be made permanently engaged with the right parts. Drain the gear oil, unbolt the shift-motor housing, fit the kit and refill with 75W-90.
TOOLS & PARTSVacuum gauge or hand pump · sockets · gasket scraper · RTV · gear oil

Cure death wobble: track bar and steering
DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · ≈ Half a daySHOP COST · Parts plus an alignment
SYMPTOMSViolent front shake after a bump at speed, wandering and clunks. The MJ front end is the XJ's, with the same weak points: the track bar, tie-rod ends, drag link and steering box.
THE FIXWith the engine running, have a helper turn the wheel back and forth while you watch the track-bar ends and bracket, the tie-rod and drag-link ends and the steering box for movement. Replace worn parts (an adjustable track bar re-centers a lifted axle), tighten everything at ride height, and check the unibody around the steering box for cracks. A steering stabilizer hides wobble; it doesn't cure it.
TOOLS & PARTSHelper · pry bar · tie-rod puller · torque wrench · alignment afterward

Asked on every forum

Jeep Comanche MJ questions, answered

What's the biggest tire I can fit without a lift?

The MJ's front end and fenders are the XJ's, so XJ consensus is a fair guide: a 235/75R15 (about 29 in) on the stock 15 in wheels fits, and a 30x9.50R15 is the practical limit, possibly rubbing the front lower control arms at full lock. NAXJA posters say a 31x10.50 needs bump stops or trimming. We found no separate MJ-only consensus. Load the bed before judging rear clearance.

SOURCE · NAXJA (XJ THREADS)
Which years and engines should I buy?

Buy a 4.0, ideally a 1991–92 HO: 190 hp and Chrysler's easier-to-diagnose engine management, the same reason Off Road Xtreme recommends the HO in the XJ. 1987–90 Renix trucks are good too, but they're diagnosed with a multimeter. 1986 trucks have no 4.0. If you do buy a 2.5, Comanche Club members say look for 4.10 or 4.56 gears.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · OFF ROAD XTREME · COMANCHE CLUB
What is a Metric Ton Comanche?

A long-bed heavy-duty package with heavier leaf springs, wheels and tires and a Dana 44 rear axle. It raised payload from 1,400 to 2,205 lb. Sellers often don't know they have one, so check the axle and springs.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · NAXJA
Which rear axle do I have?

The Dana 35 was standard. The Dana 44 came with the Metric Ton package (and on some other trucks; Comanche Club members' trucks include a 1992 short bed with a limited-slip Dana 44). Offroaders lists an AMC 20 as a 1986 heavy-duty option, and Quadratec's locker fitment shows the 27-spline Chrysler 8.25 on 1991–92 trucks. NAXJA: oval cover means Dana 35; angular, roughly hexagonal cover means Dana 44.

SOURCE · OFFROADERS · QUADRATEC · NAXJA
Short bed or long bed?

The six-foot short bed rides on a 113 in wheelbase and arrived for 1987. The seven-foot long bed is on 119.9 in and was the only length in 1986. The short bed is better on tight trails; Metric Ton trucks are long beds. The Eliminator was short-bed only, per Comanche Club.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · COMANCHE CLUB
Where do they rust?

Under the carpet, at the rear cab bulkhead and window seals, where the bed meets the cab, and where the unibody meets the rear frame. NAXJA members say bulging rail bottoms mean rust inside.

SOURCE · NAXJA · COMANCHE CLUB
Are parts hard to find?

Drivetrain and front-end parts are shared with the XJ and easy to find. Comanche Club members say everything behind the doors is different, and the bed, tailgate, taillights and bench-seat sliders are MJ-specific and scarce. An XJ fuel pump and sender don't fit without fabrication.

SOURCE · COMANCHE CLUB · NAXJA
How much can it tow and carry?

Kelley Blue Book lists a 1992 Comanche at 5,000 lb maximum towing and 1,475 lb payload. Wikipedia gives 1,400 lb for a standard truck and 2,205 lb for the Metric Ton package.

SOURCE · KBB · WIKIPEDIA
Command-Trac or Selec-Trac?

From 1987 the NP231 Command-Trac is part-time and the NP242 Selec-Trac adds a full-time 4WD mode. Both have 2.72:1 low range. 1986 trucks used the NP207 and NP228, both with 2.61:1 low range.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA
Why does my MJ stall when hot?

Usually the crankshaft position sensor, which can fail when hot and test fine cold, or, on Renix trucks, the fuel-pump ballast resistor. Comanche Club members say to check the body grounds first for any strange electrical fault.

SOURCE · COMANCHE CLUB
How many are left?

AMC and Chrysler built 190,446. Comanche Club members got 2021 vehicle-in-operation figures from Key Parts showing about 18,600 registered in the US and Canada.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · COMANCHE CLUB

Full-Size Ford Bronco problems: 8 things to check

The problems owners and specialists flag most on the Ford Full-size Bronco, the repairs they end up doing and what those cost, plus the years and recalls to watch. Sourced, not scraped.

Full-Size Ford Bronco problems to check before you buy

  1. Rust in Ford's favorite placesBronco Zone's long-time checklist names the inner rear fender lips, the bottoms of the B-pillars and the bottom of the tailgate, where spot-welded seams trap moisture. Also check the radiator core support where it bolts to the frame (mostly the passenger side), the floor around the rear seat-belt mounts, rain gutters, body mounts and the frame itself.
  2. Run the tailgate glass both waysLower and raise the rear window from the key and from the dash switch, then open and close the tailgate. Check the tailgate drains and the outer glass seal. A window that works from only one control points to the circuit, not the motor. On many trucks the driver's window switch must be in the center position before the tailgate key switch will work.
  3. Read the tires before the TTBUneven inner or outer tread wear means camber trouble. Look at the radius-arm bushings, which perish (one Bronco Zone owner replaced his at 80,000 miles), and check the ball joints and tie-rod ends. If the truck is lifted, ask whether adjustable camber bushings were fitted, because Bronco Graveyard says even 1–2-inch lift springs normally need them.
  4. E4OD fluid and the O/D lightOn 1990–96 automatics, the fluid should be red, not brown or burnt-smelling, and read full at operating temperature. A flashing overdrive light (TCIL) while driving means the transmission has stored a fault code. Owners recommend fluid and filter service every 30,000 miles, and sooner for towing.
  5. Try 4-High and 4-LowOn push-button trucks, the shift motor and its connector are known trouble spots, often from a broken travel stop or a fouled connector. Ford recalled some 1993 trucks (93V-089) whose transfer cases had the wrong shift motor and could slip out of 4-High.
  6. Compression, cooling and leaksOwners call a bad compression or vacuum test a deal-breaker. The plastic side seams of the radiator leak, most visibly after the engine cools overnight. Check the rear main and timing-cover seals, look for a cracked exhaust manifold or Y-pipe, and check for water in the oil.
  7. Decide whether you need a removable top1978–93 tops were designed to come off. From 1994 the top carries the rear three-point belts and the center brake light and is held with security Torx bolts. You can still remove it, but Wikipedia notes that driving without it can earn a ticket in some places.
  8. Open recallsOld trucks still show up with open campaigns. The 1994–96 speed-control deactivation switch (05V-388) and its 1993 equivalents (07V-336 and 08V-051) can overheat and burn, and 1988–91 trucks had an ignition-switch short-circuit recall (96V-071). Check the VIN on NHTSA's site.

Full-Size Ford Bronco recalls and service bulletins

  • Speed-control deactivation switch may overheat or burn ↗ 1994–1996 · NHTSA 05V-388The switch could leak internally and overheat. Dealers first disconnected the speed control, then reconnected it once replacement parts were available in 2006.
  • Speed-control deactivation switch (1993 trucks) ↗ 1993 · NHTSA 07V-336 · 08V-051The same overheating-switch problem on 1993 Broncos and F-Series, covered in two later campaigns.
  • Ignition switch internal short circuit ↗ 1988–1991 · NHTSA 96V-071The ignition switch could short internally; dealers replaced it.
  • Transfer case built with the wrong shift motor ↗ 1993 · NHTSA 93V-089Electric-shift cases could slip out of 4-High when coasting or reversing; dealers replaced the transfer case.
  • Parking-brake self-adjuster pawl slips ↗ 1992–1994 · NHTSA 94V-169The pawl could skip teeth on the ratchet; dealers fitted a wedge to hold it in engagement.
  • Front bumper U-clip nuts can fracture ↗ 1993–1994 · NHTSA 94V-018The nuts that hold the front bumper to its inner arms could break.
  • Door latch release cable can freeze ↗ 1992 · NHTSA 92V-006Water in the cable could freeze in sub-zero weather and make the door latch malfunction.
  • Spring-lock fuel-line coupling may not be engaged ↗ 1986–1987 · NHTSA 87V-139An incompletely engaged coupling could leak fuel.
  • Fuel-rail burrs may damage O-rings ↗ 1985 · NHTSA 84V-162Burrs on the new EFI fuel rails could cut adjacent O-ring seals during assembly and cause fuel leaks.
  • Steering-arm knuckles improperly manufactured ↗ 1979 · NHTSA 79V-035A knuckle could fracture and let the tie rod disengage.
  • Steel wheels may crack at the rim ↗ 1978 · NHTSA 78V-023Some steel wheels were improperly manufactured and could crack without warning.

Check any VIN for open recalls at nhtsa.gov/recalls ↗, or see the recall tracker.

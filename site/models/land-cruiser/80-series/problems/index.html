80 Series Land Cruiser problems: 8 things to check

The problems owners and specialists flag most on the Toyota Land Cruiser 80 Series, the repairs they end up doing and what those cost, plus the years and recalls to watch. Sourced, not scraped.

From Hank’s garageHank Pantier, BoxyTrucks founder · owned an 80 Series →
Window regulators

The biggest problem I had on my 80 Series was the window regulators going out. I owned it about ten years ago, so by now most trucks will either have had theirs replaced or be junk. Run every window up and down on a test drive anyway.

Lockers

The lockers needed attention too: a leaking vacuum seal kept them from engaging. Once that seal was fixed they worked as they should.

Exercise your lockers

Engage the lockers every six months or so, on almost any boxy truck. Both of my locker problems, on the 80 Series and the G500, came from systems that weren’t used often enough.

80 Series Land Cruiser problems to check before you buy

  1. Pull the knuckle fill plugSlee's inspection list says to look for oil and caked grease on the outside of the front knuckles, a sign the inner axle seals failed a long time ago. Remove the square fill plug and sample the grease: if it's runny, diff oil is getting into the knuckle.
  2. Look for coolant at the headCheck the head-to-block joint for antifreeze weeping, ask whether the radiator has been replaced (a possible sign of past overheating), and look for deposits in the overflow bottle. Head gaskets are on every 80 buyer's list of common problems.
  3. Rust in the footwells and rear windowsGet under it and check the front footwells on both sides and the body around the rear windows, which buyers' guides single out (owners report the same), especially on trucks from salted-road states. Also check under the battery tray for corrosion, which Slee's list calls out.
  4. Cracks around the steering boxThe frame around the steering box is a known weak point, more so on trucks that have run 33s or bigger. Look for cracks and ask whether a reinforcement plate has been fitted.
  5. Test the lockers properlyIf it has factory lockers, engage them in low range on a loose surface and make sure they actually lock. Actuators that won't engage are a common fault, and an actuator rebuild usually fixes them.
  6. Know which year you're looking at1991–92 FJ80s have the 4.0 L 3F-E, a C-clip semi-float rear with drums and no factory lockers. From 1993 you get the 4.5 L 1FZ-FE, optional lockers and a full-float rear with discs on ABS trucks. The automatic type is printed on the driver's-door label: A442F on 1993–94 trucks, A343F on most 1995–97 trucks (some '95s kept the A442F).
  7. Check for accident repairsLate 80s had VIN stickers on almost every body panel, so check they're on the doors, hood and tailgate. Look for overspray, weld marks on the inner fenders and core support, filler (use a magnet) and pulling marks on the frame.
  8. Look behind the dashBuilt trucks often have a mess of accessory wiring. Pop the panel under the steering column, look for extra leads on the battery positive and check they're fused, and test every light and switch.

80 Series Land Cruiser recalls and service bulletins

  • Reese Class II trailer hitch (aftermarket equipment) ↗ 1991–1993 · NHTSA 93E023000The receiver weld on Reese hitch part no. 06070 can break and let a trailer separate; the remedy was a replacement hitch. NHTSA's database shows no Toyota-issued vehicle recalls for 1991–97 US Land Cruisers.

Owner questions about 80 Series Land Cruiser reliability

Is it reliable? What actually breaks?

It's a tough truck, but now it's a 30-year-old one. The usual list: head gaskets on the 1FZ-FE, leaking knuckle seals, wheel bearings, frame cracks around the steering box, worn starter contacts, the 'pesky heater hose,' power-steering leaks, power windows, fraying seat belts and cracked dashes. Toyota fitted larger brakes from October 1992, so the 1991–92 trucks stop noticeably worse.

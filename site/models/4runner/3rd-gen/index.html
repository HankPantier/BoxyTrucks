PART 01

Overview

Where it came from, why it matters, and how it changed year to year.

The story

For its first twelve years the 4Runner was, essentially, a Hilux pickup wearing a hat. The third generation, launched in late 1995 as a 1996 model, was the moment Toyota stopped bolting a lid onto a truck bed and built the thing as an SUV from the frame up. It got coil springs at all four corners, rack-and-pinion steering, a chassis shared with the Land Cruiser Prado (J90) and, for the first time on a 4Runner, an optional electric rear locker. Nearly three decades later it is the generation trail people lower their voices about — and the one sellers have noticed.

THE BREAK FROM THE HILUX

The first 4Runner arrived in 1983 as a Hilux with a removable fibreglass top; the second generation swapped that for a one-piece steel body but still rode on the pickup's bones. The N180 cut the cord. Wikipedia records an all-new body and chassis shared with the J90 Prado, a wheelbase 2.3 inches longer than before, coils all round, rack-and-pinion steering and dual airbags. MotorWeek's 1996 test noted towing jumping from 3,500 to 5,000 lb, and the 3.4-litre V6 rising to 183 hp and 217 lb-ft from the old 3.0's 150 hp.

It was not expensive, either. MotorWeek priced a two-wheel-drive four-cylinder base model at $19,908 and the 4WD SR5 V6 most people actually wanted at $25,568. Under the hood sat two engines borrowed from the brand-new Tacoma: the 150 hp 2.7-litre 3RZ-FE four and the 5VZ-FE V6. Gearboxes were a five-speed manual or four-speed automatic, and two-wheel drive was standard — so plenty of the 3rd gens for sale are not the trail trucks they look like. Check the badge, then check underneath.

FACELIFT, MULTI-MODE AND THE LOST LOCKER

The 1999 refresh brought a new nose with revised bumper and headlights, a reorganised centre stack and — the bit that matters — an optional Multi-Mode transfer case that could run full-time 4WD on slippery roads. Wikipedia records the rest: a 'fat lip' bumper that lengthened the front crush zone, multi-parabola headlamps, a new instrument panel with a digital odometer, and colour-keyed bumpers, flares and running boards on the Limited and the Highlander, a trim later renamed Sport Edition. The rear E-locker stayed on the options list.

Then came 2001. The final two years made VSC stability control standard, fitted the Multi-Mode transfer case (with its lockable centre differential) to every 4WD truck and added brake-based traction control — but, as Wikipedia and HotCars both record, they lost the optional rear E-locker that had been available from 1996 to 2000. The manual had already gone for 2000 and the four-cylinder followed for 2001, leaving a V6-automatic-only line-up. For a lot of buyers that makes the 1999–2000 Limited or SR5 with a locker the sweet spot, while purists still argue for the simpler pre-facelift trucks.

WHY THE TRAIL CROWD WORSHIPS IT

The electric rear locker was the headline. Wikipedia notes Toyota had offered an electric rear locker on the US Land Cruiser since 1993 — the 80 Series was the big brother in showrooms at the time — and the 3rd gen was the first 4Runner to get one. Combine that with a compact footprint, decent ground clearance and a 105.3-inch wheelbase, and you have a truck that fits trails a full-size SUV has to winch around.

Then there is the 5VZ-FE. It is not fast — MotorWeek clocked 9.5 seconds to 60 — but it is the sort of engine owners talk about in hundreds of thousands of miles. 8020 Automotive calls it non-interference, so a snapped timing belt usually means a tow rather than a rebuild, and SlashGear notes the 3rd gen consistently ranked among the SUVs most likely to pass 250,000 miles. Because it shares so much with the first-generation Tacoma, the aftermarket for suspension, armour and parts is enormous.

THE AFTERMATH

Production ended in August 2002 and the fourth generation reached US showrooms that October as a 2003 model, built on the bigger Prado 120 platform that also underpins the Lexus GX 470. It brought a V8 and more comfort, and a lot more mass. The N180, meanwhile, slid into the used market and became the default answer to 'what cheap 4x4 should I build?' — right up until it stopped being cheap. Its shared DNA with the first-generation Tacoma kept parts plentiful long after the dealer stopped caring.

Today the clean ones are collector items in all but name: Yahoo Autos reports unmodified examples under 100,000 miles mostly trading in the mid-to-upper $20,000s. The rest are rolling restoration projects held together by forum knowledge — lower ball joints, rear axle seals, frame rust and timing belts are the usual list. None of it is exotic, all of it is documented, and that, more than any spec sheet, is why the 3rd gen refuses to die.

SIDEBAR · THE JAPANESE TWIN

Hilux Surf: the diesel 3rd gen America never got

In Japan the N180 was sold as the Hilux Surf, and it came with something US buyers never got: diesel. Yahoo Autos notes the diesel options were Japan-only, and BE FORWARD lists Surfs like the KZN185 with the 1KZ-TE, a 3.0-litre four-cylinder turbodiesel making roughly 130 hp but far more low-end torque than the petrol V6.

Much of the chassis, suspension and brakes is shared with the US 4Runner, so the parts problem is smaller than it looks. And because the US 25-year import rule frees vehicles from federal safety standards once they reach that age, a right-hand-drive Surf is now a legal, if quirky, way to own the diesel 3rd gen Toyota never sold here.

How it’s built

Underneath, the N180 is old-school Toyota done properly: a ladder frame shared with the J90 Prado, double-wishbone independent front suspension, a solid coil-sprung rear axle, and Tacoma-derived engines tuned for longevity rather than lap times. The clever bits are in the drivetrain — an optional electric rear locker in the early years, and the Multi-Mode transfer case that arrived with the 1999 facelift.

What to check before you buy

  1. 01
    Frame rust, properlyCrawl under it. Forum members flag the crossmember-to-frame welds, the rear lower control arm mounts, the triangular trailing-arm brackets, the area behind the axle where the spare hangs, and the rocker ends. Probe with a screwdriver: a hole or two is fixable, a frame thin from end to end is not.
  2. 02
    Lower ball jointsThe stock LBJ is the 3rd gen's famous weak link — if it lets go the control arm drops and the wheel folds under. 2001–02 trucks were covered by Toyota safety recall 50J (NHTSA 05V-225), which replaced the lower ball joints; on any year, ask what was fitted. AdventureTaco's advice is OEM Toyota only.
  3. 03
    Timing belt recordsThe V6 wants a belt at roughly 90,000-mile intervals, ideally with the water pump and thermostat. It's a non-interference engine, so a failure is usually a tow, not a rebuild — but no paperwork means budget for the job.
  4. 04
    Rear axle seals and brakesBlown rear axle seals are a known 3rd gen habit; gear oil then soaks the drum brake shoes and washes out the wheel bearing. Look for wet backing plates and listen for a bearing growl on the test drive.
  5. 05
    Locker and 4WD functionIf it's a 1996–2000 4WD with the E-locker, make sure it actually engages (it's meant for 4Lo and low speed). On Multi-Mode trucks, cycle every mode. 2001–02 trucks have no factory rear locker regardless of what the ad says.
  6. 06
    Steering slopWandering or a clunk over bumps often points to worn steering rack bushings (common around 100k miles) or tie-rod ends. Both are cheap to fix but useful bargaining chips.
  7. 07
    Recalls and readiness1996–98 trucks are covered by NHTSA recall 02V-021 (Toyota campaign 20A), under which dealers replaced rear suspension parts; ask whether it was done. And plug in a scanner — IH8MUD members warn about sellers clearing codes to hide a dead catalytic converter, so check all readiness monitors are complete.
  8. 08
    Who lifted it?A tidy lift with known parts is a bonus; a mystery spacer lift with no alignment records is a project. Forum veterans' advice is blunt: skip a lifted truck unless the lift was done properly.

Spotting the real thing

Plenty of 3rd gens for sale are two-wheel-drive, four-cylinder or locker-less, and the locker is the option that sets prices.

  1. ID
    Model codesUS trucks are RZN180/185 (2.7 3RZ-FE four) or VZN180/185 (3.4 5VZ-FE V6). KZN185 and KDN185 are Japanese diesel Hilux Surfs.
  2. ID
    Rear e-lockerOptional on 4WD trucks from 1996 to 2000, never standard on any trim per a 1997 brochure cited by owners, and gone for 2001–02. Look for the dash switch, the actuator and wiring on the rear diff, and the factory locker placard. A locked rear diff also takes more oil: 2.75 L vs 2.45 L.
  3. ID
    Facelift tells1999–2000 trucks have the 'fat lip' bumper and multi-parabola headlamps; 2001–02 add clear tail lamps, a new grille and five-spoke wheels; 2002 adds chrome liftgate trim.
  4. ID
    Transfer case1996–98 4WD trucks use a part-time lever (Limiteds use a pushbutton for H4); Multi-Mode was optional from 1999 and standard on 4WD from 2001.

Importing & legality

Japan's Hilux Surf (N185) came with diesels never sold in the US: the 3.0 1KZ-TE turbo-diesel (1995–2000) and the common-rail 1KD-FTV (2000–02). Once a Surf is 25 years old it can be imported without meeting US federal safety standards. Per BE FORWARD, the first 2001 models crossed that line in 2026, so late 1KD-FTV trucks are only now becoming eligible. They are right-hand drive, and state titling and emissions rules still apply.

Rules change and vary by state and country. Confirm with your DMV and a licensed importer before you buy.

Common repairs

The jobs owners actually end up doing, rated for a competent home mechanic with a decent tool kit. Always confirm torque figures, fluids and procedures against the factory service manual for your VIN, and work safely — support the truck on stands, never just a jack. Lower ball joint replacement
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · ≈ 30–60 min per sideSHOP COST · Dealer did the 2001–02 campaign in about 3 hours; otherwise parts plus labor
SYMPTOMSStiff steering, poor self-centering, front-end clunks or squeaks, a torn boot. Toyota's 50J letter warns a worn joint can separate from the knuckle.
THE FIXSupport the truck on stands and pull the wheel. Remove the four 14 mm bolts holding the LBJ to the knuckle, pull the cotter pin and loosen the 24 mm castle nut, then pop the stud from the lower control arm with a pitman-arm puller (repeat for the 19 mm tie-rod castle nut on a 4Runner). Swing the hub aside, fit the correct left/right joint, torque the knuckle bolts with blue Loctite (37 ft-lb black washer bolts or 59 ft-lb green flanged bolts), castle nuts to 103 ft-lb (LCA) and 67 ft-lb (tie rod), then new cotter pins.

DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · ≈ 3–4 hoursSHOP COST · Parts plus labor; urethane kits are inexpensive
SYMPTOMSVague steering, wander and clunks; rack visibly shifting in its mounts. AdventureTaco treats about 1/16 in of play as normal and 1/4 in or more as time for new bushings.
THE FIXSupport the front, remove wheels and the front sway bar, and separate the outer tie-rod ends from the knuckles. 4WD won't engage: ADD vacuum system and actuators
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5 (diagnosis, hoses, VSV) · 3 / 5 (actuator rebuild)TIME · An hour or two to diagnose · an actuator rebuild needs a day for the glue to cureSHOP COST · Parts plus labor; vacuum hose is cheap
SYMPTOMSLever or button in 4WD but only the rear wheels drive, and the 4WD light stays off. On a part-time truck, a slow tight turn on pavement should make 4WD bind within about thirty feet. No bind means no front drive (Toyota-4Runner.org).
THE FIXOn 1996–2000 trucks the front axle is engaged by vacuum. With the engine running and 4WD off, check for vacuum at the twin VSVs on the driver's-side fender wall. No vacuum: check the check valve to the intake manifold, the hoses and the reservoir. If one hose has vacuum, engage 4WD and see whether it moves to the other. If it moves but the axle doesn't engage, suspect the diff actuator or its line. If it doesn't move, mark and swap the two hoses: if the truck now goes into 4WD the diff is fine, and a test light shows whether the transfer-case switch signal changes sides. Signal but no change in vacuum means a bad VSV. Check the fuse and the ADD relay too; one 1998 SR5 owner found it behind the passenger kick panel. 2001–02 trucks use an electric front actuator, so the vacuum tests don't apply. On push-button trucks the transfer-case actuator motor can quit when its magnets come loose. One owner rebuilt his (1996 Limited, A340F, actuator 36410-35040) by re-gluing the magnets and fitting a new O-ring.
TOOLS & PARTSHand vacuum pump with gauge · test light · marker · 12 mm and 10 mm sockets · dielectric grease
WHERE TO BUY PARTSVacuum hose assortment · Amazon ↗Hand vacuum pump with gauge · Amazon ↗VSVs, ADD and 4WD actuators (RockAuto catalogue) · RockAuto ↗
Rear hatch window drops or won't rise
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · An afternoonSHOP COST · Parts plus labor; owners hunt used relays and switches to save money
SYMPTOMSThe back glass drops into the hatch and won't come up, often in wet weather, or lowers itself unasked.
THE FIXStart with the fuse and the rear-door power-window relay. One 1997 SR5 owner on Toyota-4Runner.org found the relay shell melted and burned wires at the window limit switch, with the damage spread into neighbouring harness wires to the rear door ECU. Pull the hatch trim, repair the harness, then feed the motor 12 V directly, using the wiring diagram to pick the right leads. That tells you whether the motor or the controls are at fault. If the window drops by itself, another owner cured it by removing the short harness section between the hatch key lock and the hatch harness; after that the window works only from the dash switch. The thread gives the limit switch as Toyota 84290-35020.

Asked on every forum

Toyota 4Runner 3rd Gen questions, answered

What's the biggest tire I can fit without a lift?

The Toyota-4Runner.org consensus is 265/75R16 (roughly a 31.6-inch tire) on stock suspension. Some owners see rubbing at full compression off-road with wide, aggressive tread or wheel spacers; if you want zero drama, 265/70R16 or 265/70R17 are the conservative picks. Anything bigger is lift territory.

SOURCE · TOYOTA-4RUNNER.ORG
Does my 4Runner have the factory rear locker?

Only if it's a 1996–2000 4WD truck that was ordered with it — it was an option on Base, SR5 and Limited 4WD models, never standard, and it wasn't common. For 2001–02 the rear E-locker was dropped in favour of VSC, traction control and a centre diff lock.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA / T4R.ORG / HOTCARS
How should I use the E-locker?

The owner's manual guidance quoted on the forums is to engage it only in 4Lo and keep speed below about 5 mph. It locks both rear wheels together, so it'll fight you in turns on high-traction surfaces — use it for the obstacle, then switch it off.

SOURCE · TOYOTA-4RUNNER.ORG
When does the timing belt need doing — and is the 3.4 an interference engine?

Plan on about every 90,000 miles, and do the water pump, thermostat and seals while you're in there. The 5VZ-FE is non-interference, so a broken belt shouldn't destroy valves, but it will leave you stranded.

SOURCE · 8020 AUTOMOTIVE
How much can it tow?

Up to 5,000 lb when properly equipped — a big jump from the 3,500 lb of the previous generation. Owners on Expedition Portal report fuel economy dropping to roughly 11–13 mpg when towing.

SOURCE · MOTORWEEK / AUTO CHANNEL
What fuel economy should I expect?

EPA ratings were around 17 city / 19 highway for the 1996 V6 and 16/19 for a 2002 SR5 4x4; owners typically report 16–18 mpg. The 18.5-gallon tank and a notoriously inaccurate fuel gauge mean range is modest, so carry a jerry can on long trips.

SOURCE · MOTORWEEK / EXPEDITION PORTAL
What are the fluid capacities?

From Toyota's 1999–2002 owner's manuals: 5VZ-FE oil 5.2 L with filter (5W-30 preferred); coolant 8.0 L (9.0 L with rear heater); automatic drain-and-refill up to 2.0 L (4WD) of Dexron III/II; transfer case 1.0–1.2 L of GL-4/5 75W-90; front diff 1.15 L, where the manual gives 75W-90 for A.D.D. trucks and Toyota-Club gives 80W-90; rear diff 2.45 L (2.75 L with diff lock) of GL-5 SAE 90, or 80W-90 below −18 °C.

SOURCE · TOYOTA OWNER'S MANUAL · TOYOTA-CLUB.NET
Which years should I buy or avoid?

Forum favourites are 1999–2000 trucks, especially Limiteds or SR5s with the Multi-Mode case and E-locker. 1996–98 trucks are simpler and cheaper to fix. 2001–02 get VSC and traction control but lose the locker and manual, and some owners prefer the simpler earlier drivetrain. Condition and rust matter far more than model year.

SOURCE · TOYOTA-4RUNNER.ORG
Is the four-cylinder worth considering?

Most owners steer you toward the 3.4 V6: 183 hp and 217 lb-ft versus 150 hp and 177 lb-ft for the 2.7 four, for similar running costs. The four is a durable engine, but it'll struggle with big tires and a loaded roof rack.

SOURCE · IH8MUD / SLASHGEAR
What lift options are there?

The classic is an Old Man Emu kit of about 2–3 in, with ICON, Total Chaos and others offering springs, coilovers and control arms. As a lift grows, aftermarket upper control arms help restore alignment range; Total Chaos makes both UCAs and a bolt-on lower ball joint conversion for this truck.

SOURCE · LCE PERFORMANCE / OVR MAG
How long will a 3rd gen last?

With maintenance, a long time. SlashGear notes it consistently ranked among the SUVs most likely to pass 250,000 miles, and 8020 Automotive puts a well-maintained 5VZ-FE at 300,000–400,000 miles. Frame rust and neglected ball joints usually kill these trucks before the engine does.

SOURCE · SLASHGEAR / 8020

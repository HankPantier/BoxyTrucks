PART 01

Overview

Where it came from, why it matters, and how it changed year to year.

The story

The first 4Runner was less designed than improvised: take Toyota's redesigned 1984 Hilux 4x4 pickup, give the bed a fibreglass lid and a back seat (sometimes), and ship it to America. It went on sale in the US in May 1984 as a 1984½ model — two doors, a four-cylinder, a five-speed and, for its first two seasons, a solid front axle on leaf springs. Four decades on it is the 4Runner people call 'pure', and the one Hagerty says can't be ignored now that clean examples keep getting more expensive.

A PICKUP WITH A HAT

Toyota had already seen the idea work. Between 1981 and 1983 Winnebago and Toyota turned short-bed Hilux 4x4s into the Trekker — a fibreglass-lined bed, an extra seat and a fibreglass top — and Curbside Classic counts only around 1,500 of them. The factory version arrived as the Hilux Surf in Japan in October 1983 and as the 4Runner in North America a few months later, just as the FJ40 Land Cruiser left the US market and the small-SUV class filled up with the S-10 Blazer, Bronco II and Jeep Cherokee.

Hagerty describes the details that make owners grin: a rear roof held on by 14 bolts, a standard rear roll bar, and a power rear window that drops into the tailgate. SR5s added an altimeter and inclinometer on the dash, a rear seat, strobe-stripe graphics and chrome wheels; the base DX often came without a rear seat at all, and Toyota never fitted rear shoulder belts.

ENGINES, AND THE AXLE ARGUMENT

The 1984½ trucks used the carburetted 2.4-litre 22R making 96 hp, paired only with a five-speed. Fuel injection arrived for 1985 as the 22R-E — Wikipedia lists 116 hp — and 1986 brought the 135 hp 22R-TE turbo, mostly sold with the automatic in SR5s and far rarer than the plain 22R-E. For 1988 the 3.0-litre 3VZ-E V6 joined the range, rated at 145–150 hp depending on the source. Hagerty's verdict on the whole run: 0–60 times improved 'from months to weeks to about 12 seconds'.

The bigger change came underneath. For 1986 the solid front axle was replaced by independent front suspension with a three-inch-wider track, which made the truck calmer on the road and, by most accounts, a little less heroic off it. That split defines the generation today: 1984–85 trucks are the solid-axle purist's choice, while 1986–89 trucks ride better and share their front end with the second generation. Both keep a leaf-sprung rear axle that Wikipedia notes was never upgraded for the extra weight of the seats and top — so sagging is normal.

WHY IT MATTERS NOW

In its own time the N60 was a slow starter: US sales were 6,498 in 1984 and dipped below 4,000 in 1987, before jumping to 20,880 in 1988 and 36,927 in calendar 1989 (a figure that includes the first 2nd gens). Today the story has flipped. Hagerty reported average values of $8,400 in #3 condition, $13,700 in #2 and $21,100 in #1, with Bring a Trailer sales of a 100,000-mile 1984 SR5 at $19,250, and found that millennials and Gen-Xers made up a far larger share of 4Runner insurance quotes than of the market overall.

The Autopian's David Tracy goes further, naming the 1985 — fuel-injected, solid-axled, convertible-topped and under 3,500 lb — his perfect 4Runner. The catch is survival. Open tops let water in, floors and quarter panels are not reproduced, and Hagerty notes that original seats and upholstery are close to unobtainable. Mechanically, though, the 22R-family engines are among the easiest Toyota ever built, and LC Engineering still runs a whole 1984–1989 4Runner catalogue.

THE AFTERMATH

For 1990 Toyota replaced it with a four-door, fixed-roof second generation that kept the independent front end and swapped the rear leafs for coils. The removable top never returned, and by the third generation the pickup-with-a-lid idea had vanished entirely. The first gen became something else: the cheap, simple base for solid-axle swaps, V8 conversions and camper builds — Expedition Portal's classifieds once listed a 1985 SR5 on Old Man Emu springs with a FlipPac pop-up roof for $8,000. Nobody is building them that way from new any more, which is rather the point.

SIDEBAR · BEFORE THE 4RUNNER

The Trekker: Winnebago's 4Runner prototype you could buy

Before Toyota built its own 4Runner, Winnebago did a version for it. Curbside Classic describes how short-bed Toyota 4x4 pickups were sent to Winnebago, which lined the bed with fibreglass, added a seat and fitted a fibreglass top; 'only some 1500 Trekkers were made and sold, between 1981 and 1983'.

Its true origins are hazy, and Curbside Classic found no evidence that the Trekker's top was meant to come off. But the concept — Hilux 4x4 plus enclosed back with seats — is exactly what Toyota put into production for 1984. LC Engineering still lists the 1981–1983 Trekker as its own model in its parts catalogue.

Specs & capability

How it’s built, what it can do, and what fits under it.

How it’s built

Mechanically the N60 is a Toyota Hilux 4x4 pickup, nearly part-for-part. That means a ladder frame, a leaf-sprung live rear axle, a part-time transfer case and an engine bay designed for the simple 22R family. The interesting choices are the 1984–85 solid front axle with manual locking hubs, the switch to torsion-bar independent front suspension for 1986, and a removable fibreglass rear roof.

What to check before you buy

  1. 01
    Rust, everywhereForum owners point to the frame ahead of the rear spring perches, the rear quarters and wheel wells, the left rear near the exhaust exit, and, on sunroof-equipped trucks, A-pillars that owners say rotted from blocked drains. Hagerty warns that floors and quarter panels aren't reproduced, so a rusty body is a fabrication job, not a parts order.
  2. 02
    Know which axle you're buying1984–85 trucks have the solid front axle; 1986–89 have IFS. Solid-axle trucks carry a price premium and some sellers stretch the truth, so look underneath: leaf springs and manual hubs up front mean solid axle; torsion bars and CV axles mean IFS.
  3. 03
    22R-E timing chain guidesThe plastic timing-chain guides are a known breakage item — LC Engineering makes metal-backed replacements specifically for it. A cold-start rattle from the front of the engine, or no record of the job, means budget for chain, guides and tensioner.
  4. 04
    V6 head gasketsThe 1988–89 3VZ-E shares the V6's head-gasket reputation. Toyota's October 1996 policy adjustment (1988–94 V6 trucks) is long expired and the later V06 campaign only covered 1/90–10/94 builds, so check for coolant loss, overheating history and white exhaust, and ask for paperwork on any head work.
  5. 05
    Sagging rear and tired suspensionRear leafs sag on nearly all of them. Feathered front tyres can mean worn front-end parts or a bad alignment; on IFS trucks check idler arm and tie-rod play.
  6. 06
    Top, glass and sealsRemovable tops and ageing seals leak; a tarp over the roof in the ad is a clue. Check the power tailgate window works — IH8MUD members flag it as a weak point on both early generations.
  7. 07
    Interior partsCarpets can be found, but Hagerty says reproduction seats and upholstery generally can't, and owners say good seats are especially scarce. An intact original interior is worth paying for.
  8. 08
    Rear seats and beltsMany 1984–86 base trucks came without a rear seat, and Toyota never fitted rear shoulder belts. If you plan to carry kids, factor that in.

Spotting the real thing

The 1984½–89 trucks look alike but split hard on axle and engine, and the front half is pure Hilux pickup, so check the hardware rather than the badges.

  1. ID
    Solid axle or IFS1984–85 trucks have a solid front axle on leaf springs; 1986–89 have independent front suspension with a 3 in wider track. Look under the front: a beam axle and leaf packs vs torsion bars and CV shafts.
  2. ID
    Engine by year1984½ is carbureted 22R only; EFI 22R-E from 1985; the 22R-TE turbo only for 1986–87, mostly with the automatic; the 3VZ-E V6 for 1988–89 on SR5.
  3. ID
    Rear seat and trimMany 1984–86 trucks came in without a rear seat (base DX omitted it), partly to classify them as trucks for import duty. The SR5 package added gauges, better trim and the rear seat.
  4. ID
    Hilux Surf twinsJapan's Hilux Surf used 3Y petrol and 2L / 2L-T diesel engines that were never sold in the US; a diesel or right-hand-drive 'first gen' is a gray import, not a US 4Runner.

Importing & legality

Japan's version was the Hilux Surf (N60), offered with a 2.0 3Y petrol or 2.4 2L diesel and later the 2L-T turbo-diesel, none of them sold in the US. Every one is now well past the US 25-year exemption from federal safety standards, so diesel and right-hand-drive Surfs can come in, but state titling and emissions rules still apply and vary.

SYMPTOMSFront hubs that won't lock, slip under load or let go in reverse. 4Runners.com notes the auto hubs fitted to some 1984–89 4Runners are less reliable than manual hubs and will sometimes unlock in reverse.
THE FIXConfirm you have auto hubs (no dial to turn on the hub cap). Remove the auto hub assemblies and the auto-hub hardware on the spindle. Fit LC Engineering's conversion kit: the locking nuts and plates that hold the hub and rotor on the spindle once the auto-hub parts are gone. Repack the wheel bearings and set the preload to the factory manual while it's apart, then fit the manual hubs on new gaskets. Solid-axle (1984–85) and IFS (1986–89) hubs have different spline counts, so order the right ones.
TOOLS & PARTSSockets · spindle-nut socket · snap-ring pliers · torque wrench · wheel-bearing grease and new seals

Power tailgate window that sticks or drops →
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · An afternoonSHOP COST · Parts plus labor; one Toyota-4Runner.org member recommends a used motor
SYMPTOMSThe roll-down rear glass works only some of the time, stalls part-way or suddenly drops into the tailgate. IH8MUD members flag the electric rear window as a weak point on both early generations; Toyota-4Runner.org owners trace it to tired motors, regulator gears and drag in the channels.
THE FIXPull the tailgate's inner access panels. Run the glass up and down and check it stays in its tracks, then push and twist the regulator to see whether its gears stay meshed. If the regulator is sound, test the motor directly on a battery, then check the relay. Clean the channels and put silicone spray on the glass edges and rubber seals; one owner says a dealer clean-and-lube cured his. Toyota-4Runner.org members describe the motors as easily overwhelmed by drag, and one says 1st and 2nd gen motors are the same apart from the connector plug. If the glass has dropped and broken before, check the regulator was actually fixed, not just the glass.

Asked on every forum

Toyota 4Runner 1st Gen questions, answered

What's the biggest tire I can fit without a lift?

Owners' consensus is 31x10.50R15 — Toyota-4Runner.org members say 31s fit with no lift and you can get away without regearing. 33x12.50s on a stock truck rub the front valance, wheel-well corners and rear pinch weld; IH8MUD owners estimate about 1.5 in of lift (plus some trimming) for 33s, and say the 22R-E will want lower gears.

SOURCE · T4R.ORG / IH8MUD
Which years have the solid front axle?

1984 and 1985. Toyota switched to independent front suspension with a three-inch-wider track for 1986, so 1986–89 trucks are IFS. Solid-axle trucks are the off-roader's favourite; IFS trucks ride better on the road.

SOURCE · HAGERTY / WIKIPEDIA
22R-E or V6?

Most enthusiasts prefer the 22R-E four-cylinder — Hagerty says so, and Expedition Portal members advise skipping the V6. The 3.0 V6 (1988–89) has more power but carries the 3VZ-E's head-gasket reputation and is harder to work on; the 22R-E is slow but famously simple.

SOURCE · HAGERTY / EXPEDITION PORTAL
Can I take the roof off?

Yes. The rear fibreglass top is held on by 14 bolts, and a rear roll bar was standard. Owners note the open-top experience mostly benefits rear passengers — and that ageing tops are a common leak point.

SOURCE · HAGERTY / TACOMAWORLD
Was there a diesel 4Runner?

Not in the US. Diesel engines were offered in Hilux pickups, but Wikipedia records that no diesel 4Runners were imported to the US.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA
What about the turbo?

The 22R-TE turbo four arrived for 1986 with 135 hp, mostly paired with the four-speed automatic in SR5s. It's much rarer than the 22R-E, and LC Engineering still sells dedicated 22R-TE short blocks and turbo parts.

SOURCE · HAGERTY / LCE
What fuel economy should I expect?

Not much. Wikipedia records an EPA city rating of 17 mpg for the 22R-TE turbo truck and 14.5 mpg in period testing; Trail4Runner quotes 19 city / 23 highway for a stock four-cylinder, which looks optimistic. Expect less with bigger tyres, a lift or a tired engine.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA / TRAIL4RUNNER
Is a 1988–89 V6 covered by Toyota's head gasket campaign?

Not any more. An October 1996 Special Policy Adjustment extended head-gasket coverage for 1988–1994 V6 trucks; the later V06 campaign covered 1/90–10/94 production and expired on 24 September 2018. Today a blown 3VZ-E head gasket is on you.

SOURCE · CARCOMPLAINTS (V06 TSB)
What's a first gen worth?

Hagerty's write-up on rising 1984–89 values gave averages of $8,400 (#3 Good), $13,700 (#2 Excellent) and $21,100 (#1 Concours), and cited Bring a Trailer sales of $19,250 for a 100,000-mile 1984 SR5 and $17,500 for a 168,000-mile 1986 SR5. Expedition Portal members reckoned a maintained 22R-E truck under about 170,000 miles could still be found for under $5,000.

SOURCE · HAGERTY
Should I solid-axle swap an IFS truck?

IH8MUD members are split. Some say the 1986–95 Toyota IFS is 'pretty stout' unless you rock-crawl hard; others who broke IFS shafts say to 'spend the money on a SAS and never look back'. Kits for 1979–85-style solid axles, long-travel IFS and stronger axles are all still sold.

SOURCE · IH8MUD
Can I still get parts?

Mechanical parts, yes — the 22R family is shared with Toyota pickups and LC Engineering runs a dedicated 1984–1989 4Runner catalogue. Body panels, floors and interior trim are the hard part; Hagerty notes reproduction seats and floors generally don't exist.

SOURCE · HAGERTY / LCE
Which years are the best to buy?

For a trail truck, a 1985: it's the last year of the solid front axle that Hagerty says purists still seek out, and it gets the fuel-injected 22R-E — an Expedition Portal member rates carbureted trucks less desirable. For a daily driver, several of the same forum's members point to a 1986–89 IFS truck with the 22R-E and a manual. Skip the 1988–89 V6: Hagerty's expert says most enthusiasts avoid it in favour of the four. Rust decides more than the model year does.

SOURCE · HAGERTY · EXPEDITION PORTAL

PART 01

Overview

Where it came from, why it matters, and how it changed year to year.

The story

By 1969 Ford had the Bronco, International had the Scout and Jeep had the CJ-5, and General Motors had nothing in the small, go-anywhere class. Chevrolet's answer was not small at all. The K5 Blazer took the half-ton K10 4x4 pickup, cut the wheelbase from 115 to 104 inches, and gave it a removable top. Wikipedia credits that pickup basis with three advantages over the rivals: more cargo room, a better on-road ride from the wider track and longer wheelbase, and lower cost, because most of the parts were already on a production line.

A PICKUP WITH THE BACK CUT OFF

MotorTrend's history of the K5 puts it bluntly: Ford had the Bronco, Jeep had the CJ, even a tractor company had the Scout, and GM had nothing. Basing the Blazer on the short-box K10 let Chevrolet reach the market quickly and with more capability than the competition. The base truck was spartan to the point of comedy. MotorTrend notes that early base Blazers came without a roof or a passenger seat, so the driver had the only seat in the house.

Underneath it was all 1969 Chevy truck: a ladder frame, tapered leaf springs at both ends, a Dana 44 front axle and a GM 12-bolt rear. Chevrolet's 1969 spec sheet lists 8 inches of ground clearance, a 35-degree approach angle, a 25-degree departure angle, a 23.5-gallon tank and a 5,000 lb maximum GVW. Engines matched the pickups: the 250 six as standard, a 307 V8, and the 350 V8, which came only with the four-speed SM465 or the Turbo Hydra-Matic 350.

THE TOP COMES OFF

The first-generation Blazer is the only K5 whose whole roof comes off. MotorTrend describes a completely removable hardtop that left only the windshield sticking up. Chevrolet's 1971 brochure describes the auxiliary top as reinforced fiberglass with textured paint in black or white, with side rear windows and a lift tailgate, and says it 'bolts on' and 'lifts off quickly.' A canvas soft top on steel framing was a dealer-installed accessory. Chevrolet claimed it went up or down in under two minutes.

The 1973–75 Blazers on the new square-body chassis kept a fully removable top. From 1976 a fixed steel roof covered the front seats and only the rear section came off. If you want to drive with nothing above you but sky and the windshield header, the 1969–72 trucks are where the K5 story begins. They're also the shortest K5s, at 177.5 to 180 inches against 184.8 for the 1973 truck, which helps on tight trails.

THE 1971–72 TRUCKS

The changes came fast. For 1970 Chevrolet added a two-wheel-drive Blazer with coil-spring independent front suspension, and GMC got its own version, the Jimmy. For 1971, power front disc brakes became standard across GM's light trucks, replacing four-wheel drums. The 1971 truck also got an egg-crate grille and grew to 180 inches long. That year's brochure pushed the CST package: bucket seats, a console, chrome bumpers, extra insulation and deep-twist carpet.

For 1972 the 292 six disappeared from the option list, and the 350 was rated at 175 net horsepower. Then sales took off. CK5's model-year figures show production going from 4,935 in 1969 to 44,266 in 1972, matching MotorTrend's claim that sales grew roughly tenfold over the first generation. In April 1970 Car and Driver wrote, and Chevrolet happily quoted in its 1971 brochure, that someone had finally discarded the idea that four-wheel-drive vehicles could only be operated by ex-tank commanders and veteran truck drivers.

WHY IT MATTERS

Wikipedia says the Blazer had overtaken its rivals by 1970. Dodge answered with the Ramcharger and Jeep with the SJ Cherokee for 1974, and in 1978 Ford moved the Bronco onto a shortened F-100 platform specifically to fight the K5. The truck-based SUV recipe that the later K5, the Tahoe and nearly every full-size SUV since have followed starts with these four model years.

Today the early Blazer and Jimmy are serious collector trucks. Recent Bring a Trailer results for original-spec examples run from the mid-$20,000s to $100,000, with resto-mods higher still. Parts are easy because so much is shared with 1967–72 Chevy and GMC pickups, and reproduction sheet metal and seals are plentiful. Rust and tired frames are where the money goes, so buy the best body you can afford and fix the drivetrain later.

SIDEBAR · THE GMC VERSION

GMC Jimmy: same truck, different badge

GMC got the truck for the 1970 model year and called it the Jimmy, a long-standing nickname for GMC itself. MotorTrend says the two were essentially identical apart from a few styling tweaks.

GMC's 1971 brochure lists four series: CE-1550 and CS-1550 for two-wheel drive, and KE-1550 and KS-1550 for 4x4. The 4x4s were rated at 4,600–5,000 lb GVW and used the same engines as the Chevrolet: the 250 six and the 307 and 350 V8s. Tires ran from E78-15B as standard up to 10-16.5 on the 4x4s. On Bring a Trailer the Jimmy is the cheaper way into a first-gen truck. Original Jimmys have sold for about $24,000–72,000 since 2025.

PART 02

Specs & capability

How it’s built, what it can do, and what fits under it.

How it’s built

The first K5 is a 1969–72 K10 half-ton 4x4 shortened to a 104-inch wheelbase. It has a ladder frame, tapered leaf springs at all four corners, a Dana 44 front axle and a GM 12-bolt semi-floating rear. The engine drives through a three- or four-speed manual or a TH350 automatic to a two-speed transfer case, which is either a Dana 20 or a New Process 205. The open body takes a full-length fiberglass hardtop or a canvas soft top.

Buying one

What to pay, what to check, and how to tell a good one from a clone.

What they cost

Prices are firm and rising. Unrusted original trucks, CST and Cheyenne trim, the V8 and four-speed command the most, and resto-mods with modern engines sell for more again.

What to check before you buy

  1. 01
    Rust first, drivetrain secondVelocity Restorations lists the floorboards, wheel wells, inner fenders and door bottoms as the usual rust spots. The Drive adds the top of the windshield frame. Fresh paint can hide all of it, so bring a magnet and look underneath.
  2. 02
    Inspect the frame at the steering boxOffroad Design says straight-axle GM trucks are notorious for frame cracks where the steering box mounts, especially with lifts, big tires and off-road use. Look for cracks and old weld repairs on the driver's-side rail ahead of the front axle.
  3. 03
    Is the top there, and does it fit?Many first-gen trucks have lost their hardtop or soft top. The Drive notes that the fiberglass top's textured finish is hard to match in repairs. Check the top's mounting edge, the liftgate and the seals, because a leaky or missing top is expensive to sort out.
  4. 04
    Work out what has been swappedRecent BaT results include LS, Vortec, 383, 454 and 6.2 L swaps alongside original trucks. Velocity advises caution unless swap work is documented and done by a qualified builder. Decide whether you're paying for originality or for the build.
  5. 05
    Read the SPID decalFactory equipment and paint are listed on the Service Parts Identification decal inside the glovebox, per a 1969 BaT listing. Use it to check a claimed CST, Positraction or V8 truck.
  6. 06
    Expect a loose chassisThe Drive warns that the chassis is flexible and the steering has play, and that a big power upgrade can make the handling worse. Budget for steering-box, tie-rod and bushing work, and drive it before you agree a price.
  7. 07
    Check which transfer case and hubs it hasManual trucks may have the Dana 20 or the NP205. Automatic trucks have the NP205. Offroad Design notes the NP205's tag sits above the front output and that the case is one piece of cast iron. Make sure the hubs lock and unlock.
  8. 08
    Mind the brakes on 1969–70 trucksFront discs became standard for 1971. Earlier trucks have four-wheel drums unless they've been converted, so ask what has been changed and how well it was done.

Spotting the real thing

Four model years, a GMC twin and decades of engine swaps and reproduction parts mean the details matter, especially on high-dollar 'original' trucks.

  1. ID
    First gen vs square body1969–72 trucks use the Action Line pickup front end and body on a 104 in wheelbase. 1973–75 square bodies also have a full removable top, but sit on a 106.5 in wheelbase with the new 1973 sheet metal.
  2. ID
    Year by grillePer CK5: 1969 has an aluminum center piece embossed CHEVROLET with built-in rectangular parking lamps; 1970 has six groups of silver 'dashes'; 1971 has an egg-crate grille; on 1972 the grille surround is no longer black. Grilles are easy to swap, so cross-check the title year and VIN.
  3. ID
    SPID decalThe Service Parts Identification decal inside the glovebox lists factory options and paint (BaT 1969 listing). Use it to check CST trim, Positraction and engine claims.
  4. ID
    GMC Jimmy series codesGMC's 1971 brochure lists the Jimmy as CE-1550 and CS-1550 (two-wheel drive) and KE-1550 and KS-1550 (4x4).
  5. ID
    Transfer case and brakesManual trucks may have a Dana 20 or an NP205; automatics have the NP205 (one-piece iron case, three-bolt idler cover, tag above the front output). Front discs are original only on 1971–72 trucks.

Asked on every forum

Chevrolet K5 Blazer (1st generation) questions, answered

What's the biggest tire I can fit without a lift?

On a 67-72 Chevy Trucks thread about a stock 1972 Blazer, several owners run 33x12.50s on 15x8 wheels. Others call 32x11.50 the limit if the springs have sagged. One owner found 33x14.50s rubbed the springs, so width matters as much as height. On CK5, a member calls 33s about 2 inches bigger than stock 31s, fine for a while on the road but likely to hit off-road.

SOURCE · 67-72CHEVYTRUCKS · CK5
What's the difference between a K5 Blazer and a GMC Jimmy?

Badging and trim. GMC started selling the Jimmy for 1970, and MotorTrend calls the two essentially identical apart from a few styling tweaks. GMC's 1971 brochure lists 4x4 Jimmys as KE-1550 and KS-1550, with the same 250, 307 and 350 engines as the Chevrolet.

SOURCE · MOTORTREND · GMC BROCHURE
Does the whole roof really come off?

Yes. On 1969–72 trucks the hardtop is a full-length fiberglass shell that bolts on and lifts off, leaving just the windshield. A canvas soft top was a dealer accessory. From 1976 the K5 had a fixed roof over the front seats and only the rear section came off.

SOURCE · CHEVROLET BROCHURES · MOTORTREND
Which engines did it come with?

The 250 six was standard, with the 307 and 350 V8s optional. A 292 six is listed for 1970–71. Chevrolet's 1971 brochure rates the 250, 307 and 350 at 110, 135 and 170 net horsepower (145, 200 and 250 gross). CK5 lists 175 hp for the 1972 350.

SOURCE · CHEVROLET 1971 BROCHURE · CK5
Which transfer case do I have?

The Dana 20 came only with manual transmissions. The NP205 was fitted with the automatic and offered with manuals too. Offroad Design says the 205 is one piece of cast iron, with a small three-bolt idler cover on the back and an ID tag above the front output.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · OFFROAD DESIGN
When did it get disc brakes?

For 1971, when power front disc brakes became standard across GM's light trucks. 1969–70 trucks have drums at all four corners unless they've been converted.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · CHEVROLET 1971 BROCHURE
Which years are the best to buy?

There's no buyer consensus on a best year. In a CK5 thread on which K5 to buy, the owners who pick a first-generation truck do it for the looks and the full-length removable top, and one calls 1971–72 the best looking. The mechanical difference worth knowing is the brakes: 1971 and later trucks have power front discs, while 1969–70 trucks have drums all round unless they've been converted. After that, rust, the top and the quality of past work decide it.

SOURCE · CK5 FORUMS · WIKIPEDIA
Were there two-wheel-drive Blazers?

Yes. Wikipedia says the 2WD version was added for 1970, and CK5 says fewer than 1,000 were built that year. It used coil-spring independent front suspension and coil springs with trailing arms at the rear, so check for a front driveshaft before you assume a truck is a 4x4.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · CK5
How many were built?

CK5's spec sheet gives 4,935 for 1969, 11,527 for 1970, 17,220 for 1971 and 44,266 for 1972. Wikipedia lists the same numbers but flags them as uncited.

SOURCE · CK5 · WIKIPEDIA
What axle gears did it have, and should I regear for bigger tires?

The 1971 spec sheet lists 3.73 gears as standard on the 4x4, with 3.07 optional on V8 trucks. Owners on CK5 warn that 33s feel big with 3.08s. Offroad Design generally recommends gearing a lifted truck deeper to keep the engine in its useful rev range.

SOURCE · CHEVROLET 1971 BROCHURE · CK5 · ORD
What's the wheel bolt pattern?

Six lugs on 5.5 inches, shared with 1967–87 half-ton GM 4x4s. That makes period wheels and later GM 6-lug wheels easy to find, but check backspacing before buying.

SOURCE · CRAWLPEDIA
What does one cost now?

On Bring a Trailer, original-spec first-gen Blazers and Jimmys sold for about $24,000–100,000 between March 2025 and October 2026, with a median a little under $50,000. Resto-mods with modern engines have reached $146,000–170,000.

Range Rover Classic

100-IN / 108-IN LWBROVER V8 3.5 · 3.9 · 4.2PERMANENT 4WDCOIL OR AIR (1993+)

“The original luxury 4x4, with the soul of a farm truck. Buy the rust-free one and you'll forgive it everything — buy the shiny rusty one and it'll teach you to weld.”

The story

In June 1970, Rover launched a two-door wagon with a 100-inch wheelbase, long-travel coil springs, permanent four-wheel drive, disc brakes at every corner and an all-alloy 3.5-litre V8 — and called it "A Car For All Reasons." Nobody had built anything quite like it. It would cross a ploughed field at lunchtime and the M1 at teatime, and it would go on doing so for 26 years and 326,070 examples. The Range Rover Classic is the reason "luxury SUV" is a phrase. Happily for us, under the walnut it is still a ladder frame, two beam axles and a V8 you can fix with spanners.

A WAGON IN DISGUISE

The project began in 1967 as the "100-inch Station Wagon", led by Charles Spencer "Spen" King with Gordon Bashford, with David Bache shaping the body. Prototypes wore "Velar" badges — from the Latin for "to veil" — to keep the press guessing. The recipe was radical for a Land Rover: coil springs rather than leaf springs, a Buick-derived all-alloy V8, and aluminium body panels hung on a steel frame. It was so well resolved visually that in 1971 it became the first car exhibited at the Louvre in Paris, praised as an exemplary work of industrial design.

For its first 11 years the Range Rover was a two-door only, with a rubber-and-vinyl interior. The four-door finally arrived in July 1981, and two-door UK sales ended in 1984, although two-door production carried on until January 1994, mostly for France. Through the 1980s the truck crept upmarket: Lucas electronic fuel injection replaced carburettors in 1986, the ZF four-speed automatic joined the range, and the luxury Vogue badge became the template for everything that followed.

AMERICA, LONG WHEELBASES AND AIR

A US launch was first pencilled in for mid-1971 at $5,300, but federal regulations and British Leyland's finances killed it. Grey-market importers (including Aston Martin's US arm) filled the gap until factory-built US trucks finally landed on 16 March 1987, officially introduced at the Los Angeles Auto Show. Only four-doors came over, through Baltimore and Long Beach, and in 1988 Playboy named it the "Best 4x4" — which tells you exactly which crowd the marketing department was chasing.

The engine grew with the image: 3.9 litres for 1989–90, and 4.2 litres in 1992 for the new long-wheelbase model, sold as the LSE in Britain and the County LWB in the US. That truck stretched the wheelbase to 108 inches and introduced electronic air suspension, which reached customers in late 1992 for the 1993 model year. The last big change was the "soft dash" of the 1995 model year, which replaced the original hard-plastic dashboard owners nicknamed the "Airfix dash" and borrowed its steering wheel from the Discovery.

WHAT MADE IT SPECIAL

The Classic's party trick was articulation with comfort. Long-travel coils on beam axles let it flex like a farm truck yet ride like a saloon, and the permanent 4WD with a dual-range transfer box meant there was no lever-pulling panic when the mud arrived. Early trucks used the LT95 four-speed with an integral transfer box; from 1983 the five-speed LT77 got a separate LT230 transfer case, and from 1989 a chain-driven Borg Warner unit with a viscous coupling replaced the manually locked centre differential. It won the 4WD class of the Paris–Dakar in 1979 and 1981, and in 1987 a Turbo D set speed and endurance records, including 24 hours at over 100 mph.

The engineering also trickled down. The coil-sprung, permanent-4WD layout was carried into the One Ten and Ninety — later the Defender 90/110 — which also borrowed the Range Rover's 3.5 V8 and, initially, its LT95 gearbox. And in 1989 Land Rover built the Discovery on the Range Rover's 100-inch chassis with a cheaper interior and a smaller diesel. Every boxy Land Rover of the next three decades owes something to Spen King's wagon.

THE LONG GOODBYE

The successor, internally P38A, was unveiled in September 1994 as the "New Range Rover" — and the old truck refused to leave. Renamed the "Range Rover Classic", it stayed in production alongside the P38A until 1996, with the 1995 County and County LWB marking the final US model year. Today the Classic sits in the odd spot of being both a collector car and a genuinely useful trail truck. Hagerty notes prices rose almost tenfold in the decade to 2020, with Velar prototypes and early two-doors at the top. But the bones that matter — frame, axles, coils, V8 — remain refreshingly agricultural, and that is exactly why it still belongs on a trail.

SIDEBAR · 1971–72 EXPEDITION

Two Range Rovers vs. the Darién Gap

Barely a year after launch, Rover lent two Range Rovers to the British Trans-Americas Expedition, led by John Blashford-Snell, to drive the Pan-American Highway from Alaska to Tierra del Fuego — roughly 18,000 miles, including the roadless Darién Gap between Panama and Colombia. The trucks carried capstan winches, double front bumpers, roof racks and bridging ladders as long as the trucks themselves.

The jungle crossing alone took 96 days. Big swamp tyres kept breaking differentials, so a Rover engineer flew out, the axles and diffs were replaced, the trucks were lightened, and smaller cross-country tyres were fitted. The team used chainsaws and explosives to get through the Atrato Swamp. Both trucks survive: one at the British Motor Museum in Gaydon, the other (VXC 765K) in the Dunsfold Collection.

How it’s built

Underneath the Vogue badges, the Classic is pure Land Rover: a steel ladder frame, live axles front and rear on long-travel coil springs, permanent four-wheel drive through a two-speed transfer box, and an all-alloy V8. The body is unstressed aluminium panels on a steel frame — the panels don't rust, the steel under them does. That simplicity is why so many survive, and why the hard parts (frame, crossmembers, sills) are where your money goes.

Buying one: what to check

  1. 01
    Get under it before you look at the paintThe aluminium skins hide a steel frame. Check body mounts, the rear crossmember, frame sides, inner rear wheel arches, inner front wings and both sills. Pre-1983 trucks are mostly bolted together and easier to repair.
  2. 02
    Lift the carpets and open the bootOwners say to check under the front carpets for floor rot. Boot floors are suspect on post-1989 trucks, and the upper tailgate commonly rusts — a replacement runs around $800 or more.
  3. 03
    Listen to the V8Listen for bottom-end knocks and timing-chain rattle. Look for weeping head gaskets — cheap if caught early — and be wary of anything that hints at a slipped liner, which means a rebuild.
  4. 04
    Test the viscous coupling (1989-on)Tyres scrubbing or chirping on tight, slow turns means the centre viscous coupling may have seized, turning the truck into a permanently locked 4x4. With the transfer case in neutral and the handbrake on, a jacked front wheel should turn slowly with resistance — not stay locked solid.
  5. 05
    Interrogate the air suspensionOn EAS trucks, pumps, height sensors and valve blocks fail and leak, and few parts are shared with the later P38A system. Many have been converted to coils — fine, as long as it was done with a proper kit.
  6. 06
    Look for oily swivelsLeaking swivel-ball seals are chronic. Check the chrome balls for pitting and wet seals, and confirm the swivel housing has its own lubricant; it is separate from the front diff.
  7. 07
    Check the small electricsWindow switches and seat controls often fail (usually cheap). On cars with electric seats, a leaking battery can kill the seat ECU and stop seats and mirrors moving.
  8. 08
    Ask about LPG and the timing beltOne UK specialist recommends removing LPG conversions because they are often poorly fitted and can harm the engine. On 200Tdi and 300Tdi diesels, get proof the timing belt was changed in the last five years.
Post-purchase baseline

After you buy it

Most Classics are now 30 to 50 years old, and their records rarely cover the transfer box, swivels or ATF. Change every fluid and fix the cooling system before anything else, because the aluminium V8 doesn't tolerate overheating. Identify your transfer box before you buy oil: the chain-drive Borg Warner (with a viscous coupling) and the gear-drive LT230 take different fluids.

Common repairs

The jobs owners actually end up doing, rated for a competent home mechanic with a decent tool kit. Always confirm torque figures, fluids and procedures against the factory service manual for your VIN, and work safely — support the truck on stands, never just a jack. SYMPTOMSOil weeping from the swivel housings onto the chrome balls and wheels; the truck rolling while in park can mean a CV joint ran dry and failed.
THE FIXRemove the bracket that retains the swivel seal on the back of the hub. Cut out the old seal (Dremel or heavy side cutters). Pull the spring from the new seal and cut the seal so it can be wrapped around the axle housing, then refit it with the cut at the top, rejoin the spring, and seal the cut with silicone. Asked on every forum

Range Rover Classic questions, answered

What's the biggest tire I can fit without a lift?

The usual forum answer is 245/75R16 (about 30.5 inches) on stock coils, with the steering stops adjusted to stop rubbing at full lock. Some owners run 265/75R16 with slight chassis rub fixed by the stops. 235/85R16 generally needs a lift or some trimming on these 100-inch trucks.

SOURCE · LANDROVERWORLD · DISCOWEB
What fuel economy should I expect?

Bad. The EPA rated a 1990 3.9 automatic at 11 mpg city and 15 highway (13 combined), and the 1995 County LWB 4.2 at 11/15 (12 combined). Hagerty says careful driving helps.

SOURCE · FUELECONOMY.GOV · HAGERTY
How much can it tow?

Towing capacity is quoted at 3.5 tons. Note that Hagerty says coil-sprung trucks are more reliable but less suited to towing than the air-suspension trucks.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · HAGERTY
Air suspension or coils — and should I convert?

Electronic air suspension arrived on late-1992 trucks for the 1993 model year, mainly the LWB. It rides beautifully but pumps, height sensors and valve blocks fail. Bolt-in coil conversion kits are widely available for 1993–95 trucks and take about 3.5 hours.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · HAGERTY · ATLANTIC BRITISH
Does it have a centre diff lock?

It depends on the year. Earlier trucks have a manually lockable centre differential. From 1989 a chain-driven Borg Warner transfer case with a viscous coupling locks automatically. On those trucks, check that the viscous coupling hasn't seized.

SOURCE · 4X4 AUSTRALIA · ASHCROFT
Can I add lockers?

Yes. ARB makes Air Lockers for Rover-type banjo differentials in both 10-spline (RD127) and 24-spline (RD128) versions. Check which axle shafts your truck has before ordering.

SOURCE · ARB USA · ROVERS NORTH
Which years or versions should I buy?

Buy the most rust-free truck you can afford; condition matters more than the year. Values run from Velar prototypes to two-doors to four-doors, and fuel-injected and LSE four-doors are worth more than carburettor trucks. In the US, choose between 1987–92 coil-sprung SWB trucks and the 1993–95 County LWB with the 4.2 and air suspension. The 1995 trucks get the soft dash.

SOURCE · HAGERTY · TFLCAR
Is it reliable?

It can be, if you look after it. Jalopnik's experts say rust is what kills Classics, not the engine. Refresh the whole cooling system, keep up with oil changes, and expect electrical gremlins. Treat it as a third car or a project rather than a daily driver unless it has been sorted.

SOURCE · JALOPNIK
What oil and how often?

Hagerty's specialist recommends good-quality 20W-50, changed every 6,000 miles or 12 months. Some owners change it every 3,000 miles. Separately, check the oil in the swivel housings — it is not shared with the front diff.

SOURCE · HAGERTY · JALOPNIK · ATLANTIC BRITISH
Was there a diesel, and did the US get one?

Yes, but not in the US. The VM Motori 2.4 turbo-diesel arrived in 1986 and grew to 2.5 in 1989. Land Rover's 200Tdi replaced it in 1992, followed by the 300Tdi at the end of 1994. US trucks were all petrol V8s.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA
Did the US get the two-door?

Not officially. Factory US imports from March 1987 were four-door only. Earlier two-doors in America came through grey-market importers.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA

PART 01

Overview

Where it came from, why it matters, and how it changed year to year.

The story

Following a 24-year-old legend is a thankless job, and the P38A did it in September 1994 with a straight face. Land Rover called it the New Range Rover; everyone else named it after the office block the engineers worked in. Underneath the squarer, plusher body it was still a proper Rover: a separate steel chassis, live axles front and rear, a two-speed transfer box and the faithful aluminium V8. On top it piled self-levelling air suspension, a body computer under the right-hand front seat and enough wiring to cable a small town. Thirty years on, that split personality is exactly why it is both the bargain and the booby trap of the Range Rover family.

PEGASUS, DISCOVERY AND BLOCK 38A

The project started life as "Pegasus", then wore the cover name "Discovery" to confuse snooping journalists — awkward, since Land Rover had a real Discovery on sale. When the team moved into Block 38A at Solihull, the building's name stuck, and P38A it has been ever since. Inside the company it was the LP. Styling director George Thomson's in-house design was chosen over a Bertone proposal, and production at Solihull began in 1994 with sales running to the 2002 model year in the US.

It could have been very different under the bonnet. During development a pair of prototypes was fitted with BMW's 5.4-litre M73 V12 from the 750i, but the idea was shelved and never reached production. Instead buyers got the Rover V8 in 4.0 and new 4.6-litre forms, plus — outside North America — a 2.5-litre BMW M51 straight-six turbodiesel with 136 hp. The petrol V8s could be had with an R380 five-speed manual or ZF automatics; the 4.6 was automatic-only.

AIR, ELECTRONS AND A SMARTER TRANSFER BOX

The headline hardware was the Electronic Air Suspension, developed from the late Classic LSE system. It offers five heights — Access, Motorway, Standard, Off-Road and an Off-Road Extended setting the computer selects on its own when it senses the truck is beached. It lowers itself at speed and lifts for obstacles, which is lovely until a perished air spring or tired compressor leaves one corner kneeling in your driveway.

The drivetrain went electronic too. The BorgWarner 4462 transfer box shifts between high and low range with an electric actuator rather than a lever, selected through an H-gate on automatic trucks and a dash switch on manuals, and the truck has to be nearly stopped to change. Traction control started out rear-only on the 4.0 and diesel, with the 4.6 getting four-wheel control; from mid-1999 the V8s switched from Lucas GEMS to Bosch Motronic engine management, and later trucks got four-wheel traction control across the range.

WHY IT'S STILL WORTH YOUR WEEKENDS

Strip away the reputation and the P38 is a long-travel, beam-axle 4x4 with real low range, 3,500 kg of braked towing capacity and a 100-litre tank — a better overland base than its price suggests. The aftermarket agrees: Terrafirma makes sliders, winch bumpers, a snorkel and lift springs for it, and Lucky8 developed a 4-inch coil kit on its own P38 project truck. Converting to coils is a bolt-in job if you'd rather not carry an air compressor through the backcountry.

The flip side is that P38s are now cheap enough to be neglected. Classic magazines put good runners at a couple of thousand pounds and project cars at a few hundred, and plenty have had years of deferred maintenance. Ignore the cosmetics and buy on cooling-system health, a dry body computer and a working EAS; then budget for blend motors, heater O-rings and bushes. Do it yourself and the P38 is one of the best-value long-travel 4x4s on the road.

THE END OF THE LADDER

The P38 was the last Range Rover with a separate chassis, live axles, a manual gearbox option and a classic two-speed transfer box. Its replacement, the L322, was developed under BMW with a monocoque body and independent air suspension, and every Range Rover since has followed that path. For buyers who want the ladder frame, the P38 sits between the hand-tool simplicity of the Classic and the computer-dependent L322. It's the last one you can still fix with mostly analogue hardware, plus a diagnostic tool for the electronics.

SIDEBAR · FUN FACT

The BMW V12 Range Rover that never was

Somebody at Land Rover clearly wanted to know what a Range Rover would be like with a German V12. Two P38 prototypes were built with the 5.4-litre M73 V12 from the BMW 750i before the project was shelved. None reached production.

BMW's influence did make it to showrooms in a quieter form: the diesel P38 used BMW's 2.5-litre M51 turbodiesel six, and from mid-1999 the V8s ran Bosch Motronic management closely related to the system in the E38 7 Series. The next Range Rover, the L322, went much further and shared its first engines and electronics with the 7 Series.

PART 02

Specs & capability

How it’s built, what it can do, and what fits under it.

How it’s built

The P38 is a transitional truck: Classic-style bones with an electronic nervous system bolted on. A separate steel chassis carries two beam axles on air springs, permanent four-wheel drive runs through an electronically shifted BorgWarner two-speed transfer box, and power comes from the long-serving aluminium Rover V8 or a BMW diesel six. The mechanical parts are tough and well supported by the aftermarket. The weak spots are the systems controlling them: the EAS valve block and compressor, the climate-control blend motors and the body computer.

What to check before you buy

  1. 01
    Coolant history comes firstThe V8's big failure is coolant loss: 4.0s usually blow head gaskets and 4.6s slip cylinder liners. The system holds roughly 11.4 litres. Check the level and condition, ask about overheating, and look up the hose and thermostat history. A properly rebuilt engine needs top-hat liners or a new block.
  2. 02
    Look for a wet floor and a wet body computerDamp in the passenger or driver footwell often means leaking heater matrix O-rings. Water in the cargo area or spare-wheel well points to failed tailgate seals. Remove the lower trim at the right-hand front seat (the passenger seat on US trucks) and check the Body Electrical Control Module (BECM) for water damage, because a drowned BECM is the expensive version of this problem.
  3. 03
    Cycle the air suspensionRaise the truck to its highest setting and look at all four air springs. Listen for the compressor running constantly or cycling often, which points to leaks. An EAS warning or a lean on one corner can mean a tired height sensor. Springs are cheap from aftermarket suppliers (under £100 each), so a leak is a negotiating point, not a deal-breaker.
  4. 04
    Read the HVAC displayA 'book' symbol on the climate-control LCD means a stored hard fault, commonly the blend motors, blower relays or A/C. Test full cold on recirculation and cycle every vent. Replacing blend motors properly means taking the dash apart, and a dealer quote can run close to $2,000.
  5. 05
    Shift into low rangeWith the truck almost stopped, select low and then high again a few times. Hesitation, 'Select Neutral' or gearbox-fault messages can come from the transfer-box motor, its harness, or a misaligned XYZ gear-position switch. Listen for centre-diff clonks on tight turns.
  6. 06
    Check rust where it hidesThe panels are mostly aluminium, but rear arches are the worst cosmetic rust spot and the tailgate rusts under its plastic flap. Check the rear brake lines and look for chassis damage from off-road use.
  7. 07
    Plug in a diagnostic toolCheck that all warning lights come on with the ignition and go out (the ABS light should go out once you drive off). A P38-capable tool such as Blackbox Solutions' Nanocom will read the EAS, BECM and HVAC fault logs that a basic OBD reader can't.
  8. 08
    Petrol V8, and buy on conditionUK buyers' guides say choose the petrol V8 and pick on condition rather than 4.0 vs 4.6. Be wary of LPG conversions without documented professional fitting. The diesel is slow and has its own head-gasket and fuel-system issues.

Spotting the real thing

P38s look much alike from year to year, but engine management, displacement and traction hardware changed underneath. Knowing which one you have decides which parts to order and what to check.

  1. ID
    GEMS or BoschOpen the hood. A rectangular plenum with '4.0' or '4.6' embossed in the middle means GEMS, the earlier Lucas system. A cluster of intake tubes with no label means Bosch Motronic, which arrived mid-1999. The two use different parts.
  2. ID
    4.0 vs 4.6The 4.6 came only with the ZF 4HP24 automatic, a 4-pin rear diff and four-wheel traction control from new. The 4.0 had the ZF 4HP22 auto or the R380 manual, and early trucks had a 2-pin diff with rear-only traction control.
  3. ID
    DieselThe 2.5 BMW M51 turbo-diesel (136 hp) was never sold in North America. Pre-1998 engines have an aluminium inlet manifold; later ones have EGR, a plastic manifold and a MAF sensor.
  4. ID
    Factory nameLand Rover called it the 'New Range Rover' or the LP. 'P38A' comes from Block 38A at Solihull, where the development team was based.
  5. ID
    50th AnniversaryThe 1998 50th Anniversary trucks have Woodcote Green paint, exclusive walnut trim, colour-keyed mirrors and lower spoiler, 18-in Proline wheels and a numbered plaque. About 250 were planned.

Importing & legality

US trucks were petrol V8s only. The BMW 2.5 turbo-diesel and the R380 manual were sold elsewhere, as were right-hand-drive trucks. P38 production ran from September 1994 to 2001, so any example built 25 or more years ago can now be imported under NHTSA's 25-year exemption from FMVSS. The 25 years count from the manufacture date. State titling and emissions rules still apply, so check them before importing a diesel.

Rules change and vary by state and country. Confirm with your DMV and a licensed importer before you buy.

Post-purchase baseline

After you buy it

The P38 rewards a full catch-up service more than almost any other Range Rover. Overheating causes the V8's slipped liners, and neglected EAS and gearbox fluid cause most of the expensive failures. Change every fluid, refresh the cooling system, and leak-test the air suspension before you trust it. Note whether the engine is a GEMS (1995–98) or Bosch (1999–2002) V8, because some parts differ.

Common repairs

The jobs owners actually end up doing, rated for a competent home mechanic with a decent tool kit. Always confirm torque figures, fluids and procedures against the factory service manual for your VIN, and work safely — support the truck on stands, never just a jack. You do this work at your own risk; see the disclaimer. Some parts links are affiliate links.

Cooling hose and thermostat refresh (GEMS V8)
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · Half a daySHOP COST · About $260 for a complete hose and thermostat kit, plus coolant
SYMPTOMSAn ageing cooling system on a 4.0 or 4.6. Cracked or soft hoses, a high or overfilling expansion tank, or a heater that blows lukewarm. On these engines, overheating is what leads to slipped liners and blown head gaskets.
THE FIXRemove the reservoir cap and drain the system from the radiator's bottom plug with a 12 mm Allen socket. Expect roughly 11–12 litres (about 3 US gallons). The thermostat is an external 'artificial heart' unit held by its hoses next to the radiator. Replace it with the lower hose from underneath, then do the top hose, heater hoses and bypass hoses. While the hoses are off, unbolt and check the metal down-tube for rust blockage. There is no bleed screw. Fill, warm the engine fully, let it cool completely, then top up again. Run the heater to confirm the core isn't air-locked.

EAS valve block reseal and compressor rebuild
DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · VariesSHOP COST · O-ring and piston-seal kits are cheap. Most of the cost is your time
SYMPTOMSThe truck sags overnight, the compressor runs for a long time or keeps cycling, the reservoir is slow to fill, or there's an audible hiss from the valve block or silencer.
THE FIXRemove the valve block and compressor (the EAS box is on the left side of the engine bay). Spray soapy water over the solenoids and fittings while the system is under pressure to find the leaks. Replace the solenoid O-rings and the O-rings on the manual-inflation T-pieces and 8 mm lines. Check that each kit's O-ring sizes actually match. On the compressor, check the piston liner and seal, the reed valve and its shim, and the O-ring under the reed. Some owners also replace the thermal overload switch on the compressor board.

Air spring replacement
DIFFICULTY · 2 / 5TIME · VariesSHOP COST · Aftermarket springs under £100 each. About £350 genuine
SYMPTOMSOne corner drops after parking, cracked or bulging bellows when the truck is at full height, or EAS faults that come back after you clear them.

Heater matrix O-rings (wet footwell)
DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · VariesSHOP COST · O-rings are pennies. A new matrix runs from about £60 (Bearmach) to about £400 (genuine Valeo)
SYMPTOMSWet carpet beside the transmission tunnel that smells or tastes sweet, a misting windscreen, or a coolant level that keeps dropping.
THE FIXStart with the O-rings at the matrix pipe connection, which go brittle after 15–20 years. Owners report the pipes come out without removing the dash. The retaining screw is often seized, so put grinding paste on the screwdriver tip, or drill the head off if you have to. Don't overtighten on reassembly, because the joint has to move with heat. If the matrix itself leaks, replacing it means pulling the dash and console. Pressure-test the old and new cores at about 20 psi, and test them hot. Clear the drain under the core while you're in there.

Blend motor diagnosis
DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · VariesSHOP COST · Motors come pre-wired in a three-pack. Owners quote dealer repairs close to $2,000
SYMPTOMSHot air on one side and cold on the other, a 'book' symbol on the climate display, or temperature that doesn't change.
THE FIXDo the cheap checks first: blower and blend-motor relays, clogged pollen filters, and coolant level and bleeding. If a motor runs on direct voltage but won't move its flap, the flap may be jammed by a warped plenum, or the motor's internal potentiometer may be worn. Owners use a Nanocom's 'calibrate flaps' function after fitting new motors. As a winter stopgap, one owner unplugged a dead motor and set its flap open by hand.

THE FIXThe transmission ECU reads the transfer-box motor position as a Gray code, so check the motor harness and its earth first. Then check the XYZ (inhibitor/gear-position) switch on the auto gearbox. Set it with the dealer alignment tool LRT-44-011 rather than by eye. Atlantic British has reissued that tool and published a video of the procedure.

DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · 4–6 hours for all four ball jointsSHOP COST · Parts plus labor
SYMPTOMSVague steering, wander, clonks over bumps, uneven tyre wear. The front usually wears out first.
THE FIXReplace the radius arm bushes and the swivel ball joints, which need a special press tool. Polyurethane bush kits such as Polybush are popular. Use poly at both ends of the truck, and don't put poly in the Panhard rod. Check the dampers, drop links and ABS sensors while you're there.

Asked on every forum

Range Rover P38A questions, answered

What's the biggest tire I can fit without a lift?

Forum reports say 245/75R16 or 255/70R16, roughly 30 to 30.5 inches. One owner runs 255/70R16 on standard suspension with no rubbing. Another found 245/75R16 clears at full lock at standard and high ride heights, but rubs at the lowest height and can touch the back of the arch at full compression. 265/75R16 (about 31.6 in) rubs when the axles cross up unless you lift the truck and fit an adjustable Panhard rod.

SOURCE · AULRO · DISCOWEB
What fuel economy should I expect?

Poor. The EPA rated a 2000 4.0 automatic at 12 mpg city, 16 highway and 13 combined, and the 4.6 at 11/14/12. The tank holds 100 litres (about 26 US gallons).

SOURCE · FUELECONOMY.GOV · CARSALES
How much can a P38 tow?

3,500 kg (7,716 lb) braked and 750 kg unbraked, according to published spec sheets for the 4.6 HSE.

SOURCE · CARSALES · CARS-DATA
4.0 or 4.6 — which should I buy?

Buy the better truck. Buyers' guides say choose on condition rather than displacement. The 4.0 is more likely to blow a head gasket and the 4.6 more likely to slip a liner, so cooling-system history matters more than size. The 4.6 has more power, a 4-pin rear diff and four-wheel traction control from new. It came only with the ZF automatic.

SOURCE · CLASSICS WORLD · WIKIPEDIA
Do I have a GEMS or a Bosch engine?

Open the hood. A rectangular plenum with '4.0' or '4.6' embossed in the middle means GEMS, the earlier Lucas system. A cluster of intake tubes and no label means Bosch Motronic, which arrived mid-1999. The two use different parts, so check before ordering.

SOURCE · ATLANTIC BRITISH · CLASSICS WORLD
Does it have low range and locking diffs?

It has full-time four-wheel drive with a two-speed BorgWarner 4462 transfer box, switched electrically. Slow almost to a stop before changing range. There are no factory locking diffs; traction control brakes a spinning wheel instead (rear-only on early 4.0 and diesel trucks, all four wheels on the 4.6 and on later trucks). For bigger tyres, Ashcroft makes heavy-duty 4.10 ring-and-pinion sets for the P38's diffs.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · LUCKY8
Should I convert to coil springs?

It's a common way to simplify the truck. Terrafirma's standard-height kit TF223 is a bolt-in swap. Before you do it, note that air springs are cheap now and leaks are often just perished seals that are easy to fix. UK guides say coil conversions are becoming less popular as EAS parts get cheaper, and a healthy EAS gives you the extra off-road height.

SOURCE · TERRAFIRMA/LUCKY8 · CLASSICS WORLD
What lift options exist?

Lucky8's 4-inch kit (Terrafirma heavy-duty springs, +2-in Pro Sport shocks, mounting plates and an EAS override harness) was developed on its own P38 project truck. Lucky8 recommends tyres up to 32.5 in with it. Owners also report about 2 in of lift from Arnott Gen III air springs with Terrafirma shocks. Bigger lifts usually need double-cardan driveshafts (Tom Wood's) to cure vibration, and adjustable Panhard rods to re-centre the axles.

SOURCE · LUCKY8 · EXPEDITION PORTAL
Is the BMW diesel worth having?

It's the economy choice outside the US: a 2.5-litre BMW M51 six with 136 hp and 270 Nm. UK buyers' guides still recommend the petrol V8 and warn about diesel head gaskets (especially on remapped trucks), glow plugs and fuel-system parts. It was never sold in North America.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · CLASSICS WORLD
What maintenance gets skipped that I shouldn't skip?

Coolant hoses and thermostat: Land Rover's schedule says every 90,000 miles. Automatic gearbox oil about every 70,000 miles. The V8's mass airflow sensor at about 90,000 miles. Ignition leads, which wear quickly on the V8. The EAS compressor filter.

SOURCE · ATLANTIC BRITISH · CLASSICS WORLD
Why does the driver's or passenger's footwell get wet?

Usually the heater matrix O-rings, or a blocked drain under the heater box. Sometimes it's a leaking matrix. Clogged drains and failed seals can also let water reach the body computer under the seat, so fix wet carpets quickly.

SOURCE · RANGEROVERS.PUB · AULRO · CLASSICS WORLD

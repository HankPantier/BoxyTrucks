PART 01

Overview

Where it came from, why it matters, and how it changed year to year.

The story

International Harvester had built farm machinery and trucks for half a century when, in the late 1950s, it decided to build a rival to the Jeep CJ, then in its CJ-5 era. Chief designer Ted Ornas later said the brief from the engineering manager was to "design something to replace the horse." The result, the Scout 80, went on sale in late 1960 as a 1961 model and was built in Fort Wayne, Indiana. Autotrader Classics puts first-year sales at about 28,000. Not bad for a tractor company's first go at a small 4x4.

A HORSE REPLACEMENT FROM FORT WAYNE

According to Silodrome, the early proposals looked a lot like the Jeep and the Series Land Rover. Ornas's curvier sketches, drawn for a plastic body, won management over. Plastic turned out to be too expensive, so the Scout was built in steel. Ornas recalled that a full-size clay model was started in late July 1959 and approved that November. For an engine, IH took its 304 cubic-inch V8 and used one bank of it: a 152 cubic-inch slant-four, rated at 93 horsepower.

The first Scout was basic. It had a fold-down windshield, vacuum wipers mounted on top of the glass, and, until early 1962, side windows that slid out rather than wound down. It sat on a 100-inch wheelbase with Dana 27 axles front and rear, both offset to the passenger side, and a Dana 18 transfer case behind a three-speed. You could buy it as a pickup, an open roadster or a TravelTop wagon, in two- or four-wheel drive. Sports Car Market says the four-wheel-drive TravelTop was the one buyers wanted most.

THE 80 GROWS UP

IH marked its 100,000th Scout with the Red Carpet edition, about 3,000 trucks in white with red interiors. The Scout 800 arrived in 1965 with a fixed windshield, wipers moved to the bottom of the glass, an aluminum grille, bucket seats, better gauges and a better heater. It was the start of the Scout's slow move from work truck toward family wagon. Japan's FJ40 was a contemporary rival.

The engines grew too. A turbocharged 152 made 111 horsepower. The 196 cubic-inch four, half of IH's 392 V8, arrived in 1966 with the same 111 horsepower and better fuel economy. An AMC-built 232 inline-six followed, and in 1967 the Scout got its first V8, IH's own 266. The 1966 Sportop had a raked fiberglass roof and a nicer interior, and the rare factory Campermobile came with fold-out bunks. Both were early signs of the lifestyle SUV, years before anyone called it that.

800A AND 800B: THE LAST OF THE ORIGINALS

The 800A arrived in November 1968 with a three-piece grille and black headlight bezels. The other half of the 304 V8 was now in the options list, and running gear was heavier. Wikipedia lists a Dana 44 rear axle and a quieter Dana 20 transfer case for the 800A, while Autotrader Classics dates both changes to 1967, so check the truck rather than the paperwork. The 800A also brought the Aristocrat, the dressiest of the original-body Scouts, in blue and silver with a chrome roof rack.

The 800B, built from August 1970 to March 1971, was almost identical except for chrome headlight bezels. It came with a Comanche package, with its own paint and decals, and a SnoStar snowplow package offered only with the six. Production of the Scout II began in April 1971. Hagerty sums up the original run well: the Scout beat Ford's Bronco to market as a more practical alternative to the Jeep CJ, and it was among the early SUV pioneers.

OWNING ONE NOW

Hagerty credits early Scouts with a reputation for durable drivetrains, which it puts down to IH's tractor-building background. The same sources agree that the bodies are the problem. Silodrome warns that trucks driven on salted roads can end up "looking like a colander", and the catalogues of IH Parts America and Super Scout Specialists, full of replacement floors, rockers and patch panels, tell the same story. The good news is that the parts exist. Autotrader Classics notes that large panels such as fenders are not reproduced, but small steel is.

Prices have moved a long way. Sports Car Market recalled that its author bought a 1967 Scout 800 V8 TravelTop for $800 in 2003. In late 2024, Autotrader Classics advised budgeting at least $40,000 for a good one and warned that anything under $15,000 is a major project. Volkswagen Group now owns the Scout name, and its Scout Motors revival is building a factory in South Carolina. Its website's coordinates, 41°03'N 85°04'W, point straight back to Fort Wayne.

SIDEBAR · THE SCOUT THAT WENT TO PATAGONIA

Pato, the factory camper, and the Pan-American Highway

IH offered an experimental factory camper body for the Scout 80. It cost $960 installed as a stripped shell or $1,850 as a deluxe unit. Few were built, and Wikipedia calls them rare today.

One of them went a very long way. According to the man who uploaded the photo below to Wikimedia Commons, his father and a college roommate were given a 1963 or 1964 Scout 80 Camper by International for a drive from Denver to Patagonia on the Pan-American Highway. In return they supplied photos from the trip for IH's print advertising. They named the Scout 'Pato', and it was sold in Santiago, Chile, at the end of the trip. It might still be there.

PART 02

Specs & capability

How it’s built, what it can do, and what fits under it.

How it’s built

The Scout is a steel body on a ladder frame with a 100-inch wheelbase, leaf springs at all four corners and drum brakes all round. Four-wheel-drive trucks use Dana axles, closed-knuckle at the front, with a two-lever Dana transfer case and hubs that stick out past the hub caps. Power steering and power brakes were options, and Silodrome says a truck with both is the better buy. The engines all came from one family: the fours are halves of IH's V8s.

Buying one

What to pay, what to check, and how to tell a good one from a clone.

What to check before you buy

  1. 01
    Rust is the whole storyAutotrader Classics says to check the rockers, the windshield and side-window frames, and the lower corners of the removable top. Silodrome adds the spring mounts, the insides of the fenders and the welds, and warns that rust starts on the inside and works out, so bubbling paint is a bad sign. The fact that IH Parts America sells front and rear floors, inner and outer rockers, and fuel-tank compartment panels tells you where else to look.
  2. 02
    Count the big panelsSmall steel parts are widely reproduced, but Autotrader Classics notes that no one makes large panels such as fenders. A truck that needs fenders is a much bigger job than one that only needs floors.
  3. 03
    Is it really a 4x4?Scouts were sold in two-wheel drive too. Autotrader's quick test: if the hub sticks out through the hub cap, it's a four-wheel-drive truck. Then look for the twin transfer-case levers.
  4. 04
    Confirm the year and the engineBinder Planet hosts Jim Allen's list of starting and ending serial numbers for the 80, 800, 800A and 800B, compiled from IHC records, so you can date the truck and confirm which model it is. On an IH V8, Super Scout Specialists says the displacement is stamped on a domino-sized flat spot on the right side of the block, toward the front and just below the head. Engine swaps are common.
  5. 05
    Listen to the drivelineAutotrader Classics advises checking the axles and transfer case for wear. A transfer case that pops out of gear needs to come out for a rebuild, according to an IH Parts America moderator. On the front axle, look for worn knuckle wipers and sloppy kingpins.
  6. 06
    Power options matterSilodrome says Scouts with both power steering and power brakes are the better buy, because without them steering and braking take real effort. Autotrader Classics calls the four-wheel drums archaic and says a four-cylinder probably won't reach 75 mph.
  7. 07
    Ask what's been changedSuspension and other modifications are common, so ask the seller for details. That includes lifts, shackle swaps, disc conversions and engine transplants. A good conversion can be a plus. An undocumented one is a list of questions.

Spotting the real thing

The 80 and the 800 series share a body, and plenty of trucks have since swapped parts between years, so a VIN and the line ticket beat badges and grilles.

  1. ID
    80 or 800Scout 80s have the IH logo in the centre of the grille and tailgate, vacuum wipers at the top of a fold-down windshield, and hooks on the tailgate. The 800 moved the IH badge to the hood and got an 'International' grille nameplate, push-button door handles and a tailgate without hooks. 800A grilles have three pieces with matte-black headlight bezels; the 800B's bezels are chrome.
  2. ID
    The '1965½' ScoutAbout 3,000 Scouts built in late 1965 with leftover 80 parts are 800s by VIN and line-setting ticket. They still have the 80's hood tie-down loop and wire-mesh grille with a gold IH emblem.
  3. ID
    Chassis serial rangesSuper Scout Specialists' parts-catalogue ranges: Scout 80 serials run FC501 (1961) to FC124894 (1965), with FC150000–FC160573 for 1965–66. Scout 800 uses the G prefix (1966–68). 800A/800B serials run from G329142 (1969) to G445189 (1971).
  4. ID
    AxlesEarly trucks use Dana 27 front and rear, with the diffs offset to the passenger side to line up with the Dana 18 transfer case. From the 800A, the rear is a centred Dana 44 with a Dana 20 transfer case. Some transition trucks mix parts and run an angled rear driveshaft. The Dana 27's axle shafts are prone to breaking off-road, so check what's under yours.
  5. ID
    Line ticketThe factory line ticket lists engine, transmission, axles, gear ratio and paint codes. Axle ratio tags often rusted off, so the line ticket is the reliable way to confirm ratios and Power-Lock limited-slip diffs.

Post-purchase baseline

After you buy it

Treat any early Scout as a 50-plus-year-old machine whose fluids could be any age. Baseline everything, then inspect the front knuckles, brakes and steering before trusting it on the trail. Factory capacities for the 80 and 800 weren't available in sources we could verify, so check the IH shop manual or owner's manual for those. The one firm rule from IH specialists: keep GL-5 gear oil out of the transfer case.

DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · A weekendSHOP COST · Kit plus labor

Asked on every forum

Scout 80 / 800 questions, answered

What's the biggest tire I can fit without a lift?

We couldn't find a forum consensus for a bone-stock 80 or 800. The best data point comes from IH Parts America, which says "a 31" tire should fit fine" with its CPT spring system. That system adds 1 to 3 inches, about 2 on most trucks, and IHPA's own 800B runs 31x10.50 Duratracs on Scout II rally wheels. IHPA also notes that the front tires can hit the rear of the front wheel well, which its reverse-shackle kit addresses by moving the axle forward. So on stock springs, stay close to stock size. With fresh lift springs, 31s are the realistic ceiling.

SOURCE · IH PARTS AMERICA
What's the difference between the 80, 800, 800A and 800B?

The 80 (1961–65) has the fold-down windshield, top-mounted vacuum wipers and the 152 four. The 800 (1965–68) got a fixed windshield, bucket seats, better heating and gauges, plus the 196 four, 232 six and 266 V8. The 800A (late 1968–70) added the 304 V8, a three-piece grille and black headlight bezels. The 800B (August 1970 to March 1971) is the same truck with chrome bezels.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · OFFROADXTREME
Which years are the best to buy?

There's no agreed best year: rust decides, and an early Scout with a rotten frame is the expensive mistake. If you're choosing, the 800A (from late 1968) brought the Dana 20 transfer case and a Dana 44 rear axle, and the V8s are the ones for highway speed. Cars Counsel rates every 80 and 800 'good with inspection' because of parts scarcity and age.

SOURCE · SILODROME · CARS COUNSEL
Which engine should I look for?

The 152 four makes 93 hp, and Autotrader Classics warns that a four-cylinder probably won't hit 75 mph. The 196 four makes 111 hp, the same as the 152 turbo, with better economy. For trail use and highway speed most people want the 266 or 304 V8, but they sit in a heavier nose. Check the block stamping to confirm what's actually fitted.

SOURCE · OFFROADXTREME · AUTOTRADER CLASSICS
Is the four-cylinder really half a V8?

Yes. The 152 is one bank of IH's 304 V8, and the 196 is half of the 392 V8. That's why the engines share so many parts, and why the fours lean over in the engine bay.

SOURCE · HAGERTY · SILODROME
What oil goes in the transfer case?

IH specialists on the IH Parts America forum recommend SAE 90 GL-1 mineral oil, or straight 50-weight motor oil as a substitute, filled to the bottom of the filler hole with the truck level. One moderator estimated the capacity at about 2.5 to 3 quarts. Don't use 80W-90 GL-5.

SOURCE · IHPA FORUMS
What gear oil for a Power-Lok or Trac-Lok rear axle?

Use 80W-90 with a limited-slip additive, according to IHPA forum regulars. One owner found Mobil 1 gear oil made his Power-Lok chatter, while Red Line and Royal Purple worked.

SOURCE · IHPA FORUMS
Were all Scouts four-wheel drive?

No. The Scout was sold in two- and four-wheel drive from the start, and Wikipedia says four-wheel drive became standard by 1968. To check a truck, see whether the hub protrudes through the hub cap.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · AUTOTRADER CLASSICS
How do I date my Scout and confirm the model?

Use the serial number. Binder Planet hosts a list of starting and ending serial numbers and VINs for the 80, 800, 800A and 800B, plus the special models. Jim Allen, author of the International Scout Encyclopedia, compiled it from IHC records and microfiche. The data plate is on the driver's side of the cowl.

SOURCE · BINDER PLANET · SPORTS CAR MARKET
What is an early Scout worth?

In late 2024 Autotrader Classics advised budgeting at least $40,000 for a good example. It said a semi-decent one starts around $25,000 and anything under $15,000 is a major project. Restored trucks have sold for far more, including $33,000 for a Scout 80 at Barrett-Jackson in 2014 and a $114,900 asking price for a frame-off 800A.

SOURCE · AUTOTRADER CLASSICS · SCM
Can I still get parts?

Yes, and more than for many trucks this age. IH Parts America and Super Scout Specialists list floors, rockers, patch panels, brakes, axle seals, transfer-case kits and wiring. Silodrome notes that engine and transmission parts are widely available. The gap is large panels: Autotrader Classics says no one reproduces fenders.

SOURCE · IHPA · SSS · SILODROME
Can an early Scout be made into a usable daily or trail truck?

Yes, if you upgrade the right things together. Common jobs are a front disc conversion, a power-brake booster, a power-steering conversion (CPT makes one for 800A and 800B with the IH V8), new springs and shackles, and electronic ignition. Silodrome's advice is that any power upgrade should come with better brakes, a stronger transmission and better suspension.

SOURCE · IHPA · SILODROME

PART 01

Overview

Where it came from, why it matters, and how it changed year to year.

The story

In April 1971 International Harvester replaced the Scout 800B with a truck it had been sitting on for years. A version nearly identical to the production Scout II was shown to IH management in December 1967. It kept the formula: a short ladder frame, leaf springs, solid axles and a removable top. What changed was everything you touched. It was longer, lower, roomier and much more civilized. Over the next decade it became the Scout most people picture, and the one most still on the trail.

REPLACING THE HORSE, TAKE TWO

The first Scout existed because IH engineering manager Mr. Reese told Ted Ornas to 'design something to replace the horse.' By 1964 IH was already planning its replacement, and the concept was approved for production in mid-1965. The Jeep CJ was still the target, but the market had moved. Ford's Bronco and Chevrolet's Blazer had shown that buyers wanted a 4x4 they could live with every day.

So the Scout II launched with a 196-cubic-inch four, a 232 AMC straight-six and IH's own 304 V8, then added the 258 six and the 345 V8. You could order the steel Traveltop, the half-cab Roadster top or a soft top. Early trucks had a Dana 30 front axle and drum brakes. Late in 1974 power front disc brakes became standard, and after 1974 every Scout II got Dana 44 axles at both ends.

LONGER, THEN DIESEL

In 1975 IH raised the Scout's gross weight rating to 6,200 pounds and badged it XLC, for Extra Load Capacity. That put it out of reach of stricter light-duty emissions rules, and Scouts didn't get catalytic converters until 1979. In 1976 the 118-inch Traveler and Terra arrived, 18 inches longer between the door and the rear wheel arch and topped in fiberglass. The Traveler replaced the truck-based Travelall, which ended in 1975, and it became a popular RV tow vehicle.

1976 also brought a 3.3-liter Nissan SD33 straight-six diesel. Jim Allen, author of the International Scout Encyclopedia, credits IH with the first domestically built diesel SUV. In late 1979 the turbocharged SD33T replaced it. Super Scout Specialists' figures give 10,182 diesel Scouts in all, more than half of them 1980 turbos.

BAJA, AND THE SUPER SCOUT

Privateer Jimmy Jones won his class in the 1972 Baja 1000 in a '72 Scout II. Five years later IH was sponsoring four desert racing teams and selling the SSII, a stripped-down Scout with soft doors, a plastic grille and a roll bar, aimed squarely at the CJ-7. It ranged from the barebones four-cylinder Rancher Special, on which even the passenger seat was a cost option, to the dressier Sport and Baja Cruiser.

In the 1977 Baja 1000, an SSII driven by Jerry Boone finished first among production 4x4s, almost two hours ahead of the next car, a CJ-7. Boone later said his team had a month to prepare a stock truck and only got IH sponsorship after the race. About 4,000 SSIIs were built between 1977 and 1979, and today they're the most valuable Scout IIs.

HOW IT ENDED

IH experimented with Scout-based minivans, station wagons, dune buggies, Hurst-built special editions and even a small motorhome, and it built a Scout III SSV concept prototype in 1979. The 1979–80 International Harvester strike and a lack of money ended all of it. The 1980 trucks got a one-piece plastic grille, the turbo diesel and, for one year only, a Dana 300 transfer case. Only 13,106 were built. The last Scout came off the Fort Wayne line on October 21, 1980.

Hagerty counts about 260,000 Scout IIs. Rust has taken many of them, and the survivors are now real money. Gen X buyers make up more than 46 percent of Scout buyers. Volkswagen Group now owns the name, and Scout Motors is reviving the Traveler and Terra badges for new electric and range-extended trucks.

SIDEBAR · THE SWISS SCOUT

Monteverdi Safari: a Scout in a dinner jacket

In 1976 Swiss sports-car maker Peter Monteverdi showed the Safari, a luxury SUV on Scout II underpinnings with a new body by Carrozzeria Fissore. It went into production in 1977, aimed straight at the Range Rover, and offered air conditioning, leather, electric windows and options up to a television and a refrigerator.

Engines were a Chrysler 5.2-liter V8, IH's own 5.7-liter 345 and, Monteverdi claimed, Chrysler's 7.2-liter 440. Unlike the Range Rover of the day it had an automatic and switchable four-wheel drive. A cheaper Sahara kept most of the Scout body, and an estimated 30 were made. Production ended in 1982, after the Scout itself.

How it’s built, what it can do, and what fits under it.

How it’s built

The Scout II is a body-on-frame 4x4 with live axles on leaf springs at both ends. Most were four-wheel drive, with a manually shifted transfer case (2WD high, 4WD high and 4WD low) and free-wheeling front hubs. Engines were IH's own fours and V8s, AMC sixes and Nissan diesels, behind Borg-Warner manuals or a three-speed automatic. Factory line tickets record each truck's exact axles, ratios and options.

Buying one

What to pay, what to check, and how to tell a good one from a clone.

What they cost

The Scout II is the most-traded Scout, and prices keep rising. Rust-free stock 345 trucks bring strong money, but the top of the market belongs to LS-swapped restomods.

What to check before you buy

  1. 01
    Structural rust firstScouts were never fully galvanized. Corsetti Cruisers says to start with the rear spring mounts, the body mounts and the area under the doors. Structural rust around the rear crossmember or along the inner rockers can mean a full rebuild. Surface rust is manageable.
  2. 02
    Look under bedliners and fresh paintIn one Scout II rebuild reported on the 4WD Mechanix forum, a previous owner's bedliner reportedly hid so much floor rust that the repair ran past budget. Front fenders, door sills and the tailgate area hold moisture and can hide filler.
  3. 03
    Frame cracks at the steering boxThe frame is prone to fatigue cracks where the steering box bolts on, behind the front spring hangers and at the body mounts. Pull the box (three bolts) or at least inspect the welds, especially on a truck with big tires.
  4. 04
    Panel gaps aren't always crash damageScouts were hand-assembled with wide, uneven gaps even when new. Very uneven panels, doors that won't close properly or messy welds can still point to a bent frame or a poor repair.
  5. 05
    VIN tags, title and line ticketCheck for a clean title and matching VIN tags on the chassis and body. The line ticket (the factory build sheet) lists the engine, transmission, axles, gear ratio, paint and options. On 1971–76 trucks it's under the hood on the cowl; on 1977–80 trucks it's inside the glove box door. Replacements can be ordered.
  6. 06
    Which front axle?Dana 44 axles became standard on all Scout IIs after 1974. Compare the covers: if the front cover is smaller and rounder than the rear, it's a Dana 30. The line ticket also shows the axle, the ratio and whether there's a Trac-Lok rear.
  7. 07
    Engine swaps and wiringMany Scouts now have LS or other swaps. Check the wiring, cooling and driveline geometry; Corsetti warns that amateur conversions cause endless electrical and mechanical trouble. Look under the dash and around the firewall for brittle, patched factory wiring.
  8. 08
    Diesels and small partsThe SD33 diesels are rare and interesting. IH Parts America still lists filters, water pumps and seals for them. Dash pads, gauge clusters and original seat frames are hard to find for any Scout II, so price those in.

Spotting the real thing

Scout IIs have been re-powered, re-axled and re-bodied for decades. The serial number and the factory line ticket tell you what the truck started as.

  1. ID
    Serial prefix = year1971–72 Scout IIs use G-prefix serials (G425128 onward). From late 1972 the prefix carries a year letter: CGD = 1973, DGD = 1974, EGD = 1975, FGD = 1976, GGD = 1977, HGD = 1978, JGD = 1979, KGD = 1980 (Super Scout Specialists' catalogue ranges).
  2. ID
    Line ticket location1971–76 Scout IIs carry a copy of the line ticket under the hood on the cowl cover panel; 1977–80 trucks have it inside the glovebox door. Lost tickets can be reordered from Scout specialists. Use the ticket to confirm engine, transmission, axles, gear ratio, Trac-Lok limited slip and paint.
  3. ID
    Codes worth knowingLine-ticket engine codes include 12211 (Nissan SD33 diesel, '76–'79) and 12212 (SD33T turbo-diesel, '79–'80). Transfer-case code 13146 is the Dana 300, which was only fitted in 1980. Front-axle code 02044 is the Dana 44 with disc brakes. The 1978–79 Selective Edition package is code 10992.
  4. ID
    Wheelbase and bodyThe standard Scout II is on a 100-in wheelbase. The Traveler (full fibreglass top with a liftgate) and Terra (half-cab pickup) are 18 in longer between the door and the rear wheel well, on a 118-in wheelbase. Super Scout IIs (SSII) have soft doors, a roll bar and a unique plastic grille.
  5. ID
    Grille dates the nose1971–72: three horizontal bars. 1973: 14 vertical bars split in the middle. 1974–75: a vertical-bar overlay. 1976 only: a chrome 15-bar three-section grille. 1977–79: two horizontal and three vertical bars. 1980: a one-piece ABS grille in black or silver. Noses get swapped, so check against the serial.

DIFFICULTY · 4 / 5TIME · ≈ Several weekends, depending on how far it goesSHOP COST · Panels from about $89 (outer rocker) to $1,429.95 (complete aftermarket rear floor), plus welding
SYMPTOMSBubbling under the doors, soft or holed floors, a sagging door opening, water on the carpets, and rust at the rear spring mounts and rear crossmember.
THE FIXStrip the interior and probe everything with a pick or screwdriver. Support the door openings before cutting out any rocker or floor structure, then cut back to clean metal. Seam-seal and coat the new metal. If the inner rockers and rear crossmember are badly gone, Corsetti Cruisers says a frame-off restoration is often the better long-term answer.

DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · ≈ Half a day, more if cracks need weldingSHOP COST · Plate $16.95; Straight Steer brace about $164.95–199

DIFFICULTY · 4 / 5TIME · ≈ A weekend (the transmission has to come out)SHOP COST · Seal $69.95 plus spacer; full V8 gasket set about $162

Anything Scout sells three versions for V8 Scout IIs: 1971–75 points distributors, the 1975–77 'gold box' electronic system and the 1978–80 Prestolite electronic distributor. The first two install under the distributor cap. The 1978–80 Prestolite version needs the distributor removed, and Anything Scout says it must be used with a points-style coil, not the stock coil. Set the timing afterward. One Binder Planet owner's module and Flame-Thrower coil caught fire when a mechanic left the key on with the engine off for half an hour, so don't leave the ignition on.

Front ball joints (Dana 30 / Dana 44)
DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · ≈ A day for both sidesSHOP COST · About $157 per side (Spicer pair) or $49.95 per joint (Moog greaseable)
SYMPTOMSWandering, clunks over bumps, uneven tire wear, and play at the top and bottom of the tire with the front end jacked up.
THE FIXScout II Dana 30 and Dana 44 open-knuckle axles use the same ball joints: two upper and two lower per axle. They press into the knuckles, and the lowers have snap rings. Remove the wheel, caliper, locking hub, wheel bearings and spindle, then slide out the axle shaft. Unbolt the tie rod, separate the knuckle from the axle C, and press out the old joints with a ball-joint tool. Press in the new joints, reassemble, set the camber sleeve, and get the alignment checked. On the IH Parts America forum, one owner advises to buy good joints rather than doing the job twice.

Front disc brakes, wheel bearings and seals
DIFFICULTY · 3 / 5TIME · ≈ A daySHOP COST · About $350 for a complete front disc kit; wheel bearing kit $74.95
SYMPTOMSPulling, a soft pedal, grinding, or play in the wheel. Even when new, the brakes were the weak point: Motor Sport's 1978 test said they tended to pull and twitch.
THE FIXJack and support the front, remove the wheels, calipers and locking hubs, and take off the hub and rotor. Clean out the old grease, replace the inner seal, and repack or replace the bearings. Fit new rotors, pads and calipers, adjust the bearing preload with the spindle nuts, and bleed the brakes. Anything Scout's front kit covers 1974–80 Scout IIs with a Dana 44, and it notes that excessive lateral wheel movement means you need new bearings and seals. Do the rear drums at the same time.

Asked on every forum

International Scout II questions, answered

What's the biggest tire I can fit without a lift?

There's no clean stock answer, because 31s are already marginal. One owner of a 1977 Scout II said his 31s rubbed. IH Parts America's owner recommends a reverse-shackle kit and a 2.5-inch spring lift to run 31s without rubbing, and at least a 1-inch body lift plus a steering upgrade for 33s. Anything Scout rates the Rough Country 2.5-inch kit for up to a 32-inch tire. Plan on stock-sized tires (around 30 inches) if you won't lift it.

SOURCE · IH PARTS AMERICA FORUM
Which engine should I look for?

The 345 V8 is the one most people want. Moses Ludel of 4WD Mechanix calls it 'one of the strongest and most durable gasoline V-8s ever built,' and Motor Sport's 1978 test of a 345 automatic gave 163 bhp and 292 lb-ft. The 304 is the same family. The 196 four is slow but simple, and the AMC sixes and Nissan diesels are rarer.

SOURCE · 4WD MECHANIX · MOTOR SPORT
Does mine have a Dana 30 or Dana 44 front axle?

Until 1974 the Dana 30 was standard at the front, with a Dana 44 as a special order in 1974. After 1974 every Scout II had Dana 44s at both ends. If the front cover is smaller and rounder than the rear one, it's a Dana 30. The line ticket confirms the axle and ratio.

SOURCE · SUPER SCOUT SPECIALISTS
How do I tell what year it is?

Look at the grille. 1971–72 trucks have three horizontal bars; 1973 has 14 vertical bars; 1974–75 adds a vertical-bar overlay, with square chrome-and-black headlight rings on 1975. 1976 has a one-year 15-bar grille in three sections, 1977–79 has two big horizontal bars, and 1980 has a one-piece plastic grille with square headlights.

SOURCE · SUPER SCOUT SPECIALISTS
What's a line ticket and where do I find it?

It's the factory build sheet for your VIN, showing the engine, transmission, axles, gear ratio, paint and options. On 1971–76 Scout IIs it's under the hood on the cowl; on 1977–80 trucks it's inside the glove box door. If yours is missing, you can order a replacement.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · IH PARTS AMERICA
What oil should I run?

About 7 quarts with a filter, according to an IH Parts America tech moderator. The IH V8 has a flat-tappet cam, and Binder Planet members recommend a high-zinc oil such as a 15W-40 diesel oil (or 20W-50 in a worn engine) rather than a modern low-zinc synthetic.

SOURCE · IHPA FORUM · BINDER PLANET
How good is the fuel economy?

Not good with a V8. Motor Sport got 10 mpg on a fast motorway run in a 345 automatic and expected about 14 in gentler use. Super Scout Specialists claims 20 mpg in the city and up to 30 on the highway for the Nissan diesels.

SOURCE · MOTOR SPORT · SSS
Is the diesel worth hunting for?

If you want something rare, yes. Super Scout Specialists counts 10,182 diesel Scouts: 4,222 naturally aspirated SD33s from 1976 to 1979, then 571 SD33T turbos in 1979 and 5,389 in 1980. Filters, water pumps and seals are still listed, but it's a slow, specialist engine, so check that the seller has records.

SOURCE · SUPER SCOUT SPECIALISTS
Traveltop, Traveler, Terra or SSII: what's the difference?

The Scout II is the 100-inch-wheelbase truck with a steel Traveltop, half-cab Roadster top or soft top. The 1976–80 Traveler and Terra are 18 inches longer, with a fiberglass full top and hatch (Traveler) or a half-cab pickup (Terra). The 1977–79 SSII is the stripped soft-top off-roader with a plastic grille and roll bar.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · OCTANE PRESS
Does it have lockers, and how does the 4WD work?

There were no factory lockers. Trac-Lok limited-slip differentials were optional. The transfer case is manually shifted, with 2WD high, 4WD high and 4WD low, and the front hubs are free-wheeling. For 33-inch tires, Jim Weed's Off-Road.com build list recommends a front locker, a Detroit or ARB at the rear, and 4.10 gears.

SOURCE · WIKIPEDIA · MOTOR SPORT · OFF-ROAD.COM
What are they worth?

Hagerty gives a median condition #2 value of $42,200 for 1972–80 Scout IIs. SSIIs are the most valuable at $64,750, while some configurations sit below $30,000. Values have levelled off or dipped slightly since 2022.

SOURCE · HAGERTY
How many were built?

Hagerty puts Scout II production at about 260,000. Super Scout Specialists' yearly figures run from 31,866 (1975) to 44,434 (1979), with only 13,106 in 1980. About 4,000 SSIIs were built from 1977 to 1979.

SOURCE · HAGERTY · SSS
